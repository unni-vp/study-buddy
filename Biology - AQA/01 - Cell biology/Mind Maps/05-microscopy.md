# Microscopy

## Magnification & resolution

- **Magnification:** how many times larger the image is than the real object.
- **Resolution:** ability to distinguish two nearby points as separate.
- Enlarging a blurry photograph makes it bigger; better detail makes it clearer.
- Do not confuse image size with the amount of detail.

## Light & electron microscopes

- Light microscopes let you observe cells and recognise visible structures.
- **Electron microscopes** have much greater magnification and **resolving power**.
- Finer detail reveals and improves understanding of more **sub-cellular structures**.
- Improved resolution, rather than enlargement alone, reveals nearby details separately.

## The size formulae

- **Magnification = image size ÷ actual size**.
- Actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
- Image size = actual size × magnification.
- Convert image and actual size to **the same units** before dividing.
- Magnification has **no unit**; actual and image size do.

## Worked magnification

- Image width = 30 mm; actual width = 60 µm.
- 30 mm × 1,000 = **30,000 µm**.
- Magnification = 30,000 ÷ 60 = **×500**.
- Total microscope magnification = **eyepiece × objective**: ×10 × ×40 = ×400.
- Drawing magnification depends on the size of the drawing; it is not automatically the microscope setting.

## Units & standard form

- **1 cm = 10 mm**; **1 mm = 1,000 µm**; **1 µm = 1,000 nm**.
- 1 µm = **1 × 10⁻⁶ m**; 1 nm = **1 × 10⁻⁹ m**.
- Small unit → large unit: divide. Large unit → small unit: multiply.
- Standard form: a × 10ⁿ, with **1 ≤ a < 10**.
- For example, 0.000060 m = **6.0 × 10⁻⁵ m**.

## Scale & estimating area

- 20 µm ÷ 2 µm = **10**: a tenfold difference is **one order of magnitude**.
- Convert units before comparing sizes.
- A **scale bar** represents an actual distance: compare the cell's image length with that bar.
- Use estimates to judge relative size or area when exact boundaries are unclear.
- For similar shapes, doubling length gives about **four times the area**; state the shape assumption.

## Practical: prepare the slide

- Observe both **plant and animal cells**.
- Place thin onion epidermis on a slide with water. Use a suitable stain, such as iodine, to improve contrast.
- Lower the **coverslip at an angle** → fewer trapped air bubbles.
- Thin sample → light passes through; less overlap.
- Use a prepared animal-cell slide or a teacher-approved preparation.
- Handle glass, stains and biological samples safely.

## Practical: focus & draw

- Start with the **lowest-power objective**; focus with coarse, then fine adjustment.
- Centre the cells; increase magnification; use **fine focus**.
- Draw clear outlines in pencil; no shading. Label only structures you can identify.
- Include a **magnification scale or scale bar**.
- Use visible features to interpret images, not a memorised diagram alone.
