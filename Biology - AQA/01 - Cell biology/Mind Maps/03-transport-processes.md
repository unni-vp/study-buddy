# Transport processes

## Diffusion

- **Net movement of particles** of a gas or dissolved substance from **higher to lower concentration**.
- Particles move randomly; the overall movement is **down the concentration gradient**.
- **No energy from respiration** is required.
- Examples: oxygen and carbon dioxide in gas exchange; **urea from cells into blood plasma**.
- At equilibrium, particles still move but there is **no net movement**.

## Faster diffusion

- **Steeper gradient** → greater net movement per second.
- **Higher temperature** → particles have more kinetic energy and move faster.
- **Larger membrane area** → more particles cross at once.
- **Thinner barrier** → shorter diffusion distance.
- Always connect the feature to why exchange becomes faster.

## Osmosis

- Net movement of **water molecules** from a **dilute solution** to a **more concentrated solution**, through a **partially permeable membrane**.
- The dilute side has more water molecules relative to solute.
- Water can cross; some dissolved substances cannot.
- No energy from respiration is needed.
- Water moves both ways; the arrow shows **net movement**.

## Cells gain or lose water

- Dilute surroundings → water enters by osmosis.
- Plant: vacuole fills → **turgid**; the wall prevents bursting. Animal: may swell and burst.
- Concentrated surroundings → water leaves.
- Plant: becomes **flaccid**; severe loss can cause **plasmolysis**, where the membrane pulls from the wall. Animal: shrinks.

## Active transport

- Movement from **lower to higher concentration**, **against the concentration gradient**.
- Requires **energy from respiration**.
- Membrane transport proteins carry substances.
- Memory cue: moving uphill needs energy; the 'hill' represents concentration.
- Diffusion and osmosis go down gradients. Only osmosis is **water-only**.

## Where active transport helps

- **Root hair cells:** absorb mineral ions even when soil concentration is lower than inside the cell. Ions are needed for healthy growth.
- **Small intestine:** absorbs sugars from a lower concentration in the gut into blood with a higher concentration.
- Sugars can be used in **cell respiration**.
- Many mitochondria support a high energy demand.

## Osmosis practical: method

- Vary **salt or sugar concentration**; measure **percentage mass change** of plant tissue.
- Prepare equal-size potato cylinders; record **initial masses**. Use equal solution volumes, including distilled water.
- Keep tissue source, dimensions, time and temperature constant.
- Remove; **blot consistently**; measure final masses. Repeat each concentration and calculate means.
- Blotting removes surface liquid; use cutting tools safely.

## Osmosis practical: results

- **% change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100**.
- 2.50 g → 2.20 g: −0.30 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = **−12%**; water left.
- Positive → net gain; negative → net loss. Zero → **no net movement**, not no movement.
- Plot concentration on x; mean % change on y. Use a best-fit line/curve; its **0% crossing** estimates balanced concentration.
- Rate = mass change ÷ time: 0.12 g ÷ 30 min = **0.004 g/min**. Repeat; check anomalies; test more concentrations near zero.
