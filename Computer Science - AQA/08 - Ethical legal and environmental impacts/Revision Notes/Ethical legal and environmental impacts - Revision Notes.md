# Ethical, legal and environmental impacts

## Ethical, legal and environmental questions

Digital technology can make life easier while creating problems for other people. Consider **who benefits**, **who could be harmed** and **what could reduce the harm**.

Think of a new road. It might shorten journeys, but disturb nearby homes and use resources. A digital system also has consequences beyond the person using it.

| Perspective | Question to ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ethical** | Is the action fair and responsible? | Employee location tracking may feel intrusive. |
| **Legal** | Does the action meet the law? | Unauthorised access to a computer can be a criminal offence. |
| **Environmental** | How are energy, resources, waste and pollution affected? | Both manufacturing and running a device have impacts. |


An action can be **legal but still raise ethical concerns**. One decision may involve all three kinds of issue.

## Privacy and personal data

**Privacy** means having control over access to your personal life and information. **Personal data** relates to an identifiable person. Names, locations, account details and identifiable health readings can all count.

A fitness app recording a running route may also reveal where somebody lives. Useful data can become risky when it is combined or shared.

Important data-protection principles include:

- **Lawfulness, fairness and transparency:** have a lawful basis and explain how information is used.
- **Purpose limitation:** collect information for clear purposes; do not simply reuse it for an incompatible purpose.
- **Data minimisation:** collect only what is needed. A homework app should not need continuous location tracking.
- **Accuracy:** keep information appropriately accurate. Wrong information can lead to unfair decisions.
- **Storage limitation:** keep identifiable information only as long as needed.
- **Security:** protect information against unauthorised use, loss and damage.
- **Accountability:** organisations must be able to show that they handle data responsibly.

**Consent** is one possible lawful basis, not a requirement for every use of personal data. Clear choices and explanations still matter. Collecting less data reduces the damage if a breach happens.

## Fair access and differing views

A system can affect people who did not choose to use it. A wearable camera may record bystanders, and a vehicle's cameras may capture pedestrians. The benefits to the device owner do not remove other people's privacy concerns.

People can also differ in what they consider acceptable monitoring or sharing. Do not assume that everyone in one country or community has the same attitude. Clear explanations, appropriate choices and consideration of people affected indirectly make a design more responsible.

**Digital exclusion** occurs when people cannot use a service because of cost, poor connectivity, disability, limited skills or inaccessible design. Moving a service online may save time for connected users while making access harder for others. An accessible interface, training or a suitable alternative can reduce that harm.

Legal duties and individual data rights have conditions and exceptions. Avoid claims such as “all data must always be deleted on request” or “consent is the only lawful basis”. For a scenario, explain the principle and the actual use of data rather than inventing an absolute rule.

## Security and surveillance

**Cyber security** protects systems and data against attacks. A breach can interrupt essential services, expose private information or cost people money. Security controls also cost time and money, so organisations must choose measures that fit the risks.

| Feature | Privacy | Security |
|---|---|---|
| Main concern | Access to and use of a person’s private life and information. | Protection against unauthorised access, attacks, loss or unwanted changes. |
| Example concern | A service collects more location history than its purpose needs. | An attacker steals the stored location history. |
| How they connect | Collecting less data can reduce the information exposed by a breach. | Strong protection helps keep private records safe, but does not justify unnecessary collection. |


**Hacking** in this context means **unauthorised access**. Accessing a system without permission can breach the Computer Misuse Act, even if the person claims they were only curious. **Authorised penetration testing**, within an agreed scope, helps find weaknesses.

### Privacy versus public safety

Governments and security services may argue that access to communications helps identify threats and prevent attacks. Citizens may fear that extensive monitoring exposes innocent people's private lives.

- **Benefit:** targeted access can supply evidence and help investigators connect suspects.
- **Risk:** mass collection can include innocent people, enable misuse or expose a large store of sensitive data to attackers.
- **Safeguards:** lawful authorisation, independent oversight, restricted access, proportionate collection and limited retention.

A balanced judgement asks whether access is **necessary and proportionate**. Saying either “collect everything” or “never access any data” ignores the trade-off.

### Responsibility for protecting systems

Organisations holding personal information need to protect it, while users also need to follow safe procedures. A breach can affect people whose records were stored, not just the organisation that owns the computer.

Online attackers may conceal their identity or operate across borders, making investigation and enforcement difficult. Technology can also develop faster than detailed rules or organisational procedures are updated. These difficulties support careful security design and ongoing review; they do not make unauthorised access acceptable.

## Explaining and evaluating an impact

An explanation links a feature to a real consequence:

**Location tracking** allows an app to build a record of journeys. If that record is shared without an appropriate basis, it could reveal a user's daily routine and reduce their privacy.

For an evaluation:

1. Identify the **stakeholders**: users, workers, businesses, governments or people affected indirectly.
2. Explain a benefit and its consequence in the stated situation.
3. Explain a risk and its consequence.
4. Consider a safeguard and any remaining limitation.
5. Give a **reasoned judgement** that answers the question.

Avoid bare statements such as “it is good”, “it is dangerous” or “it uses electricity”. Explain **how and why** the effect matters.

### Develop the point before moving on

A claim such as “monitoring is bad for privacy” does not yet explain what happens. Develop the link between **the feature, the information collected and the person affected**.

For example, a company phone that records location throughout the day can reveal an employee's movements outside working hours. That creates a privacy concern because information unrelated to the job is being collected. Restricting tracking to a defined work purpose and appropriate times would reduce unnecessary collection.

The employer might need location during deliveries to allocate nearby jobs. That benefit supports limited work-related use, rather than proving that continuous monitoring is justified. A useful judgement weighs the particular benefit against the remaining harm and explains why a safeguard changes the balance.

## Mobile technologies

Phones and tablets let people use services while moving around.

- **Access:** people can contact others, navigate, study or work away from a fixed computer.
- **Safety:** a phone can help somebody call for assistance or share their location when they choose.
- **Distraction:** notifications can interrupt learning, work or driving.
- **Unequal access:** device costs, data costs and poor coverage can exclude some people.
- **Privacy:** apps may collect contacts, location or browsing activity. Permissions should match the service's purpose.

An employer giving staff a mobile device could improve communication. Expecting constant replies outside working hours may harm their wellbeing. Clear working-hour rules reduce this problem.

An app's permissions should match the function the user expects. A navigation app may need location during a journey, but retaining continuous location history for unrelated purposes creates a different privacy issue. Explain the purpose, duration and possible sharing of collection instead of treating all location use as equally justified.

Constant notifications can create pressure to remain available. Giving people control over notifications and setting clear expectations can preserve the benefit of mobile access without expecting uninterrupted attention.

## Wireless networking

**Wireless networks** connect devices without a cable to each one. They allow movement and can be easier to extend in a building.

- **Benefit:** staff can move between rooms while staying connected.
- **Security risk:** an unsecured network may allow interception or unauthorised access.
- **Protection:** use suitable encryption, strong authentication and properly configured network equipment.
- **Reliability:** interference, distance and obstacles can affect a connection.

Wireless does **not automatically mean insecure**. Explain the network's actual safeguards. An organisation should also consider who can afford equipment and who can reach the network.

On public Wi-Fi, a user may not know who operates the access point. A convincing network name alone does not prove that it belongs to the intended organisation. Secure applications and protected connections reduce exposure, while the operator still needs to configure and maintain the network appropriately.

The convenience of wireless access should be weighed against reliability and accessibility needs. A service that requires continuous connectivity may disadvantage users in places with weak coverage.

## Cloud storage

**Cloud storage** holds data on remote servers accessed through a network. It is like keeping documents in a shared office that authorised people can reach from different places.

- **Convenience:** users can access files from different devices and collaborate.
- **Recovery:** off-site copies can help after theft or damage at one location, when backups are arranged properly.
- **Dependence:** an internet outage or provider failure can interrupt access.
- **Security:** weak account protection or incorrect sharing permissions can expose files.
- **Control:** users need to understand who can access their data, how it is retained and where it is processed.

Security is a **shared responsibility**. A provider's secure servers do not fix a user's publicly shared folder or stolen password. Strong authentication, restricted access and appropriate backups reduce risk.

Storing data abroad is not automatically illegal. Organisations must meet the applicable data-protection rules and safeguards.

A provider outage or closure can interrupt access even when the user's own device is working. A suitable recovery plan includes an accessible copy and a way to move important files to another service, rather than assuming continued access is guaranteed.

Organisations should understand the provider's access, retention and data-handling arrangements. Storing data remotely does not automatically transfer ownership to the provider or give it unlimited permission to sell the information. The issue is what the relevant agreement and rules allow, and whether users understand the arrangement.

### Why cloud use has grown

Explain the link between a change and the reason it makes remote storage useful:

| Change | Why it can encourage cloud use |
|---|---|
| More reliable, higher-bandwidth connections | Users can transfer large files more conveniently and depend on access from more locations. |
| Several devices per user | A person can reach the same documents from a phone, tablet or computer. |
| Remote work and collaboration | People in different places can share a central file rather than send separate copies repeatedly. |
| Capacity supplied as a service | An organisation can obtain more remote storage without first installing all the equipment itself. Ongoing charges still need consideration. |

These changes explain adoption; they do not remove the costs or risks. Remote servers still require physical equipment, electricity and cooling. The user also depends on suitable access, account security and the provider's arrangements.

## Wearable technologies

Wearables are devices worn on the body, such as smartwatches and fitness trackers.

- **Useful feedback:** movement and activity records can help users understand habits.
- **Convenience:** reminders and messages can be checked quickly.
- **Sensitive data:** identifiable health readings and location history can reveal private information.
- **Reliability:** an inaccurate reading could cause worry or lead to an unfair decision.
- **Fairness:** users may feel pressured to share data with an employer or insurer.

Explain who sees the data and why. Limiting collection, offering meaningful choice and preventing unrelated use help protect users. A fitness reading should not be treated as a complete medical assessment.

A wearable camera raises a different issue from a step counter: it can collect information about people nearby. A health tracker may instead expose sensitive readings if shared with an employer or insurer. Identify the data and the affected person before judging the impact.

A choice may not feel voluntary when refusing to share information could disadvantage an employee. Limiting collection and preventing unrelated decisions based on the data can address this pressure more directly than simply adding a password.

## Computer-based implants

A **computer-based implant** is a device placed inside the body that uses computing to perform a function. For example, a device may help manage a heart rhythm or support hearing.

- **Benefit:** it can improve a person's quality of life or support a function they struggle to perform.
- **Reliability:** software or hardware failure can have serious consequences.
- **Maintenance:** long-term support, battery management and safe updates may be needed.
- **Privacy and security:** a connected implant may transmit sensitive information or need protection from unauthorised control.
- **Fair access:** cost could mean that some people cannot obtain a useful device.

Not every implant connects to the internet. Discuss cyber risks **when the device's connection makes them relevant**, rather than assuming all implants work identically.

The person receiving an implant needs understandable information about its benefits, risks, ongoing support and any data collection. Pressure to accept a device raises questions about meaningful choice, even when it could have a health benefit.

Responsibility can involve designers, manufacturers, healthcare providers and maintainers. A discussion should ask how the failure occurred and what safeguards were in place, rather than assume that every problem is automatically one person's fault.

## Autonomous vehicles

An **autonomous vehicle** uses sensors and software to carry out driving tasks within the conditions for which it is designed. Driver-assistance features do not necessarily make a vehicle fully self-driving.

- **Possible safety benefit:** automation could reduce errors caused by tiredness, distraction or poor human judgement.
- **Access:** some systems could give greater independence to people unable to drive.
- **Technical risk:** software faults, poor sensor readings or difficult weather may affect performance.
- **Employment:** some driving jobs could change or disappear, while new technical roles emerge.
- **Privacy:** cameras and journey records may collect information about passengers and bystanders.
- **Legal responsibility:** accidents raise questions about responsibility, approval and insurance. The answer depends on the applicable rules and circumstances.

Testing, clear operating limits and secure software are important. Do not claim that autonomous vehicles cannot crash or that one party is always legally responsible.

Difficult situations may require software to make decisions that affect passengers and other road users. There may be competing ideas about which risks are acceptable. Transparent safety requirements, testing and accountability matter because the consequences extend beyond the purchaser.

A security flaw could also expose journey data or affect connected vehicle functions. The relevant risk depends on the actual system and access available. Explain the possible consequence and a suitable safeguard without assuming that every vehicle can be remotely controlled in the same way.

## Environmental impacts across a device lifecycle

Consider the whole **lifecycle**, not just electricity used while a device is switched on.

| Lifecycle stage | Possible impact | Ways to reduce it |
|---|---|---|
| **Manufacture and transport** | Mining materials can damage habitats and cause pollution. Manufacturing and transport use energy and may release greenhouse gases. | Use working devices for longer; choose repairable designs and longer software support to reduce repeated replacement. |
| **Use** | Devices, networks and data centres use electricity. Cooling also uses resources and may use water. | Improve energy efficiency and use lower-carbon electricity. Online meetings can reduce travel, but their equipment still has impacts. |
| **End of life** | Electronic waste contains valuable materials and may contain harmful substances. Poor disposal can pollute soil and water. | Repair and reuse devices; recycle to recover useful materials. Securely remove personal data before passing on or recycling a device. |


A new, efficient device is not automatically the greener choice. Compare the energy saved with the resources needed to manufacture its replacement.

### Balancing environmental gains and costs

Electronic messages can reduce paper use, and remote meetings can reduce some journeys. These benefits still require devices, networks and data centres, so “digital” does not mean “no environmental cost”.

Manufacturing uses finite materials as well as energy. Repair, reuse and longer support can reduce the demand for replacement devices. Recycling can recover useful materials and reduce harmful waste, but it also takes processing and does not recover everything.

Compare the whole lifecycle in the stated situation. Keeping a working device for longer may avoid manufacturing impacts, while an unusually inefficient device used heavily may justify replacement. A reasoned answer explains the balance rather than claiming either choice is always greener.

## A worked balanced judgement

**Situation:** a school wants pupils to use wearable activity trackers with cloud storage.

The trackers could help pupils set activity goals because they make patterns in movement visible. Cloud storage would let pupils see their own records on different devices. However, identifiable health and location data could reveal sensitive routines. Sharing it widely might embarrass pupils or create pressure to meet the same targets despite different needs.

The school should collect only the data needed for its stated purpose, use an appropriate lawful basis and explain access clearly. Restricted accounts and limited retention would reduce exposure, although they would not remove every breach risk. An alternative should be available for pupils unable to use a tracker. Reusing equipment and supporting it for longer would reduce replacement waste.

The scheme is more defensible if participation and access arrangements are fair and data collection is limited. Its convenience alone does not justify unrestricted monitoring.
