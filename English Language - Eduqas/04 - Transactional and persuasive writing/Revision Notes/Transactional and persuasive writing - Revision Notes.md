# Transactional and persuasive writing

## Writing with a clear purpose

**Transactional writing does a job for a reader.** It might explain an idea, advise someone, argue a case, review an experience or persuade people to act. A lively article can inform and entertain without trying to win an argument.

Think of planning a journey: **purpose** is the destination, **audience** is who travels with you, and **form** is the vehicle. A sensible route for one group may not suit another.

- **Purpose:** what should the reader understand, think, feel or do afterwards?
- **Audience:** who are they, what matters to them and what do they already know?
- **Form:** article, letter, speech, review or another named type of writing.
- **Tone:** the attitude you convey: encouraging, concerned, enthusiastic, thoughtful or firm.
- **Register:** how formal or informal your language is.

**Answer the exact task.** An article advising teenagers about starting a hobby needs practical steps and encouragement. An article arguing that schools should fund clubs needs reasons, consequences and a convincing proposal.

### What the exam asks you to do

Component 2 has **two compulsory writing tasks**, each worth **20 marks**. For each: **12 marks for communication and organisation**, and **8 for vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation**. Complete both.

Recent papers advise about **300–400 words per task** and **30 minutes each**, including about five minutes to plan. Use the instructions on your paper. A workable approach is five minutes planning, around 22 minutes drafting and three minutes checking. Length helps you develop ideas; padding does not.

The strongest priorities are **a clear purpose**, **relevant developed detail**, **logical organisation**, **a sustained suitable voice** and **accurate, controlled language**.

## Decode the task before you write

Example task: Write an article for your school magazine persuading students to try an unfamiliar school club.

| Decision | Useful answer | What changes in the writing? |
|---|---|---|
| Reader | Students who may be busy or nervous | Acknowledge homework and the fear of arriving alone. |
| Purpose | Encourage one first visit | End with a realistic first step. |
| Form | School-magazine article | A relevant headline and connected prose; no greeting or sign-off. |
| Main message | Trying once is manageable and worthwhile | Keep each paragraph linked to that claim. |
| Voice | Friendly and encouraging | Use familiar situations and respectful direct address. |

**The audience is not always the person with power to act.** A letter to a newspaper editor can influence readers and public discussion; the editor cannot personally change school policy. Address the actual reader rather than pretending they control the decision.

Before drafting, finish this sentence: **“I want this reader to ... because ...”** If you cannot, your main message is still vague.

## Find ideas you can develop

Aim for a few distinct ideas with useful detail. Three well-developed points can be more convincing than eight repeated claims. There is **no compulsory paragraph count**.

For school clubs, avoid three versions of “clubs are fun”. Try three different angles:

- **Skills:** a beginner learns something through a small first task.
- **Belonging:** a shared activity makes starting a conversation easier.
- **Practicality:** a short trial reduces the pressure to commit every week.

Use these questions when you get stuck: **Who is affected? What happens? Why? What would it look like? What could make it difficult? What would help?**

| Thin idea | Develop it with a concrete detail | Explain why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Clubs build confidence. | At drama club, a beginner starts with a short scene in a small group. | A manageable first success makes speaking in front of others less daunting. |
| Clubs help friendships. | Two students work together to debug a short program. | They already have a problem to discuss, so conversation feels less forced. |
| Clubs can fit busy weeks. | A student asks whether a lunchtime taster is available. | They can try the activity before making a longer commitment. |

Use believable examples. You do not need impressive statistics to sound convincing. **Do not present invented surveys or expert quotations as established facts.** A clear hypothetical example—“Imagine arriving at ...”—can do the job honestly.

## Turn your ideas into a plan

Write **paragraph jobs**, not a miniature finished essay. Give each paragraph a different purpose and put it where the reader needs it.

A flexible persuasive-article route:

1. **Opening:** recognise the reader’s concern and state the main idea.
2. **First reason:** make a benefit concrete.
3. **Develop the case:** add a different benefit and its consequence.
4. **Obstacle and response:** acknowledge a reasonable concern; offer a workable answer.
5. **Ending:** bring the idea together and suggest the next step.

For an advice article, use a sequence the reader can follow: **choose a manageable goal → take the first step → handle a difficulty → keep going**. For a balanced discussion, compare genuine advantages and limitations before reaching a judgement. Match the structure to the purpose.

**Coherence** means the whole argument makes sense. **Cohesion** means its parts connect. “However” helps only if the next point really contrasts with the previous one.

- Add: “Another advantage ...”
- Explain a result: “This means ...”
- Acknowledge a difficulty: “That sounds simple until ...”
- Refer back: “That first visit ...” or “This small change ...”

Repeated key ideas can link paragraphs naturally; you do not need to start every paragraph with “Firstly”, “Secondly” and “Finally”.

## Develop a paragraph rather than list points

A useful route is **point → detail → explanation → connection to the reader**. Use it to check your thinking, not as a rigid sentence template.

**Thin:** “School clubs are good. They are fun and help confidence. Everyone should join.”

**Developed:** “A school club gives you somewhere to be a beginner without needing to apologise for it. At a coding session, your first achievement might be making a character move across the screen. That small success shows that an unfamiliar skill can be learned one step at a time. If you have spent years saying you are ‘not a computer person’, one lunchtime could challenge that assumption.”

- **Point:** beginners are welcome.
- **Detail:** a specific, modest coding task.
- **Explanation:** small success makes learning feel possible.
- **Reader connection:** challenges a familiar worry.

Notice that the paragraph stays on **one main idea**. It does not switch halfway through to the price of school lunches.

## Open and close an article effectively

A headline should reveal the angle, not merely announce the topic. **“One lunchtime, something new”** gives a clearer direction than “Clubs”. If the question supplies a title, use it.

An opening needs to interest this reader **and lead into the subject quickly**.

- **Recognisable moment:** “The club list appears, and you walk past it again.”
- **A useful contrast:** “You do not need to be talented before you begin; beginning is how you find out what you can do.”
- **A focused question:** “What could one unfamiliar lunchtime change?” Follow it with an answer or idea.

Avoid a long fictional story that takes over the article. A short anecdote earns its place by supporting the point.

An ending should leave a clear thought or action. It can return to the opening image, make a recommendation or weigh up the argument. **Do not introduce a major new reason in the final sentence.**

**Weak ending:** “That is why clubs are good. Thanks for reading.”

**Stronger ending:** “Tomorrow, stop at that noticeboard. Choose one room, one lunchtime and one chance to surprise yourself.”

## A complete article, with the choices explained

Task: Write an article for your school magazine persuading students to try an unfamiliar school club.

### One lunchtime, something new

The club list appears on the noticeboard, and you walk past it again. Chess, coding, drama: interesting, perhaps, but surely those rooms are full of people who already know what they are doing? Before you dismiss the whole list, consider this: you only need to try one session. You are choosing a first step, not signing away every lunchtime until July.

A club gives you somewhere to be a beginner. At coding club, your first achievement might be making a character move across a screen. At drama club, it might be speaking two lines without looking at the floor. Neither sounds spectacular. Both matter, because they turn “I cannot do that” into “I could learn”. The point is not to arrive with a talent ready to display; it is to discover what practice can do.

There is another advantage that a club poster cannot quite capture: having something to talk about. Walking into a room of strangers is awkward. Solving a puzzle or rehearsing a scene together gives that first conversation a purpose. You do not have to produce a brilliant opening line. “How does this work?” is enough. Friendships are not guaranteed, of course, but shared interests make meeting people feel less like an interview.

Perhaps your real objection is time. Homework, travel and family commitments already compete for your afternoons, and pretending otherwise would be unfair. Start by asking what is possible. Is there a lunchtime taster? Can you bring a friend? Does the club expect a weekly commitment? A sensible choice fits your life; it does not make you feel guilty for having one. If a particular session cannot work, that is a reason to investigate another option rather than abandon the idea entirely.

So tomorrow, pause at the noticeboard. Find one activity that makes you curious, ask one question and give it one visit. You may decide it is not for you. You may also discover that the room you kept walking past is exactly where you want to spend your next lunchtime.

### Why the article holds together

| Part | The writing choice | What it achieves |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Noticeboard scene, followed quickly by the first-visit idea | Establishes the school audience and a manageable proposal. |
| Skills paragraph | Two small examples, then an explanation | Develops the benefit rather than merely claiming “confidence”. |
| Friendship paragraph | A different benefit and a realistic limitation | Adds substance without promising that everyone will make friends. |
| Time paragraph | Takes an objection seriously and offers practical questions | Sounds fair; the response is useful rather than dismissive. |
| Ending | Returns to the noticeboard and the “one” pattern | Connects the whole piece and gives a specific next step. |

The phrase **“Neither sounds spectacular. Both matter”** uses short sentences to emphasise a turning point. Longer sentences then explain the idea. The article has a friendly voice, but it still uses complete sentences and accurate punctuation.

## Persuasion that grows from the idea

Persuasion is helping a reader see **why a claim matters**. Think of a bridge: reasons and examples are its supports. Attractive language cannot replace missing supports.

| Choice | Example | When it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Direct address | “You only need to try one session.” | Makes a manageable action feel personally relevant. Avoid accusing the reader. |
| Inclusive language | “We all need somewhere to begin.” | Builds a shared concern when the claim is fair. |
| Rhetorical question | “Why should being new mean being unwelcome?” | Sharpens an issue; follow with reasoning rather than more questions. |
| Contrast | “A first attempt, not a final judgement.” | Clarifies the difference that supports your argument. |
| Three-part pattern | “Ask a question, try a session, make your own decision.” | Gives a final recommendation rhythm and direction. |
| Anecdote or example | A nervous newcomer completes a small task. | Makes an abstract benefit concrete; keep it relevant and brief. |
| Carefully chosen emotion | “No one should spend lunchtime feeling invisible.” | Shows the human consequence, without exaggerated claims. |

**Use a technique because it helps the meaning.** Repeating a device checklist can produce an artificial voice. Writing “This is a rhetorical question” in your article would break that voice completely.

### Answer a counterargument fairly

A **counterargument** is a reasonable objection. A **concession** admits where it has some truth. A **response** then explains why your position still makes sense.

“An after-school club will not suit everyone, especially students with a long journey home. A lunchtime trial would offer another route in. The aim should be more choice, rather than another demand on everyone’s timetable.”

That is stronger than “Some people disagree, but they are wrong.” Not every advice piece needs a formal opposing argument; use one when it improves the task you are writing.

### Answer a reasonable objection

**Claim:** A quiet lunchtime room would help pupils who need a break from noise.

**Objection:** The school may not have staff available every day.

**Developed response:** Start with two supervised lunchtimes each week, using an existing room. This limits the demand on staff while allowing the school to see whether pupils use it.

The objection changes the proposal into something more workable. This is **reasoned persuasion**, rather than adding an opposing view only to dismiss it. Use a hypothetical example as a possibility, not as a fabricated survey result or invented expert quotation.

## Change the form without losing the purpose

An article is only one possible form. **Recognisable conventions help the reader, but developed prose does most of the work.**

| Form | Useful shape and conventions | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Article | Relevant headline; engaging opening; developed paragraphs; purposeful ending. Subheadings can help a guide. | Writing an essay addressed to an examiner, or spending time drawing a magazine layout. |
| Formal letter | Appropriate greeting; clear reason for writing; developed case; specific request or conclusion; suitable sign-off. | Rudeness, vague complaints or asking the recipient to do something outside their control. |
| Speech or talk | Address listeners; clear opening; audible signposting; connected ideas; memorable close. | Listing stage directions or forgetting that the writing must sound natural when spoken. |
| Review | Identify what is reviewed; select criteria; describe enough to support judgement; recommend for a particular audience. | Retelling the whole plot instead of evaluating the experience. |
| Report | State purpose; group findings clearly, often with headings; explain what they imply; recommend if asked. | Making unsupported claims sound like measured findings. |
| Guide or leaflet | Organise advice around the reader’s needs; clear sections; practical detail and explanations. | A decorative page or a bare checklist with too little developed writing. |

### Letters

For an unknown recipient: **Dear Sir or Madam** and **Yours faithfully**. For a named recipient, such as **Dear Ms Patel**, use **Yours sincerely**. A letter to an editor can use **Dear Editor**. Keep any address block brief; do not let formatting consume the writing time.

**Firm and courteous:** “I am concerned that removing the lunchtime club would leave students with fewer supervised activities.”

**Too hostile:** “Your ridiculous plan proves you do not care about anyone.”

Challenge the proposal, explain its consequences and offer an achievable alternative. A letter to a friend would need a different register.

### Speeches and talks

Write the words you would actually say. A class talk can be warm and humorous; a speech to a formal panel may be more restrained. Link abstract ideas to familiar situations. Use short signposts such as “The first problem is ...” so listeners can follow without rereading.

### Reviews and advice

A useful review combines **detail + judgement + reason**: “The clear beginners’ instructions make the game easy to learn, although experienced players may want a harder challenge.” End by explaining **who would enjoy it and why**.

Useful advice combines **action + how + why**: “Choose a club you can reach at lunchtime. That removes the need for another journey and makes a first visit easier to fit into your week.”

### Choose conventions that serve the task

| Form | Useful conventions | Keep the main focus on |
|---|---|---|
| Article | A relevant headline and a clear opening that introduces the issue. | Ideas developed for the stated readership. |
| Formal letter | An appropriate greeting, clear reason for writing and suitable closing. | A precise request or argument in a consistent register. |
| Speech | Direct address, clear signposting and sentences that work aloud. | Leading listeners through a reasoned case. |
| Review | Identification of what is reviewed, specific evidence and a judgement. | Helping the audience decide whether it suits them. |
| Report | A clear purpose and organised findings, with headings where useful. | Evidence, explanation and relevant recommendations. |

These conventions orient the reader; they do not replace developed content. A headline alone does not make a piece a successful article. Avoid spending time drawing a newspaper layout when clear prose will communicate the ideas.

## Control sentences and vocabulary

**Precision beats unnecessary complexity.** “The fee excludes students on tight budgets” says more than “The fee is an incredibly terrible and unacceptable thing.” Choose words you can use accurately.

Vary sentences to control pace and emphasis:

- **Short:** “That matters.” Useful after a developed explanation, not instead of one.
- **Compound:** “The club is free, but students still need time to attend.” Two complete ideas joined by a conjunction.
- **Complex:** “Although the club is free, students still need time to attend.” The opening subordinate clause cannot stand alone here.

**Comma splice:** “The club is free, everyone can try it.” Two complete sentences have been joined by only a comma.

**Repairs:** “The club is free, so everyone can try it.” / “The club is free. Everyone can try it.” / “The club is free; everyone can try it.” Choose the version that suits your meaning. A comma can correctly separate clauses when used with the right conjunction or structure.

| Punctuation | Use it for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Full stop | A clear sentence boundary | “The first visit is free. Ask about later costs.” |
| Colon | An explanation or list after a complete clause | “The first step is simple: ask when the club meets.” |
| Semicolon | Two closely linked complete clauses | “The session is short; the opportunity is worthwhile.” |
| Apostrophe | Possession or omitted letters | “a student's choice”; “students' choices”; “it's” = “it is”. |
| Commas | Clauses, lists and added information where needed | “If you are unsure, ask the organiser.” |

**Its** shows possession; **it's** means “it is” or “it has”. **Their** shows possession, **there** refers to a place or introduces something, and **they're** means “they are”. **Could have**, not “could of”.

Do not force every punctuation mark into a piece. Control and appropriateness matter more than display.

## Edit for meaning, then accuracy

Read the ending against the task first: did you achieve the required purpose for the named reader? Then check a manageable set of things.

1. **Relevance:** does each paragraph add a different useful idea?
2. **Development:** have you explained how or why, with a specific detail?
3. **Organisation:** is there a clear route from opening to ending? Do links express the right relationship?
4. **Voice:** does the tone stay suitable, especially when disagreeing?
5. **Accuracy:** check sentence boundaries, tense, agreement, apostrophes and spellings from the question.

**Before:** “Everyone need clubs, its obvious they make every single student successful.”

**After:** “Clubs can give students a place to practise new skills. A beginner who learns a short piece of music, for example, has a clear sign that steady practice can lead to improvement.”

The rewrite fixes **agreement** and the **comma splice**, removes an unsupported absolute claim and develops a credible example. It improves both meaning and accuracy.

### Common traps and better choices

- **Listing:** “cheap, fun, useful” → choose a point and show what it means in practice.
- **Repeating:** three paragraphs saying “good for confidence” → give each paragraph a new job.
- **Overclaiming:** “everyone will succeed” → make a proportionate claim and support it.
- **Overwriting:** a long dramatic introduction → reach the task’s central issue quickly.
- **Device collecting:** endless questions and exclamation marks → use a few choices with a clear purpose.
- **Memorised answer:** forcing a prepared piece onto a new task → adapt the plan and details to the actual reader.
- **One polished response, one rushed response:** protect time for both tasks, including checking.
