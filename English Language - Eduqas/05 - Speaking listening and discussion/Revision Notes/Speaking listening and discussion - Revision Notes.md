# Speaking listening and discussion

## Know what the assessment involves

A presentation is a **guided journey through an idea**. Your audience should understand where you are going, why the subject matters and what each example adds. Speaking is more than reading an essay aloud: the listener cannot turn back a page to untangle a sentence.

Prepare a formal presentation on a specific topic agreed with your teacher. You also need to **listen and respond to questions or feedback**. Notes and slides are allowed, but neither is compulsory. As a guide, the whole assessment should take no more than about ten minutes, including questions. Agree practical arrangements and any access needs with your teacher.

The result is a separate **Pass, Merit or Distinction** endorsement; a performance below Pass is Not Classified. It does not add to the written GCSE grade, and no numerical marks are assigned.

You need to be audible and intelligible, using language appropriate to a formal setting. Spoken Standard English does not mean copying a particular accent.

| Level | What the performance demonstrates |
|---|---|
| Pass | Clear straightforward ideas, an attempt at organisation and audience awareness, and appropriate straightforward responses. |
| Merit | Challenging ideas, suitable vocabulary, clear purposeful organisation and formal responses with detail. |
| Distinction | Sophisticated ideas and vocabulary, effective audience engagement, achievement of purpose and perceptive responses that develop ideas where appropriate. |

The performance must meet all the criteria for its level. A list of devices or an impressive opening cannot replace the rest of the presentation and discussion.

## Choose a focused purpose and audience

Turn a broad subject into a question you can explore. **School life** is too wide; **Should our school offer a quiet lunchtime space?** gives a manageable purpose, audience and decision.

Choose something you can discuss beyond a memorised script. A personal interest can work, but give the audience a reason to care. You might explain an overlooked problem, argue for a change or explore a question with competing answers.

Before researching, write:

- **Audience:** classmates and teachers who use the school at lunchtime.
- **Purpose:** persuade them to support a small trial of a quiet space.
- **Central claim:** pupils need a choice of environment, not one preferred kind of lunchtime.
- **Question to investigate:** how could the school provide it fairly and practically?

This keeps the talk focused. A specific proposal creates more room for developed reasoning than a long list of complaints.

## Develop points rather than list them

Build each main point through **claim, reason, example and significance**. The example makes the idea concrete; the final explanation shows why it matters to this audience.

**Claim:** a quiet space could give pupils a choice. **Reason:** the same crowded setting does not suit everyone every day. **Example:** someone preparing for an afternoon presentation may want ten calm minutes. **Significance:** offering an option supports participation without demanding that everyone spend lunchtime in silence.

This is an illustrative situation, not a statistic about all pupils. If you use research, check its source, date, sample and relevance. Say *a survey of our class* rather than *students everywhere* when that is the limit of the evidence. Never invent a percentage to sound convincing.

Include a meaningful **counterargument**. Rooms and supervision cost time. Answer the actual difficulty with a workable trial, not with the claim that anyone who disagrees is selfish. A qualified answer can show a more developed understanding than absolute certainty.

## Organise a talk the audience can follow

A useful route is **focus → reasons and examples → challenge → response → conclusion**. Adapt it to the purpose instead of forcing every talk into the same mould.

1. **Opening:** establish a concrete situation and the central question.
2. **Development:** explore a few connected points, with enough explanation to make each worthwhile.
3. **Qualification:** acknowledge a real limitation or alternative view.
4. **Conclusion:** return to the purpose and give a clear final thought or action.

Signpost a change with natural phrases: *The practical problem is…*, *That brings me to…*, or *The strongest objection is…*. A listener needs these connections more than a written reader does.

An ending should complete the argument, not introduce a new major issue. For the quiet-space proposal: **“We do not need one perfect lunchtime. We need room for different ones.”** The contrast returns to choice and makes the recommendation memorable.

## Make language work when spoken

Use precise vocabulary you understand. Explain a technical term if the audience needs it. **Formal** means controlled and appropriate, not packed with long words.

- **Direct address:** use *you* when it genuinely connects an experience to listeners.
- **Inclusive language:** *we* can establish shared responsibility, but do not pretend everyone agrees.
- **Contrast:** distinguish a quiet option from a demand for universal silence.
- **Repetition:** repeat a short key phrase at a useful moment, rather than repeating the whole argument.
- **Question:** create a real issue that the talk then explores. Too many unanswered rhetorical questions can become tiring.

Write sentences that can be heard on one listening. Break a complicated explanation into stages. Read it aloud: if you cannot say it naturally or keep losing the main point, simplify its construction.

## Deliver with control

**Audibility and clarity come first.** Speak to the audience rather than down into your notes. Check that a listener at the back can hear you. Adapt the room or equipment with your teacher when needed.

Use a pace that lets people follow a new idea. Pause after a significant question, a contrast or the end of a point. Vary emphasis to make priorities clear; shouting every sentence removes that contrast.

Use a comfortable posture and natural gestures. Find a manageable way to connect with the audience, such as looking towards different parts of the room. Continuous eye contact and dramatic acting are not the assessment in themselves.

If you lose your place, pause, find the next keyword and continue. A brief correction is usually clearer than repeatedly apologising or rushing to catch up. Rehearse recovery as well as the opening.

## Use notes, slides and rehearsal purposefully

Cue cards can carry **a point, an example and a transition**. For one section: *choice — afternoon presentation example — not everyone needs the same thing*. Keep enough detail to help without making every glance a search through a dense page.

A slide should add something useful: a clear image, a short quotation, a labelled result or the proposal itself. Explain what the audience should notice. Do not make listeners read a paragraph while you say a different paragraph.

Rehearse aloud with a timer, leaving room for questions. Practise with someone who can tell you where the reasoning became difficult to follow. Ask for feedback on one or two specific things, such as pace or whether the objection was answered.

After rehearsal, revise an unclear explanation or remove repetition. Do not simply learn to say an overcrowded script faster. You need enough command of the idea to adapt when a listener asks something unexpected.

## Listen and respond thoughtfully

Listen to the whole question. Identify its central concern before replying. If the wording is unclear, briefly check what the questioner means.

Use **direct answer → reason → example or qualification**. For *Would a quiet room make pupils more isolated?*:

**“It could if pupils felt excluded from other spaces. That is why I would make it an open choice, not a room for a labelled group. Someone could use it one day and join friends outside the next. I would also ask pupils during the trial whether it increased choice or made separation worse.”**

This recognises the risk and develops the proposal. It does not dodge the objection by repeating the original speech.

If you do not know a fact, say so clearly and explain what would be needed to find out: **“I do not have a staffing cost. The next step would be to ask the school before proposing a timetable.”** Honest limits are more convincing than invented certainty.

## Discuss, refine and reflect

In a discussion or debate, respond to another person's actual point. Acknowledge a useful contribution, explain agreement or disagreement and add a reason. Help the discussion progress rather than treating it as a competition to speak most often.

Feedback is information to work with. **“The example was clear, but I could not see how it supported your proposal”** suggests that the missing step is explanation. Add that link rather than changing every word.

Before the final presentation, check:

- A specific purpose and a coherent route through the ideas.
- Evidence or clearly identified examples supporting the claims.
- Appropriate vocabulary, audible delivery and controlled pace.
- A fair response to a serious alternative view.
- Readiness to explain, qualify and develop ideas in the questions.

The Q&A includes a complete quiet-space speech and model follow-up responses. Use the relationship between its claim, examples and limitations to develop your own topic.
