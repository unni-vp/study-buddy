# Phonics and sound symbol correspondences

## From letters to sounds

French spelling works like a code: a **group of letters can make one sound**, and some written letters are silent. Learn the group, not an English sound for every letter.

The symbols between /slashes/ show sounds. Examples and mouth position help you connect them to real French. Use the [pronunciation sound bank](https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/french/gcse/french-8652/soundbank) to hear words spoken.

Keep vowels steady rather than sliding between two sounds. French rhythm groups words into phrases; the final spoken syllable of a phrase often receives emphasis. Do not stress every word as you would in English.

## Clear vowel contrasts

| Spelling | Sound and useful example |
|---|---|
| a | /a/: avec, arriver; open the mouth |
| i / y | /i/: public, physique; a high front vowel |
| closed eu | /ø/: deux, peu; tongue forward, lips rounded |
| e | /ə/: le, de; a short, relaxed vowel that can sometimes disappear in connected speech |
| au / eau / closed o / ô | /o/: beau, sauf; rounded lips, a steady vowel |
| ou | /u/: sous, toujours; tongue towards the back, rounded lips |
| u | /y/: dur, connu; tongue as for /i/, with rounded lips |
| silent final e | jeune, chaque; final written e usually adds no syllable, but the preceding consonant is heard |
| é / infinitive -er / verb -ez | /e/: été, parler, parlez; do not pronounce -er as two separate letters |
| è / ê / ai | /ɛ/: collège, fête, vrai; more open than /e/ |
| oi / oy | /wa/, often /waj/ for oy before another vowel: mois, moyen |
| open eu / œu | /œ/: leur, cœur; more open than the vowel in deux |
| open o | /ɔ/: porte, alors; more open than the vowel in beau |

**Ou and u are different:** sous /su/ and su /sy/ do not sound the same. For u, keep your tongue forward and round your lips. For ou, move the tongue further back.

**É and è differ:** the vowel in été is closer; the vowel in mère is more open. An accent can change pronunciation and spelling.

### Practise a contrast as a mouth movement

For **u /y/**, keep the tongue position of French **i** while rounding the lips. For **ou /u/**, the tongue is farther back. Compare **tu** and **tout** slowly, then use each in a phrase. The two words are not interchangeable sounds.

For **é /e/** and **è /ɛ/**, keep the vowel steady. The mouth is more open for è. A written **-er**, a past participle in **-é** and the ending **-ez** can sound alike, so pronunciation alone cannot choose the spelling in dictation.

## Nasal vowels

A nasal vowel sends some air through the nose. Do not add a separate strong n or m after it. The spelling is a guide to the vowel, rather than another consonant to pronounce.

| Spelling | Sound | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| en / an / em / am | /ɑ̃/ | grand, immédiatement |
| on / om | /ɔ̃/ | bon, tomber |
| ain / in / aim / im | /ɛ̃/ | matin, faim |
| -ien | /jɛ̃/ | rien, canadien |
| un | /œ̃/, often merged with /ɛ̃/ in modern accents | lundi, emprunter |

These combinations are commonly nasal before a consonant or at the end of a word. A following vowel or doubled consonant can change the pattern: **bon** has a nasal vowel; **bonne** has a pronounced n and a different vowel.

Avoid confusing **an, on and in**. The final sounds in grand, bon and matin are different.

### Notice when the consonant returns

In **bon**, the written on represents a nasal vowel; do not add a separate final n. In **bonne**, the n is pronounced and the vowel is no longer the same nasal sound. Learn the whole pair with its spelling.

Before a vowel-starting word, liaison can make a normally silent consonant audible: **un ami** includes a linking n. This does not mean pronouncing every written n in every nasal vowel. Listen to the complete phrase in the sound bank.

## Consonant groups

| Spelling | Sound and example |
|---|---|
| ch | /ʃ/: chose, marché; like the start of English ship |
| ç / soft c | /s/: façon, français; c is normally soft before e/i/y |
| qu | /k/: banque, informatique; usually no extra w sound |
| j | /ʒ/: jusque, jeunesse; a voiced sound like the middle of English measure |
| -tion | /sjɔ̃/: application, construction; not English shun |
| h | no h sound: homme, hôpital; h aspiré words block liaison despite the silent h |
| -gn- | /ɲ/: campagne, gagner; the n-like sound is formed further back, with the tongue against the palate |
| r | /ʁ/: rester, comprendre; made towards the back of the mouth, without an English r glide |
| -s- | /s/ in chanson, sensible; a single s between vowel sounds is usually /z/, as in maison |
| th | /t/: thème, bibliothèque; not the English th sound |
| -ill- / -ille | often /j/: famille, brouillard; ville is an important /l/ exception |
| -aill- / -ail | /aj/: taille, travail |

For c, **ca/co/cu** usually have /k/; **ce/ci/cy** usually have /s/. A cedilla keeps /s/ before a/o/u: français, garçon.

## Silent endings and liaison

**Silent final consonants:** many final -s, -t, -d, -x are silent: petits, grand, deux. It is not a rule that every final consonant is silent: avec and neuf have a final sound; a final -r can be heard in words such as partir.

Regular -er present **-e, -es and -ent** can sound alike: parle, parles, parlent. The spelling still depends on the subject. **-Ent** is silent as a verb ending, but not in every word containing these letters.

**Liaison** makes a normally silent ending audible before the next word's vowel sound, when that word combination permits it.

| Written ending | Linking sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| s | /z/ | les amis |
| t | /t/ | petit ami |
| n | /n/ | un ami |
| x | /z/ | deux amis |

Do not add liaison everywhere. There is **no liaison after et**. A silent h may allow it (les hommes), while an h aspiré blocks it (les héros).

### Read and spell the whole phrase

**Les petits enfants jouent dans le jardin.**

- Les petits: no vowel-starting second word, so no liaison between these two words.
- Petits enfants: a linking /z/ may be heard.
- Jouent: its -ent ending is silent.
- Jardin: the final in is a nasal vowel.

### Common mistakes

- Saying the final -s in every plural.
- Treating u and ou as the same sound.
- Pronouncing a separate n after every nasal vowel.
- Reading -tion with an English sound.
- Pronouncing French th as English th.
- Omitting written agreement because the ending cannot be heard.

Sound helps you read aloud; grammar helps you choose the correct silent letters in dictation.

### Sound and spelling need different checks

**Je parle**, **tu parles** and **ils parlent** have similar-sounding verb endings, but their written forms match different subjects. Read the subject before choosing an ending. In dictation, a silent letter may need grammatical evidence rather than a separate sound.

**Les amis** has a linking /z/. **Et** does not take liaison before the next vowel. First identify the word combination, then decide whether liaison belongs there; adding a linking sound everywhere makes speech less accurate.
