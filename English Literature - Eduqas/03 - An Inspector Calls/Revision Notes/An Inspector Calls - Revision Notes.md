# An Inspector Calls

## The central idea

A wealthy family's celebration is interrupted by a visitor who reveals how their choices have harmed a vulnerable woman. The central question is **whether people accept responsibility when their actions affect others**.

Think of a chain of falling dominoes. One person's decision changes somebody else's situation, making the next harm easier. The play connects separate decisions into a shared moral problem.

The action takes place in **Brumley in 1912**, in the Birlings' dining room. Priestley wrote the play in **1945**. This gap gives audiences knowledge that the characters lack, especially about war and the Titanic.

## Act 1: celebration interrupted

**What happens:** Arthur and Sybil Birling celebrate Sheila's engagement to Gerald Croft with their son Eric. Arthur discusses business, social advancement and individual self-interest. Inspector Goole arrives and reports a young woman's death. Arthur admits dismissing Eva Smith after a strike for better pay. Sheila admits using her influence to have Eva dismissed from a shop. Gerald recognises the name Daisy Renton.

**What it means:** the family's comfort rests partly on unequal power. Arthur can remove somebody's income; Sheila can turn a personal grievance into unemployment. The Inspector redirects attention from the prosperous family to the person they have harmed.

**Methods:** the doorbell interrupts Arthur's confidence. The contrast between celebration and investigation creates tension. Knowledge of the Titanic's later sinking makes Arthur's certainty an example of **dramatic irony**: the audience knows more than he does.

## Act 2: respectable appearances break down

**What happens:** Gerald admits an affair with Daisy. He provided shelter, but the relationship depended on his money and ended when he chose. Sheila questions the engagement and returns the ring. Sybil admits refusing help to a pregnant woman who approached her charity. She condemns the unknown father without realising he is Eric.

**What it means:** apparent kindness can coexist with unequal power. Gerald helped Daisy, yet had much more control over the arrangement. Sybil mistakes social status for moral worth and refuses to examine her own part.

**Methods:** the audience can work out Eric's involvement before Sybil does. Her condemnation creates **dramatic irony** and suspense. Sheila's growing understanding contrasts with her mother's resistance.

## Act 3: confession and the final reversal

**What happens:** Eric admits a sexual encounter involving pressure, the pregnancy and taking money from his father's business to help Eva. Eva refused the money when she learned it was stolen. The Inspector connects everyone's conduct and leaves. Gerald questions whether Goole was a real police inspector. A telephone enquiry suggests no such death has yet been reported. Arthur, Sybil and Gerald are relieved; Sheila and Eric still recognise their wrongdoing. A final call reports a young woman's death and an inspector's arrival.

**What it means:** being caught and being wrong are different. Some characters relax when punishment appears unlikely. Sheila and Eric understand that their conduct still matters.

**Methods:** the **cyclical ending** returns the family to the prospect of an investigation. The sudden telephone call overturns their relief and leaves the audience with the moral question. The Inspector's identity remains uncertain; do not present one explanation as a proven fact.

## Arthur Birling

- **Role:** wealthy manufacturer, father and spokesman for self-interest. He hopes the marriage will improve his business and social standing.
- **Choices:** dismisses Eva for seeking higher wages, resists responsibility and worries about scandal.
- **Development:** his authority is weakened by the investigation, but he does not show Sheila's lasting change.
- **Quotation:** “lower costs” and “higher prices” — the paired commercial aims reduce relationships to profit. **Language:** the economic vocabulary contrasts with the Inspector's concern for human welfare.

Arthur's wrong predictions also weaken trust in his wider opinions. Explain this connection rather than merely calling him foolish.

## Sybil Birling

- **Role:** upholds class boundaries and the family's respectable appearance.
- **Choices:** rejects Eva's appeal to the charity partly because she dislikes her manner and use of the Birling name.
- **Development:** condemns the father before discovering Eric's involvement; still resists responsibility afterwards.
- **Quotation:** “girls of that class” — the distancing phrase turns individuals into an inferior social category. **Language:** the demonstrative and class label expose prejudice.

A charity role does not make her automatically compassionate. Priestley tests what she actually does with her power.

## Sheila Birling

- **Role:** begins as a sheltered daughter but becomes increasingly perceptive.
- **Choices:** admits that jealousy and anger led her to misuse influence at the shop.
- **Development:** accepts guilt, questions Gerald and understands that the lessons remain important even if Goole's identity is doubtful.
- **Quotation:** “these girls aren't cheap labour” — she challenges the reduction of workers to a business expense. **Language:** the contrast between people and their economic value signals a change in outlook.

Her remorse does not erase the harm. Her willingness to face it gives the play a possibility of change.

## Eric Birling

- **Role:** uneasy son whose drinking and strained relationship with his parents conceal serious wrongdoing.
- **Choices:** uses pressure in his encounter with Eva, then steals money to help her. Neither attempted help nor later remorse excuses the earlier harm.
- **Development:** admits his actions and continues to acknowledge responsibility when others become relieved.
- **Quotation:** “in that state” — his indirect description of drunken conduct avoids saying clearly what he did. **Language:** the evasive phrase exposes shame and discomfort.

Eric is a flawed agent of change, not a simple moral hero. Priestley allows regret while keeping the consequences visible.

## Gerald Croft

- **Role:** socially desirable fiancé and link to a more prestigious business family.
- **Choices:** rescues Daisy from a threatening situation, gives her somewhere to live and begins an unequal affair.
- **Development:** initially admits the relationship, but later investigates Goole's identity and joins the older Birlings' relief.
- **Quotation:** “young and fresh and charming” — the three admiring descriptions foreground Daisy's attraction to him. **Language:** the listing helps expose the personal desire within his account of rescue.

He is neither wholly cruel nor wholly selfless. A stronger answer recognises both help and control.

## Inspector Goole and Eva Smith

### The Inspector

The Inspector controls the pace of disclosure, challenges evasions and temporarily displaces Arthur's authority. He acts as a **moral investigator**, although his precise identity is unresolved.

**Quotation:** “one body” — the **metaphor** presents society as interconnected: harm to one part matters to the whole. His speeches broaden the enquiry from one household to everyone affected by selfish choices.

### Eva Smith / Daisy Renton

Eva never appears on stage. The audience learns about her through others, which reflects how powerful people control the telling of a vulnerable person's story.

She seeks fair pay, loses work, experiences unequal relationships and refuses stolen money. These details give her moral agency; she is not simply a passive object of pity. The common surname can suggest that her situation represents many ordinary people.

## Responsibility and social inequality

**Responsibility:** the Inspector links decisions made at different times. Sheila and Eric accept the moral lesson; others focus on avoiding consequences. The final call forces the issue back into view.

**Class and power:** Arthur controls employment; Sheila influences a shop; Sybil controls charitable help. Their different kinds of power all affect Eva's options. Contrast their secure dining room with the precarious life described outside it.

**Money and individualism:** Arthur treats success as personal achievement. The investigation reveals that his choices have effects beyond his family. Gerald's financial help is valuable, but also gives him power in the relationship.

Use a specific action to explain each theme. Do not replace the play with a general speech about rich and poor people.

## Gender, generations and appearance

**Gender:** Eva's low-paid work and dependence on more powerful men make her vulnerable. Sheila's initial focus on the engagement changes as she challenges male accounts. Do not claim that every woman responds in the same way: Sybil defends the hierarchy.

**Generations:** Sheila and Eric become more willing to accept responsibility than their parents. The younger characters still begin with serious faults. Gerald's position complicates a simple young-good, old-bad reading.

**Appearance and reality:** respectable roles conceal selfishness, betrayal and coercion. The important contrast is between the family's reputation and its behaviour.

**Guilt and change:** feeling ashamed is only a start. The ending tests whether a lesson changes somebody's values when a legal penalty seems avoidable.

## Dramatic methods and context

- **Stage directions and lighting:** the opening is “pink” and “intimate”. The lighting becomes harsher when the Inspector arrives. The visual change suggests comfortable appearances being exposed.
- **Single setting:** the dining room keeps the characters together, like a pressure cooker. Eva's story enters this protected space through the investigation.
- **Entrances and exits:** arrivals interrupt speeches and alter who controls a scene.
- **Cliff-hangers:** the breaks after Gerald's recognition and before Eric's confession encourage the audience to anticipate revelations.
- **Dramatic irony:** Arthur's predictions and Sybil's condemnation reveal limits to their judgement.
- **Detective form:** evidence is disclosed gradually, but the central mystery becomes a moral one.
- **Morality-play features:** the visitor confronts wrongdoing and offers a lesson. This is an interpretation of the form, not proof that Goole is a ghost.

The 1912 setting foregrounds class divisions, limited opportunities and complacency before major conflict. Writing after two world wars gave Priestley a perspective from which to challenge that confidence. Use context to illuminate a particular choice, rather than adding unrelated history.

## Building an answer

The source-based task asks you to discuss the printed extract **and the play as a whole**. Read the wording carefully. An answer focused only on the extract misses the wider development; a memorised essay may miss the task.

1. Give a direct argument about the named character or theme.
2. Explain an important moment in the extract, with short evidence.
3. Analyse a precise word, stage direction, contrast or structural choice.
4. Connect it to a relevant moment elsewhere in the play.
5. Show what develops or remains unchanged.

### Example of a developed argument

Priestley makes Sheila's willingness to admit wrongdoing a source of hope. Her dismissal of Eva began in jealousy, so her later insight does not make her innocent. Instead, the contrast between that impulsive action and her questioning of Gerald shows that she is learning to examine power. Her parents' relief after the investigation appears doubtful makes her continuing concern more significant. The ending therefore tests moral growth through changed attitudes, rather than through the avoidance of punishment.

### Common mistakes

- Retelling every event without linking it to the question.
- Treating all five people's actions as identical or equally motivated.
- Saying the final call proves a single explanation of Goole.
- Discussing language without explaining its meaning or stage effect.
- Excusing Eric's conduct because he later regrets it.
- Adding historical facts without using them to clarify the play.
