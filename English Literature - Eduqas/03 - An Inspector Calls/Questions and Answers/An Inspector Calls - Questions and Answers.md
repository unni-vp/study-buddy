# An Inspector Calls questions & answers

## 1 mark

**1.** In which year is the play set?

**Answer:** 1912.

**2.** In which fictional town do the Birlings live?

**Answer:** Brumley.

**3.** Whose engagement is being celebrated?

**Answer:** Sheila and Gerald's.

**4.** Why does Arthur dismiss Eva from his factory?

**Answer:** She takes part in a strike for higher wages.

**5.** Which character gets Eva dismissed from a shop?

**Answer:** Sheila.

**6.** What name does Gerald recognise during the investigation?

**Answer:** Daisy Renton.

**7.** Which character refuses the pregnant woman's application for charitable help?

**Answer:** Sybil Birling.

**8.** Who is revealed to be the father of Eva's unborn child?

**Answer:** Eric.

**9.** What is dramatic irony?

**Answer:** The audience knows something that a character does not.

**10.** What interrupts the family's relief at the end?

**Answer:** A telephone call announcing a young woman's death and an inspector's arrival.

## 2 marks

**11.** Explain why Arthur's predictions about the Titanic weaken his authority.

**Answer:**

- The audience knows the ship later sank, so his certainty is wrong.
- This **dramatic irony** encourages doubt about his confident views on business and society too.

**12.** Explain why Sheila's actions at the shop show unequal power.

**Answer:**

- As a wealthy customer, she can influence the manager's decision.
- Her jealousy can therefore cost Eva her job and income.

**13.** Explain why Gerald's relationship with Daisy is not simply a story of kindness.

**Answer:**

- He does provide protection and shelter.
- His money and social position also give him more control, so the help exists within an unequal relationship.

**14.** Explain the irony in Sybil's condemnation of the unknown father.

**Answer:**

- She demands punishment before understanding who he is.
- Her condemnation then applies to her own son Eric, exposing her limited judgement.

**15.** Explain why Eva's refusal of stolen money matters.

**Answer:**

- It shows that she makes a moral choice despite being in need.
- This complicates prejudice that treats poor people as morally inferior.

**16.** How do Sheila and Eric differ from their parents after doubts arise about Goole?

**Answer:**

- They still recognise that their actions were wrong.
- Their parents focus on the possibility of avoiding scandal or punishment.

**17.** Give two ways the Inspector controls the investigation.

**Answer:**

- He directs the order in which the characters are questioned.
- He challenges evasions and redirects attention towards the consequences for Eva.

**18.** Explain why Eva's absence from the stage is significant.

**Answer:**

- Her story reaches the audience through more powerful people.
- This reflects her lack of control over how others describe her life.

**19.** Explain why feeling guilty does not make Sheila innocent.

**Answer:**

- Her remorse cannot undo Eva's dismissal or its effects.
- Its value lies in her willingness to accept responsibility and change her attitude.

**20.** Explain why the final call matters even if the first investigation was not genuine.

**Answer:**

- The family's harmful choices remain real within their own admissions.
- The renewed investigation prevents them simply escaping the moral lesson.

## 4 marks

**21.** Analyse the Inspector's image of society as one body.

**Answer:** The **metaphor** of “one body” presents people as connected parts of a whole. Harm to one part affects the wider community, challenging Arthur's individualism. The image enlarges responsibility beyond the family and makes the Inspector's message a social argument rather than simply a demand for one confession.

**22.** Analyse how Sybil's reference to class exposes her prejudice.

**Answer:** Her distancing phrase “that class” groups vulnerable people together as socially inferior. The **demonstrative** places them outside her own circle, while the category replaces attention to an individual's needs. Her charity role therefore becomes a source of exclusion, revealing a gap between respectable position and compassionate conduct.

**23.** Analyse the change in lighting when the Inspector arrives.

**Answer:** The opening lighting supports the family's comfortable celebration. Its later harshness makes the atmosphere feel exposed and less forgiving. As a **stage direction**, this changes what an audience sees as well as what it hears. The visual contrast supports the investigation's movement beneath respectable appearances.

**24.** Analyse why the ending can be described as cyclical.

**Answer:** The final telephone call returns the family to the prospect of a young woman's death being investigated. The **structural return** follows a brief period of relief, so it reverses their belief that consequences have disappeared. It also leaves the audience to ask whether the lesson will now be accepted, rather than offering a comfortable final resolution.

**25.** Analyse Eric's indirect description of his drunken conduct.

**Answer:** The phrase “in that state” avoids a direct account of what he did. This **evasive language** can reveal discomfort and shame, but also limits his willingness to name the harm clearly. His later admission matters, yet Priestley does not allow drunkenness or attempted financial help to excuse his coercive behaviour.

**26.** Analyse the function of the single dining-room setting.

**Answer:** The **confined setting** keeps the family under pressure as disclosures accumulate. Eva never enters their protected room, but the Inspector brings her experience into it. The contrast between their material comfort and the insecurity described outside prevents the celebration staying separate from its social consequences.

## 6 marks

**27.** Explain how Priestley develops Sheila across the three acts.

**Answer:** Sheila begins within the family's comfortable world and has used her customer influence impulsively against Eva. Her admission makes her wrongdoing clear, but she responds by questioning herself. In Act 2 she challenges Gerald's account and returns the ring, showing that she no longer accepts the engagement's appearance without examining its reality. In Act 3 she rejects her parents' relief because a doubtful investigation does not erase their conduct. Priestley uses **contrast and development** to present her as capable of learning, while preserving responsibility for the original harm.

**28.** Explain how responsibility links the family's separate actions.

**Answer:** Arthur's dismissal removes Eva's factory income, and Sheila's complaint costs her another job. Gerald provides help within an unequal affair, while Eric's conduct and Sybil's refusal of aid add further pressures. These actions have different motives and should not be treated as identical. The Inspector's **ordered disclosures** nevertheless make the characters see consequences beyond the moment of each choice. The family's changing reactions then reveal a second question: whether they value moral responsibility itself or only fear punishment.

**29.** Explain why the Inspector's identity should not be treated as a solved fact.

**Answer:** Goole appears to have unusually detailed knowledge and authority, but later enquiries cast doubt on his police identity. The final telephone call also unsettles the sequence of events. These features support different interpretations, including a supernatural warning or a dramatic moral agent, without conclusively proving one. The important **ambiguity** keeps attention on the characters' admitted actions. Sheila and Eric recognise that the ethical lesson survives even when his official status is questioned.

## 40 marks

**30.** Starting with the Inspector's challenge to the family's self-interest, discuss how Priestley presents responsibility across the play.

**Answer:**

Priestley presents responsibility as the recognition that private choices have consequences for others. The Inspector's challenge opposes Arthur's assumption that business success and family advantage can be separated from wider duties. His metaphor of “one body” makes society interconnected: individual security cannot justify indifference to the people outside it.

The investigation makes this argument concrete. Arthur dismisses Eva when workers seek higher wages, so managerial authority determines whether she has an income. Sheila later turns personal jealousy into another dismissal. Their motives differ, but the sequence exposes how several powerful people can narrow one woman's options without following her subsequent life. Priestley develops the moral pattern through ordered revelations rather than through an abstract debate alone.

Gerald complicates the division between kindness and selfishness. He helps Daisy escape a threatening situation, yet the relationship depends on his resources and can end at his choice. Eric's attempted financial help likewise cannot cancel the earlier pressure he used. These examples show that an apparently generous action does not remove the need to consider power and consent.

Sybil's refusal of charitable aid reveals the limits of respectable appearance. She judges the applicant through class prejudice instead of attending to her situation. The irony of condemning the father before identifying Eric makes her confidence collapse under the consequences she failed to understand.

After the Inspector leaves, the characters' reactions test whether his message has changed them. Sheila and Eric keep acknowledging wrongdoing, whereas others welcome the possibility that no official consequences will follow. The final call reverses that relief. Through this cyclical ending, Priestley refuses to let technical doubts settle the moral question. Responsibility depends on conduct and lasting values, not simply on being caught.

**31.** Starting with Sheila's admission that she caused Eva's dismissal from the shop, discuss how Priestley presents Sheila's development across the play.

**Answer:**

Priestley presents Sheila as capable of moral growth, while making her original harm undeniable. Her admission about the shop exposes jealousy supported by social influence: a wealthy customer's anger can remove a worker's livelihood. The scene therefore establishes the power she has misused before showing how she responds to recognising it.

Unlike her father, Sheila increasingly understands that workers are people whose experiences matter. She does not merely accept the Inspector's information; she begins to question her own assumptions. This contrast makes her development a change in judgement rather than a simple expression of embarrassment.

In Act 2, her response to Gerald extends that questioning into her personal life. His help to Daisy involved real protection but also an affair and unequal control. Sheila's decision to return the ring challenges the smooth appearance of the engagement. Priestley lets her recognise complexity without treating either Gerald or herself as wholly innocent.

Her insight also changes her relationship with the investigation. She recognises how disclosures fit together and warns others against resisting what they reveal. The **contrast between generations** develops through these interactions: her mother continues judging by class, while Sheila becomes more attentive to consequences. Yet youth itself does not guarantee goodness, since Sheila's initial behaviour helped create Eva's insecurity.

The decisive test follows Goole's departure. Doubts about his identity offer the family an excuse to return to normal. Sheila refuses that excuse because it does not undo the conduct they have admitted. Her continuing concern contrasts with her parents' relief and shows that the lesson has affected her values.

The final telephone call prevents a settled conclusion about what happens next. However, Sheila's changed response offers a possibility of a more responsible society. Priestley presents growth as a willingness to face harm and challenge comfortable assumptions, rather than as a claim that remorse has erased guilt.
