Act 5: collapse & restoration

Act 5 — Collapse and restoration

What happens

  • Lady Macbeth sleepwalks, rubbing her hands and revealing fragments of their crimes. A doctor and gentlewoman witness distress she can no longer conceal.
  • Malcolm's forces advance. Soldiers cut branches from Birnam Wood to hide their numbers, making the wood appear to move towards Dunsinane.
  • Macbeth hears that Lady Macbeth has died and speaks about life's emptiness. News of the moving wood begins to destroy his confidence.
  • He continues fighting, but Macduff reveals that he was cut from his mother's womb. Macbeth's interpretation of the birth prophecy fails. Macduff kills him and Malcolm is acclaimed king.

Why this act matters

Earlier images return with changed meanings. Lady Macbeth's imagined bloodstain contradicts her claim that a little water could clear the crime. Her fragmented prose and repeated hand-rubbing reveal a loss of control very different from her earlier commands.

The prophecies are fulfilled through meanings Macbeth did not expect. His final resistance recalls the courage praised at the start, but bravery cannot cancel his cruelty. Malcolm's closing speech promises repair. His description of the couple is condemnatory; the audience has also witnessed their private fears and wasted potential. Political order can be restored, but the dead cannot be brought back.

Important quotations

QuotationWho and whereWhat to analyse
“Out, damned spot”Lady Macbeth, 5.1Language / dramatic form — failed imperative within fragmented sleepwalking prose: The imperative fails to control an imagined stain. “Damned” gives guilt a moral and religious weight.
“Tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow”Macbeth, 5.5Language / structure — repetition in the late speech of despair: Repetition makes time feel monotonous and empty. His pursuit of a glorious future has ended in exhaustion.
“Life's but a walking shadow”Macbeth, 5.5Imagery — metaphor of insubstantial existence: The metaphor suggests something insubstantial and passing. It expresses his despair, rather than a fact the audience must accept about life.
“Untimely ripped”Macduff, 5.8Language / structure — violent verb resolving the deceptive prophecy: The violent wording reveals the unexpected meaning of the prophecy and destroys Macbeth's confidence in it.
“I will not yield”Macbeth, 5.8Language — defiant negative statement: The refusal shows continuing defiance after his assurances fail. It can preserve a trace of his soldierly courage without excusing him.
“dead butcher and his fiend-like queen”Malcolm, final sceneImagery / structure — condemnatory labels in the closing judgement: The brutal labels judge the pair by their destructive rule. Compare this public judgement with the complicated private lives Shakespeare has shown.
“the perfumes of Arabia”Lady Macbeth, 5.1Language / imagery — hyperbole and imagined fragrance: The exaggerated scale of possible cleansing recalls Macbeth's ocean. Even imagined fragrance cannot remove her sense of guilt.
“Turn, hellhound, turn!”Macduff, 5.8Language / imagery — repeated imperatives and demonic animal metaphor: Repeated imperatives demand confrontation, while animal and religious imagery condemn Macbeth's savagery.
“juggling fiends”Macbeth, 5.8Language / structure — deception metaphor and belated recognition: He finally recognises the deceptive promises. Awareness comes too late to undo actions driven by false confidence.

Turning point: Macbeth loses both the crown and the future he tried to force into existence. Lady Macbeth's death is reported rather than shown; Malcolm says she is thought to have taken her own life.

What the doctor can and cannot establish

The doctor observes Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking and repeated actions. He recognises a disturbance that his ordinary medical skills cannot cure. His religious language connects conscience with suffering, but he does not establish that she is possessed. Her words return to the crimes the audience already knows, making the scene a revelation of concealed guilt.

Malcolm's final speech promises political repair. Because the play ends before his reign is shown, describe the prospect of restored order, rather than claiming that the audience witnesses a successful reign.
