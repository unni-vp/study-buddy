Infection and response questions & answers

1 mark2 marks3 marks4 marks6 marks

1 mark

1. What is a pathogen?

Answer:

A microorganism that causes disease.

2. Which type of pathogen causes malaria?

Answer:

A protist.

3. Name the cell formed by fusing an antibody-producing lymphocyte with a tumour cell.

Answer:

Hybridoma.

4. Name the scientist who discovered penicillin.

Answer:

Alexander Fleming.

5. Which plant was the original source of chemicals used to develop aspirin?

Answer:

Willow.

6. Name the yellowing of leaves caused by magnesium deficiency.

Answer:

Chlorosis.

7. What does a valid control line on an antibody test strip show?

Answer:

That the test has worked.

2 marks

8. Give two ways bacteria can cause illness after entering the body.

Answer:
  • They multiply rapidly, increasing the number of bacteria present.
  • They may release toxins that damage tissues.

9. Describe how measles spreads and give one characteristic symptom.

Answer:
  • It spreads when people inhale droplets from infected people’s coughs or sneezes.
  • A red skin rash is a characteristic symptom; fever also occurs.

10. Give two ways to reduce the transmission of gonorrhoea.

Answer:
  • Use condoms to reduce transmission during sexual contact.
  • Treat infected people with an effective antibiotic, reducing the bacteria available to spread.

11. Explain why mosquito nets help reduce malaria transmission.

Answer:
  • Mosquitoes are vectors carrying the malaria protist.
  • Nets reduce mosquito bites, so fewer protists are transferred into people.

12. Explain how cilia and mucus protect the airways.

Answer:
  • Mucus traps pathogens and particles.
  • Cilia move the mucus towards the throat to be swallowed.

13. Explain the difference between antibodies and antitoxins.

Answer:
  • Antibodies bind to specific antigens on pathogens, helping the immune system destroy them.
  • Antitoxins neutralise toxins released by bacteria.

14. Why do antibiotics not treat influenza, and why is developing drugs against viruses difficult?

Answer:
  • Influenza is caused by a virus, which antibiotics do not kill.
  • Viruses reproduce inside body cells, so a drug may damage the body’s cells as well as affecting the virus.

15. Explain why antibiotic resistance is a concern.

Answer:
  • Some bacteria survive existing antibiotics, making infections harder to treat.
  • Developing effective replacements takes time and money, while infections may continue to spread.

16. Explain why a nitrate shortage can cause stunted plant growth.

Answer:
  • Nitrate ions are needed to make amino acids and proteins.
  • Without enough protein synthesis, fewer new cell materials can be made, so growth is reduced.

17. Explain how a waxy cuticle and poisonous chemicals protect a plant.

Answer:
  • The tough cuticle is a physical barrier resisting entry by microorganisms.
  • Poisons deter herbivores from feeding on the plant.

3 marks

18. Describe the progression from HIV infection to AIDS if infection is not successfully controlled.

Answer:
  • HIV may initially cause a flu-like illness.
  • The virus attacks and damages immune cells.
  • Severe immune damage leads to AIDS, when other infections or cancers can develop because defence is weakened.

19. Explain how removing infected leaves and applying fungicide help control rose black spot.

Answer:
  • Rose black spot is caused by a fungus.
  • Removing and destroying infected leaves removes a source of fungal spores, reducing spread by wind or water.
  • A fungicide acts against the fungus, reducing infection.

20. Explain why magnesium deficiency reduces a plant’s growth.

Answer:
  • Magnesium is needed to make chlorophyll.
  • Less chlorophyll means less light absorbed and a lower rate of photosynthesis.
  • Less glucose is available for respiration and making new plant material, so growth slows.

21. Explain why a monoclonal antibody is useful for locating one particular protein in a tissue sample.

Answer:
  • Its binding site is specific/complementary to an antigen on that protein.
  • Attach a fluorescent dye to the antibody so it binds to the target protein.
  • The fluorescent signal shows where the protein is located.

22. Explain how a hybridoma is used to produce a large supply of one antibody.

Answer:
  • Select a hybridoma making the required antibody and clone it.
  • Many identical cells divide and produce the same antibody.
  • Collect the antibodies from the culture and purify them.

23. A town records 54 cases of an infection among 18,000 people. Calculate cases per 100,000 people and explain why this measure helps compare towns.

Answer:
  • 54 ÷ 18,000 × 100,000.
  • 300 cases per 100,000 people.
  • It adjusts for different population sizes, making the comparison fairer than raw case counts.

24. A plant has yellow leaves. Give three ways of investigating whether a specific pathogen is responsible.

Answer:
  • Compare its symptoms with a reliable gardening manual or website.
  • Send a sample to a laboratory for pathogen identification.
  • Use a monoclonal-antibody test kit for that pathogen; combine the result with other evidence because yellowing can also result from mineral deficiency.

4 marks

25. Explain how vaccination can prevent illness when the same pathogen enters the body later.

Answer:
  • The vaccine introduces dead/inactive pathogen or its antigens.
  • White blood cells make specific antibodies against those antigens.
  • Memory cells remain and produce a faster, larger antibody response on later exposure.
  • The pathogen is destroyed before it can cause significant illness.

26. Explain how vaccination of poultry against Salmonella can reduce food poisoning in humans.

Answer:
  • Vaccination stimulates poultry to produce specific antibodies to Salmonella antigens.
  • Later infection triggers a rapid antibody response that helps destroy bacteria.
  • Fewer bacteria contaminate poultry products such as meat or eggs.
  • People swallow fewer bacteria, reducing infection and the toxins produced in their bodies.

27. A pregnancy test uses coloured antibodies that bind a hormone in urine. The hormone–antibody complexes are trapped at a test line. Another line acts as a control. Explain a positive result and why a strip with no control line is invalid.

Answer:
  • The hormone binds to antibodies with a complementary binding site.
  • The complexes are trapped at the test line, where the coloured antibodies build up, giving a visible line.
  • A control line shows the test has functioned, for example that liquid and antibodies have moved along the strip.
  • Without a control line, a missing test line could be caused by test failure, so it cannot be interpreted as a reliable negative result.

28. In a trial, 84 of 120 patients improve with a drug and 60 of 120 improve with a placebo. Calculate both improvement rates and the difference in percentage points. Give one reason these data alone do not show whether the drug should be used.

Answer:
  • Drug: 84 ÷ 120 × 100 = 70%.
  • Placebo: 60 ÷ 120 × 100 = 50%.
  • Difference = 20 percentage points.
  • We also need evidence about side effects, or whether the sample and allocation provide a fair, reliable comparison.

29. Explain how TMV and aphids can each reduce plant growth.

Answer:
  • TMV causes mosaic leaf discolouration, reducing photosynthesis.
  • Less glucose is available for respiration and building new cell material.
  • Aphids feed on the plant’s sugar-rich sap.
  • This removes resources needed for growth and respiration.

6 marks

30. Describe how a new drug is tested before it is used widely. Explain why controls are needed in clinical trials.

Answer:
  • Carry out preclinical tests on cells, tissues and live animals to investigate effects and potential harm.
  • Begin clinical trials with very low doses in healthy volunteers, monitoring safety and side effects.
  • Test on patients to assess effectiveness and find the optimum dose. Check toxicity, efficacy and dose throughout.
  • Randomly allocate people to comparable groups; use a placebo or appropriate comparison treatment to separate drug effects from expectations and natural recovery.
  • Make the trial double-blind so patients and assessors do not know allocations, reducing bias. Use a large enough sample and compare side effects as well as benefits.
  • Submit methods and results for peer review, so other scientists can scrutinise the evidence before publication.

31. Describe how monoclonal antibodies are produced and explain how they can deliver a toxic drug to cancer cells.

Answer:
  • Introduce the target antigen into a mouse to stimulate antibody-producing lymphocytes.
  • Collect lymphocytes and fuse them with tumour cells to form hybridomas, combining antibody production with repeated division.
  • Select a hybridoma making the required antibody and clone it.
  • Grow the clone and collect and purify its identical antibodies.
  • Attach a toxic drug to an antibody whose binding site is complementary to an antigen on the cancer cells.
  • The antibody binds to those cells and delivers the drug to them, reducing damage to other cells compared with a less selective delivery method; side effects can still occur.

32. A vaccination programme reduces infection rates, but uptake is low in some areas. Explain how vaccination protects individuals and why improving uptake may protect other people too.

Answer:
  • Vaccine antigens stimulate white blood cells to make specific antibodies.
  • Memory cells remain, allowing a faster and larger response to the same pathogen later.
  • Rapid destruction of the pathogen reduces the chance of significant illness.
  • When many people are immune, an infected person meets fewer susceptible hosts, so transmission chains are interrupted.
  • This helps protect people who cannot be vaccinated, although protection is not guaranteed.
  • Low-uptake areas contain more susceptible hosts, so outbreaks are more likely; improving access and giving reliable information about benefits and risks can increase protection.

33. Compare physical, chemical and mechanical plant defences. Give examples and explain how each helps.

Answer:
  • Cellulose cell walls form a physical barrier to invading microorganisms.
  • A tough waxy cuticle protects leaf surfaces; dead bark protects stems and shedding it can remove pathogens.
  • Antibacterial chemicals act against bacteria.
  • Poisons discourage herbivores from eating the plant.
  • Thorns and hairs make feeding more difficult or unpleasant; leaves that droop or curl when touched can also deter animals.
  • Mimicry can trick animals into avoiding the plant. These adaptations act in different ways, rather than providing an antibody-based immune response.
