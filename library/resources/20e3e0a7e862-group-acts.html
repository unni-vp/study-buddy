Story / Acts

The central idea

A wealthy family's celebration is interrupted by a visitor who reveals how their choices have harmed a vulnerable woman. The central question is whether people accept responsibility when their actions affect others.

Think of a chain of falling dominoes. One person's decision changes somebody else's situation, making the next harm easier. The play connects separate decisions into a shared moral problem.

The action takes place in Brumley in 1912, in the Birlings' dining room. Priestley wrote the play in 1945. This gap gives audiences knowledge that the characters lack, especially about war and the Titanic.

Act 1: celebration interrupted

What happens: Arthur and Sybil Birling celebrate Sheila's engagement to Gerald Croft with their son Eric. Arthur discusses business, social advancement and individual self-interest. Inspector Goole arrives and reports a young woman's death. Arthur admits dismissing Eva Smith after a strike for better pay. Sheila admits using her influence to have Eva dismissed from a shop. Gerald recognises the name Daisy Renton.

What it means: the family's comfort rests partly on unequal power. Arthur can remove somebody's income; Sheila can turn a personal grievance into unemployment. The Inspector redirects attention from the prosperous family to the person they have harmed.

Methods: the doorbell interrupts Arthur's confidence. The contrast between celebration and investigation creates tension. Knowledge of the Titanic's later sinking makes Arthur's certainty an example of dramatic irony: the audience knows more than he does.

Act 2: respectable appearances break down

What happens: Gerald admits an affair with Daisy. He provided shelter, but the relationship depended on his money and ended when he chose. Sheila questions the engagement and returns the ring. Sybil admits refusing help to a pregnant woman who approached her charity. She condemns the unknown father without realising he is Eric.

What it means: apparent kindness can coexist with unequal power. Gerald helped Daisy, yet had much more control over the arrangement. Sybil mistakes social status for moral worth and refuses to examine her own part.

Methods: the audience can work out Eric's involvement before Sybil does. Her condemnation creates dramatic irony and suspense. Sheila's growing understanding contrasts with her mother's resistance.

Act 3: confession and the final reversal

What happens: Eric admits a sexual encounter involving pressure, the pregnancy and taking money from his father's business to help Eva. Eva refused the money when she learned it was stolen. The Inspector connects everyone's conduct and leaves. Gerald questions whether Goole was a real police inspector. A telephone enquiry suggests no such death has yet been reported. Arthur, Sybil and Gerald are relieved; Sheila and Eric still recognise their wrongdoing. A final call reports a young woman's death and an inspector's arrival.

What it means: being caught and being wrong are different. Some characters relax when punishment appears unlikely. Sheila and Eric understand that their conduct still matters.

Methods: the cyclical ending returns the family to the prospect of an investigation. The sudden telephone call overturns their relief and leaves the audience with the moral question. The Inspector's identity remains uncertain; do not present one explanation as a proven fact.
