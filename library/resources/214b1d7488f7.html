Speaking listening and discussion questions & answers

1 mark

1. Which three successful outcomes can be awarded for the spoken language endorsement?

Answer:

Pass, Merit and Distinction.

2. Does the spoken language endorsement contribute to the written GCSE grade?

Answer:

No.

3. Who should agree your presentation topic in advance?

Answer:

Your teacher.

4. Besides presenting, what must you do during the assessment?

Answer:

Listen and respond appropriately to questions or feedback.

5. Are slides compulsory?

Answer:

No.

6. What is the central purpose of a talk arguing for a quiet lunchtime space?

Answer:

To persuade the audience to support a quiet-space proposal.

2 marks

7. Turn the broad topic “sport” into a focused presentation question and identify an audience.

Answer:

Question: should our school offer a non-competitive lunchtime sports club?

Audience: pupils and staff who would use or organise it.

8. Why should a personal example not automatically be described as proof about all pupils?

Answer:

It only shows that particular experience. Other pupils may have different needs, so a wider claim needs wider evidence.

9. Give two useful things to put on a cue card.

Answer:
  • A keyword naming the main point.
  • A brief example or transition to the next point.

10. Explain why speaking faster is often a poor solution to an overlong rehearsal.

Answer:

It gives the audience less time to follow the reasoning. Removing repetition or simplifying the structure preserves clarity better.

11. Does using Spoken Standard English mean removing your regional accent? Explain.

Answer:

No. The focus is intelligibility and language appropriate to the formal setting, rather than imitating a particular accent.

12. Why is a memorised list of rhetorical devices insufficient for a strong presentation?

Answer:

The devices must serve a clear purpose and developed ideas. Organisation, delivery and responses to questions also matter.

4 marks

13. Develop this claim into a reasoned point: “A quiet space gives pupils more choice.”

Answer:

A quiet space would give pupils a choice because a busy room does not suit everyone every day. Someone preparing for an afternoon presentation might want ten calm minutes. That does not mean other pupils must stop talking or enjoying lunch. The value is an additional option, so pupils can choose the environment that helps them on that day.

The answer links a claim to a reason, an illustrative example and its significance. It avoids presenting the example as evidence about every pupil.

14. Write an opening that introduces the quiet-space proposal without beginning with a list of statistics.

Answer:

Imagine trying to remember a presentation while chairs scrape and voices cross the room. Now imagine that the same noise is exactly what your friend enjoys about lunchtime. Neither response is wrong. I want our school to consider a quiet option alongside the busy spaces we already have.

The concrete contrast establishes different needs. The proposal follows directly from the situation, giving the audience a clear direction.

15. Respond to this objection: “There is no spare room and staff are already busy.”

Answer:

That is a serious practical limit. I would not ask the school to promise a room without checking its use and supervision. My first step would be to discuss whether a short trial could fit an existing arrangement. If there is no workable space or staffing, we would need to change or postpone the proposal rather than ignore the problem.

The response acknowledges the constraint, proposes a next step and accepts a limit. It develops the discussion rather than dismissing the questioner.

16. Respond to this question: “Would a quiet space make pupils more isolated?”

Answer:

It could if it became a place people were sent to or a room for a labelled group. I would make it an open choice that pupils could use briefly and leave. During a trial, I would ask whether it helped people feel included or created separation. If it made that problem worse, the arrangement would need to change.

The answer recognises a risk, offers a reasoned safeguard and explains how the proposal could be reviewed.

17. You are asked, “What would supervision cost?” You have not researched a figure. Give an appropriate response.

Answer:

I do not have a staffing cost, so I cannot give a reliable figure. Before taking the proposal further, I would ask the school what supervision would be needed and whether any existing arrangement could support a trial. Cost would have to be part of deciding whether it was practical.

The answer states the limit honestly, identifies the missing evidence and explains how it affects the proposal.

18. A listener says, “Your example was interesting, but I did not understand its relevance.” Explain how you would revise the talk.

Answer:

I would keep the example only if it supported the purpose. After describing a pupil preparing for a presentation, I would add: “This matters because a short calm break can be one useful option, without changing everyone else’s lunchtime.” I would then connect that need to the proposed trial.

The revision supplies the missing explanation instead of adding another anecdote or more decorative language.

19. Write a conclusion that returns to the main purpose and suggests a realistic next step.

Answer:

I am not asking for every lunchtime to be silent. I am asking us to find out whether one calm option could help. Let us discuss a small trial, check what it would need and listen to pupils afterwards. We do not need one perfect lunchtime. We need room for different ones.

The conclusion restates the distinction, proposes a manageable action and closes with a memorable contrast. It does not introduce a new argument.

20. Explain how pace, pauses and emphasis could improve this line: “A choice is useful because it can change from day to day.”

Answer:

I would speak the first clause steadily, pause briefly after “useful”, then give some emphasis to “change”. A short pause after the sentence would let the audience connect it to the example. I would keep the volume audible and natural rather than stressing every word.

The delivery highlights the main reasoning without making the sentence theatrical or difficult to follow.

6 marks

21. Plan a presentation supporting a quiet lunchtime space. Include a purpose, development, a serious objection, a conclusion and preparation for questions.

Answer:
  • Purpose and audience: persuade pupils and staff to consider a practical quiet-space trial.
  • Opening: contrast two pupils who want different kinds of lunchtime.
  • Development: explain choice, then show through an example how a calmer break could support participation.
  • Objection and response: acknowledge room and staffing limits. Propose checking feasibility before a limited trial.
  • Conclusion: return to choice and ask for an agreed next step.
  • Question preparation: consider isolation, fair access, supervision and how success would be judged. Prepare reasons and acknowledge evidence still needed.

The sequence develops a proposal through explanation and qualification. The preparation addresses likely concerns rather than rehearsing unrelated answers.

22. Give a complete model speech proposing a quiet lunchtime space, using clear development and a practical response to objections.

Answer:

Picture the last five minutes of lunch. A chair scrapes behind you. Someone calls across the room. You are trying to remember the opening of a presentation you have to give next lesson, but every time you find the sentence, another sound pushes it away.

For someone else, that same room is the best part of the day: friends, conversation and a welcome break from sitting quietly. Neither person is doing lunchtime wrong. They need different things from it.

I want our school to try a quiet lunchtime space. Not a silent school. Not a rule that everybody must read. Just a place people can choose when they need a calmer break.

The first reason is simple: choice makes a shared environment fairer. We already accept that people enjoy different clubs and different sports. Yet we sometimes expect one kind of social space to suit everyone. A pupil preparing for a presentation might want a few calm minutes. Another might want to draw, read or sit without having to explain why. Those are possible uses, not claims that everybody has the same need.

The second reason is that a quiet option could support participation elsewhere. A break from noise does not have to mean a break from friendship. Someone could spend ten minutes in the room and then return to friends outside. A choice is useful precisely because it can change from day to day.

There is an important objection. A room is not just an empty box waiting for a good idea. It may already be needed for teaching or clubs. Supervision takes time, and the school cannot promise a space without considering those practical demands. If we ignore that, we have a slogan rather than a proposal.

So I suggest a small trial, agreed with the school. It might use an available room on one lunchtime each week, if suitable supervision can be arranged. Before it begins, pupils and staff should agree simple expectations: keep the space calm, respect anyone using it, and leave it ready for the next group. It should be open to any pupil who wants that kind of break, rather than presented as a room for a labelled group.

The trial would also need a way to learn. We could ask whether pupils used it, whether they felt welcome and whether it caused problems for other activities. We should listen to people who chose not to use it as well. If the arrangement created exclusion or was not practical to supervise, that would be a reason to change it, not to pretend the difficulty did not exist.

I cannot promise that one room will solve every lunchtime problem. I can suggest that a modest, reversible trial is better than assuming the current choice suits everybody.

At the end of the trial, the important question would not be whether the whole school had become quieter. It would be whether more pupils had a break that worked for them. We do not need one perfect lunchtime. We need room for different ones.

Purpose and organisation: the opening establishes different needs. The reasons lead to a practical objection, a limited trial and a return to the central idea of choice.

Development: examples make the proposal concrete without inventing statistics. The speech recognises uncertainty and suggests how a trial could test the idea.

Spoken style: direct questions, clear transitions and the final contrast help listeners follow. Delivery would still need rehearsal, and the assessment also includes responding to questions.

23. A listener challenges the proposal: “You say everyone can use the room, but what if there are too many people? And how would you know the trial worked?” Give a developed response.

Answer:

Those are connected problems, because a room that is always full would not provide a useful choice. Before a trial, we would need to agree its safe capacity and a fair way of handling demand. I would avoid promising unrestricted access if that could not be delivered.

To judge the trial, I would want more than the number of visitors. I would ask whether pupils felt welcome, whether the space stayed calm and whether supervision or other activities were disrupted. People unable to get a place should also be heard; otherwise the feedback would miss a major problem.

If demand was too high, the next step might be to change the arrangement, but that would depend on available rooms and staff. Success would mean a practical and fair additional option, not simply a popular room.

The response answers both parts, uses a clear definition of success and develops the proposal while respecting practical limits.
