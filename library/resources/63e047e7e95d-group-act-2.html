Act 2: murder & disorder

Act 2 — Murder and disorder

What happens

  • Macbeth sees a dagger leading towards Duncan's room. A bell signals that the preparations are ready; he goes to kill Duncan offstage.
  • He returns carrying the bloody daggers. He is distressed by imagined voices and cannot return to the room. Lady Macbeth takes the daggers back and smears the guards with blood.
  • Knocking interrupts them. The Porter delays opening the gate; Macduff then discovers Duncan's body. Macbeth kills the guards, claiming that anger overwhelmed him.
  • Malcolm and Donalbain flee because they fear for their lives. Their flight makes them appear suspicious. Unnatural events mirror the disorder, and Macbeth goes to Scone to be crowned.

Why this act matters

The crime brings immediate psychological punishment. Macbeth gains a route to power but loses peace of mind. Blood is both visible evidence and the beginning of a lasting guilt motif. Lady Macbeth treats washing as a practical solution; he fears a stain that water cannot remove.

Keeping Duncan's murder offstage concentrates attention on anticipation and aftermath. Knocking makes discovery feel close. The Porter's imagined role as a gatekeeper of hell mixes comedy with a disturbing suggestion that the castle has become a place of evil. Darkness and unnatural behaviour in the animal world echo the broken political order.

Important quotations

QuotationWho and whereWhat to analyse
“Is this a dagger which I see before me”Macbeth, 2.1Language / form — question within a soliloquy: The question makes his senses uncertain. The vision can suggest a mind already consumed by the murder; it does not remove his choice.
“Macbeth does murder sleep”Macbeth reporting a voice, 2.2Imagery — personification of sleep: Sleep represents innocence and rest. The image suggests that killing Duncan also destroys Macbeth's own peace.
“all great Neptune's ocean”Macbeth, 2.2Imagery / language — classical allusion and hyperbole: The enormous scale is hyperbole: even an ocean seems unable to remove his guilt.
“A little water clears us of this deed”Lady Macbeth, 2.2Language / structure — minimising adjective and later reversal: The minimising adjective “Little” downplays the crime. Her practical confidence contrasts with Macbeth's distress and prepares the later reversal in her sleepwalking.
“Wake Duncan with thy knocking. I would thou couldst.”Macbeth, 2.2Language — imperative followed by an impossible wish: The impossible wish reveals regret. The knocking cannot reverse the irreversible act.
“My bosom franchised and allegiance clear”Banquo, 2.1Imagery / language — freedom metaphor and vocabulary of loyalty: He wants conscience free and loyalty untainted. Advancement should not require moral compromise.
“My father as he slept”Lady Macbeth, 2.2Language / structure — personal kinship reference revealing a limit to cruelty: Duncan's resemblance to her father stops her killing him herself. The memory reveals a limit to her performed ruthlessness.
“O horror, horror, horror!”Macduff, 2.3Language — repetition and exclamation: Repetition and exclamation convey genuine shock, contrasting with Macbeth's attempt to hide responsibility.

Turning point: Duncan's death makes Macbeth's ambition real, but kingship begins in fear and concealment.
