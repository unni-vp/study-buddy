Hormones and glucose

Endocrine glands and hormone messages

An endocrine gland secretes hormones directly into the blood. Blood carries them around the body; target cells with suitable receptors respond.

Compared with nervous responses, hormonal effects are generally slower and longer lasting. Think of nervous impulses as direct urgent messages and hormones as messages circulated to recipients able to read them. This is a comparison, not an absolute rule for every hormone.

Gland and positionExamples of hormones or roles
Pituitary, base of brainSeveral coordinating hormones, including FSH, LH and ADH release
Thyroid, neckThyroxine
Adrenal glands, above kidneysAdrenaline
Pancreas, abdomenInsulin and glucagon
Ovaries, pelvisOestrogen and progesterone
Testes, scrotumTestosterone

The pituitary is called a master gland because some of its hormones stimulate other endocrine glands. Not all hormones come from the pituitary, and not all its hormones work through another gland.

Comparing the two communication systems

FeatureNervous communicationHormonal communication
Signal and routeElectrical impulses travel along neurones; chemical transmitters cross synapses.Hormones travel in blood after release from endocrine glands.
TargetA connected muscle, gland or other nerve cell receives the signal.Cells respond only if they have suitable receptors, even though blood carries the hormone widely.
Usual timingResponses are rapid and often brief.Responses generally develop more slowly and last longer.
ExampleA withdrawal reflex rapidly contracts a muscle.Insulin changes glucose uptake and storage after a meal.

A hormone does not act on every cell merely because blood reaches it. Receptor specificity explains why a widely circulated message produces particular effects.
