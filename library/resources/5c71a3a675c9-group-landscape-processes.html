Landscapes & processes

The UK landscape

The broad pattern

Think of Britain as a table with a higher, rougher end. The north and west are generally higher, steeper and wetter. The south and east are generally lower, flatter and drier. Local exceptions matter: a lowland region can contain a resistant ridge, and an upland region can contain a broad valley.

Relief describes the height and shape of the land. Geology describes its rocks and their structure. Landscapes develop through the interaction of geology, climate, geomorphic processes and human activity.

LandscapeDistribution and exampleDistinctive characteristics and uses
UplandCommon in Scotland, Wales and northern England; examples include the Highlands, Eryri/Snowdonia, the Lake District and the Pennines. Dartmoor is an upland exception in southern England.High relief, steep slopes and exposed rock are common. Cooler, wetter conditions and thinner soils often favour grazing, forestry and recreation rather than extensive arable farming.
LowlandWidespread in southern and eastern England, including the Fens and the London Basin.Gentler slopes and deeper soils often favour arable farming, settlement and transport. Drainage has made some very low-lying land available for farming, but it can remain exposed to flooding.
GlaciatedIce modified much of Scotland, Wales and northern England, including the Cairngorms and Lake District.Ice widened and deepened valleys and left lakes and deposits. Steep-sided valleys and exposed scenery support tourism, while relief can restrict roads and farming.

Glaciated describes how an area was shaped, rather than a separate height category. An upland landscape can also be glaciated.

Rock type and resistance

Rock groupHow it formsExample and landscape connection
IgneousMolten rock cools and solidifies. Crystals can interlock tightly.Resistant granite helps explain Dartmoor's upland relief.
SedimentarySediments accumulate and become compacted and cemented; some rocks form from organic remains.Clay can be eroded readily, while limestone and sandstone vary in resistance. Limestone contributes to the steep lower Wye valley.
MetamorphicHeat and pressure change an existing rock without melting it.Rocks such as slate occur in upland areas. Their mineral arrangement can influence how they split.

Rock groups are useful background, but not every sedimentary rock is soft. Resistance also depends on cementation, cracks and layers. Water can enter cracks, and weak layers can provide surfaces along which material slips. These details explain a particular landscape more accurately than its rock-group name alone.

People change the physical system

Removing woodland can reduce interception and expose soil to runoff. Quarrying removes rock and changes slopes. Buildings and roads replace vegetation with hard surfaces, allowing rainfall to reach drains and rivers more quickly. Management then changes the system again: planting trees, restoring habitats or building defences can alter runoff, sediment supply and erosion.

Processes that shape landscapes

Weathering breaks rock down in place

Weathering happens where the rock is located. Material may move later through erosion or mass movement, but movement is not part of the definition of weathering.

TypeHow it worksWhat conditions matter?
Mechanical: freeze–thawWater enters a crack. It freezes and expands, putting pressure on the sides. Repeated freezing and thawing widens the crack until pieces break away.Water must be available, and temperature must cross the freezing point repeatedly. Continuous freezing alone does not provide repeated cycles.
ChemicalA reaction changes minerals in the rock. Slightly acidic water can dissolve calcium carbonate in limestone.Water, suitable rock and temperature affect the rate. Natural rainwater can be weakly acidic without industrial pollution.
BiologicalGrowing roots push into cracks and widen them; burrowing organisms can also disturb material.Plants or other organisms must be present. Their activity can prepare material for later removal.

Gravity causes mass movement

Mass movement moves rock or soil downslope under gravity. Rain can add weight and reduce stability; wave or river undercutting can remove support at the foot of a slope.

  • Sliding: a block moves along a relatively flat failure surface, such as a weak layer.
  • Slumping: a mass rotates along a curved failure surface, often leaving a stepped or tilted profile. Saturated weak material is especially vulnerable.

These processes can work together. A cliff is weakened by weathering, undercut by erosion and then collapses or slumps. Water removes the fallen material, exposing the cliff foot to further attack.

Moving water erodes and transports

ProcessWhat happens?Important distinction
Hydraulic actionThe force of moving water loosens material; pressure in cracks helps break it away.Water supplies the force.
AbrasionCarried stones scrape or strike the bed, bank or cliff.The load acts like sandpaper against a surface.
AttritionTransported particles collide, chip and become smaller and rounder.The particles change; this does not directly scrape away the channel itself.
SolutionSoluble minerals dissolve in water.Dissolved material can then be transported invisibly.
Transport processHow material moves
TractionLarge particles roll along the bed.
SaltationSmaller stones bounce along the bed.
SuspensionFine particles are carried within the flowing water.
SolutionDissolved minerals travel in the water.

The method depends on particle size and the strength of the flow. A particle that moves during a flood may be left on the bed when discharge falls.

Deposition lays material down

Deposition occurs when flowing water can no longer transport part of its load. This often happens where flow slows, spreads out or becomes sheltered. Coarser particles usually settle before fine sediment as flow weakens.

Erosion, transport and deposition can occur in different parts of the same river bend. They are not three jobs allocated exclusively to the upper, middle and lower courses.
