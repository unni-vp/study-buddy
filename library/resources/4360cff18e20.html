Practice passage: Morning platform

1. The platform was empty except for a porter pushing a trolley. Above the locked ticket office, the clock showed ten minutes to seven. Rain shone on the benches. At the far end, a lamp blinked, steadied itself and blinked again. Ada counted the benches twice, as though another count might make the train late.

2. Her suitcase stood against her ankle. Under one arm she held a portfolio tied with string; in her coat pocket was an envelope addressed to her father. She had written the letter three times before settling on the shortest version. Now its folded corner scratched her gloveless hand whenever she touched it.

3. The train was due at five to seven. By breakfast, she would be on her way to the art college. For months she had saved the money from her evening work, adding small coins to a tin under her bed. Her father had called the college a pleasant idea for somebody who could afford pleasant ideas. She had stopped mentioning it.

4. A gust lifted the edge of the station newspaper. Ada stepped on it before it could blow against her case, then apologised to nobody. The porter looked up. She straightened her back and pretended to study the timetable, although she knew the little black figures by heart.

5. At home, her father would be lighting the kitchen fire. He would set out two cups and discover that her room was empty. She imagined his hand resting on the second cup. For a moment she moved towards the exit. Then she gripped the string around the portfolio until it pressed a red line into her palm.

6. “Five minutes,” said the porter. Ada nodded as if this were useful news. The clock swallowed another minute. She took the envelope from her pocket and asked whether he would deliver it to the house after the train had gone. He glanced at the address but asked no questions.

7. A whistle sounded beyond the bend. The rails began to hum. In the distance a white light appeared, small at first, then steadily larger, until Ada could no longer pretend it was another lamp. She lifted her case. The handle slipped in her damp fingers.

8. Behind her, footsteps struck the stone steps: hard, quick beats, getting closer. She knew that uneven rhythm. She had heard it crossing the kitchen, climbing the stairs, coming home from work. There was nowhere on the platform to hide.

9. She turned.

10. Her father stood beneath the clock, his coat unbuttoned and his breath showing. He was holding a small brown parcel. Ada waited for him to reach for the suitcase. Instead, he held out the parcel. “You forgot these,” he said.

11. Inside were the woollen gloves her mother had made. One thumb had been mended with darker thread. Her father looked at the portfolio, then at her hands. “No use trying to draw with fingers like that.” He said it roughly, as if tenderness were something he had found unexpectedly and did not know where to put.

12. The train arrived in a rush of steam. For a few seconds she could see only his outline. When it cleared, his hand was still held out between them. She took the gloves. “I wasn't going to disappear,” she said, though the envelope in the porter's hand made this sound less certain than she wanted.

13. He lifted the suitcase onto the carriage step. “Write when you get there.” It was not quite an apology, and it did not settle all the arguments waiting behind them. But he had moved the case towards the train, not away from it.

14. From the window, Ada watched the porter pass back the unopened envelope. Her father folded it into his pocket. As the carriage began to move, he raised one hand. This time she did not mistake the movement for an instruction to stop.

15. She put on the gloves. The dark stitch lay across her thumb like a small, crooked bridge. On the first clean page of her sketchbook, she drew a hand raised above a platform. Under it she wrote, Dear Dad. The first line came easily.

1 mark

1. What time does the clock show in paragraph 1?

Answer:

6:50.

2. Who is the only other person on the platform at the start?

Answer:

The porter.

3. Where is Ada planning to go?

Answer:

Art college.

4. What does the parcel contain?

Answer:

Woollen gloves.

5. What is an inference?

Answer:

An interpretation suggested by evidence rather than stated directly.

6. Which narrative viewpoint is used?

Answer:

Third person, closely following Ada.

7. Name the method in “The clock swallowed another minute”.

Answer:

Personification.

8. Name the method in “like a small, crooked bridge”.

Answer:

Simile.

2 marks

9. Give two details from paragraph 1 that make the station seem quiet or unwelcoming.

Answer:
  • It is empty except for the porter.
  • The ticket office is locked.

The wet benches or flickering lamp would also be relevant.

10. Give two details showing Ada has prepared for the journey.

Answer:
  • She has a packed suitcase and portfolio.
  • She has saved money for months.

Her repeated letter-writing also shows preparation.

11. What do her repeated counts of the benches suggest?

Answer:

She seems anxious and wants to delay the moment of departure. Repeating an unnecessary action gives her something to do while she waits.

12. Why is saying “her father hates her” an insecure inference?

Answer:

The passage shows conflict and Ada's fear, but his arrival with the gloves and help with the suitcase complicate that view. Disagreement does not prove hatred.

13. Explain the difference between analysing and retelling.

Answer:

Retelling describes what happens. Analysis explains how a particular choice of wording or arrangement shapes meaning and the reader's response.

14. Why is “She turned” important in its position?

Answer:

The isolated sentence slows attention onto one decisive movement, delaying the revelation of who has arrived and what he will do.

15. Explain why the father's rough voice does not necessarily mean he is uncaring.

Answer:

The dialogue is paired with a caring action: bringing the gloves. The narration suggests he struggles to express tenderness directly.

16. What should a response to an evaluation statement include?

Answer:

A clear judgement, precise textual evidence and explanation of how that evidence supports or qualifies the judgement.

5 marks

17. List five facts about the station or platform in paragraph 1.

Answer:
  • Only Ada and the porter are present.
  • The porter pushes a trolley.
  • The ticket office is locked.
  • The clock shows 6:50.
  • The benches are wet with rain.

The flickering lamp is another acceptable fact. Choose five distinct details.

18. How does the writer show Ada's anxiety in paragraphs 2–4? Refer to the language and details.

Answer:

Ada repeatedly touches the letter, and its corner “scratched” her hand, making her worry physically uncomfortable. Writing it “three times” shows her difficulty explaining the departure. The contrast between knowing the timetable “by heart” and pretending to study it suggests that she wants to conceal her nerves. Even apologising “to nobody” makes a small, unnecessary action reveal her unsettled state.

The answer selects several details from the required range and explains what each reveals, rather than only stating that she is anxious.

19. What impressions do you get of Ada's determination in paragraphs 3–5?

Answer:

Ada has saved small amounts of money “for months”, showing patience rather than a sudden whim. She has continued planning despite her father's dismissive comments about “pleasant ideas”. When she briefly moves towards the exit, gripping the portfolio until it marks her palm shows the effort needed to hold on to her decision. Her determination therefore includes doubt: she chooses to continue even while imagining her father's hurt.

Each impression is tied to an action or quotation. The qualification distinguishes determination from having no worries.

20. Explain how the gloves gain significance between paragraphs 10 and 15.

Answer:

At first the parcel overturns Ada's expectation that her father will stop her. The gloves are a practical act of care, while the darker repair recalls her mother and the family she is leaving. At the end, the stitch becomes “a small, crooked bridge”, suggesting an imperfect but surviving connection. Putting the gloves on makes that support something Ada takes with her, rather than something she must reject to leave.

The answer follows the object's changing meaning across the stated range and explains the image, instead of simply identifying repetition.

10 marks

21. How does the writer make paragraphs 6–10 tense and dramatic? Explain what happens, the language and its effects.

Answer:

The approaching departure creates a deadline. Personifying the clock as having “swallowed another minute” makes time seem to consume Ada's remaining freedom to change her mind. Her decision to leave a letter with the porter also shows that she expects an emotional confrontation and wants to avoid it.

The train is introduced gradually through a whistle, humming rails and a light growing larger. These sensory details bring the event closer in stages. The slipping handle makes Ada's nervousness physical, suggesting that even the simple act of holding the suitcase is becoming difficult.

The footsteps interrupt this approach. Their “hard, quick beats” sound forceful, while Ada's recognition of the rhythm connects the approaching person with home and authority. Because the narration stays with her expectations, the reader shares her fear before knowing the father's purpose. “Nowhere ... to hide” presents the open platform as a trap.

The separate sentence “She turned” concentrates attention on the confrontation and delays the answer. Yet the father holds out a parcel instead of taking the case. This reversal releases some of the tension while creating a new question about his intentions.

The model follows the sequence, connects precise wording to uncertainty and explains the turning point. It does not treat tension as a label that automatically applies to every short sentence.

22. What impressions do you get of Ada's father in paragraphs 10–15? Support your answer with the text.

Answer:

He first appears urgent and physically unsettled: his coat is unbuttoned and his breath is visible, suggesting that he has hurried rather than calmly arranged a confrontation. Ada expects him to seize the suitcase, but he offers a parcel. The difference between her expectation and his action begins to make him seem more supportive than she imagined.

Bringing the gloves is a practical form of care. He notices her cold hands and the needs of her drawing, so he is paying attention to the future she wants. His rough remark does not cancel that care: the narration presents tenderness as something he does not know how to express comfortably.

He is not transformed into an entirely different person. The comment that this is “not quite an apology” leaves conflict unresolved. Nevertheless, carrying the case towards the train makes his action more significant than a speech promising support: he helps the departure happen.

His raised hand is finally understood as a farewell rather than an order. The shift in Ada's interpretation allows the reader to see a father learning to let go, while the image of the repaired glove suggests a relationship that can continue imperfectly.

The answer develops a changing impression from several points in the range and qualifies it using the unresolved disagreement.

23. “The writer presents Ada as selfish and impulsive.” To what extent do you agree? Consider the whole passage and support your judgement.

Answer:

I agree that Ada's secret departure could hurt her father, but selfish and impulsive oversimplify the way the writer presents her. The unopened letter and her plan to have it delivered only after leaving show that she avoids a difficult conversation. Imagining him finding the second breakfast cup also makes the possible hurt visible.

However, she has saved money “for months”, so the decision is prepared rather than a sudden impulse. Holding on to the portfolio when she almost turns back connects her determination to a serious ambition. The close viewpoint lets the reader experience her guilt as well as her desire to leave, encouraging sympathy without making the choice entirely comfortable.

Her response to the gloves further challenges the idea that she is uncaring. She accepts the family object and begins a new letter, “Dear Dad”, rather than treating departure as a complete rejection of home. The father's practical support also suggests that independence and family connection need not exclude one another.

Overall, the passage presents a determined young person making an imperfect choice under emotional pressure. The “crooked bridge” acknowledges difficulty while leaving the relationship open. It would be too simple either to condemn her without considering the preparation and guilt or to claim her departure causes no pain.

The judgement is qualified, supported from across the text and linked to viewpoint and imagery. It evaluates the given interpretation rather than writing a general character description.
