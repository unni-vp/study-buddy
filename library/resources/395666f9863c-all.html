Geographical skills

Cartographic skills

Grid references

Read along the corridor, then up the stairs.

  • A four-figure grid reference identifies a grid square: easting first, then northing.
  • A six-figure grid reference identifies a point more precisely by dividing the square into tenths.
  • Always read eastings from left to right and northings from bottom to top.

Scale and distance

Map scale compares map distance with real distance.

  • At 1:50,000, 1 cm on the map represents 50,000 cm = 500 m = 0.5 km.
  • At 1:25,000, 1 cm represents 250 m = 0.25 km.
  • For a winding route, follow it with string or the edge of paper, then compare the measured length with the scale.

Worked example: A straight line is 6 cm on a 1:50,000 map. Real distance = 6 × 0.5 km = 3 km.

Relief and gradient

  • Contour lines join points of equal height.
  • Close contours show a steep slope; wide spacing shows a gentle slope.
  • A spot height gives the exact height of one point.
  • A cross-section shows the side profile of land along a line.

Gradient = vertical change ÷ horizontal distance

Keep both measurements in the same units. A rise of 100 m over 2 km is 100 ÷ 2000 = 1/20, written 1:20.

GIS

A Geographical Information System stores, layers and analyses location-based data.

  • Layers might include roads, flood risk, population and land use.
  • Overlaying layers helps reveal patterns and relationships.
  • GIS is powerful, but results depend on the accuracy, date and scale of the source data.

Read the labels before explaining a graph

Start with what is measured, where, when and in which units. A chart of UK electricity generation cannot describe all energy consumption. A historical disaster map cannot automatically describe the present day. A population pyramid shows age and sex, but its shape alone does not establish why people migrated or births changed.

Percentage points and percentage change are different. If one generation share is 50.4% and another is 31.8%, the difference is 18.6 percentage points. Percentage change instead divides the change by a starting value and multiplies by 100.

Scatter graphs

  • Put the possible cause on the x-axis and the possible effect on the y-axis.
  • A positive correlation rises from left to right.
  • A negative correlation falls from left to right.
  • No correlation means no clear relationship.
  • A line of best fit summarises the trend; an anomaly lies away from it.
  • Correlation suggests a relationship but does not prove causation.

Building a clear presentation

Use a suitable title, labelled axes, units, even scale and an accurate key. Choose intervals that use the available space without distorting the pattern.

To analyse, use pattern + evidence + exception:

  1. State the overall trend.
  2. Support it with values and locations.
  3. Identify and suggest a reason for an anomaly.

Numerical and statistical skills

Averages and spread

  • Mean: total of all values ÷ number of values.
  • Median: middle value after ordering the data.
  • Mode: most frequent value.
  • Modal class: group containing the greatest frequency.
  • Range: highest − lowest.
  • Interquartile range: upper quartile − lower quartile; it measures the spread of the middle 50%.

Worked example: 2, 3, 3, 6, 11

  • Mean = 25 ÷ 5 = 5
  • Median = 3
  • Mode = 3
  • Range = 11 − 2 = 9

The mean is raised by the extreme value 11, so the median may better represent a typical value.

Percentages

Percentage = part ÷ whole × 100

Percentage change = change ÷ original value × 100

Example: traffic rises from 80 to 100 vehicles. Change = 20. Percentage increase = 20 ÷ 80 × 100 = 25%.

Interpolation and extrapolation

  • Interpolation estimates within the known data range; it is usually safer.
  • Extrapolation estimates beyond the known range; it is less reliable because the trend may change.

Enquiry and data quality

Sampling

  • Random: every item has an equal chance; reduces selection bias.
  • Systematic: data is collected at regular intervals; easy to repeat and gives coverage.
  • Stratified: sample proportions match important groups in the population.
  • Opportunity: use whoever or whatever is available; quick but more biased.

Reliability, accuracy and validity

  • Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the true value.
  • Reliability: whether repeating the method gives similar results.
  • Validity: whether the method actually answers the enquiry question.

Improve data by using clear instructions, calibrated equipment, repeated readings, a larger representative sample and consistent conditions where possible.

Sources and bias

For every source, ask:

  • Who produced it and why?
  • When was it collected?
  • Is the sample or area representative?
  • Does the scale hide local differences?
  • Has the presentation selected or omitted information?

Reaching a conclusion

A strong conclusion:

  • answers the enquiry directly;
  • supports each claim with processed evidence;
  • recognises anomalies and limits;
  • avoids claiming that correlation proves cause;
  • suggests realistic improvements and explains how they would help.

High-yield checklist

  • Show every calculation and include units.
  • Choose a graph or map because it suits the data.
  • Quote evidence when describing a pattern.
  • Separate description (what) from explanation (why).
  • Evaluate the method, data and conclusion rather than saying only “get more data”.
