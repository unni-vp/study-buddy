Motion and Newton’s laws

Describing motion

Average speed = total distance ÷ total time

At constant speed:

s = vt

  • s: distance, in m.
  • v: speed, in m/s.
  • t: time, in s.

Average velocity = displacement ÷ time

Velocity includes direction.

A runner travels 200 m in 40 s: average speed = 5.0 m/s. If the runner returns to the starting point, average velocity for the full journey is zero even though average speed is not.

Useful approximate speeds

Walking1.5 m/s

These are estimates, not fixed properties. Terrain, fitness, traffic and conditions matter.

a = (v − u)/t

  • a: acceleration, in m/s².
  • u: initial velocity, in m/s.
  • v: final velocity, in m/s.
  • t: time, in s.

Example: a car increases its velocity from 5 to 17 m/s in 4 s.

a = (17 − 5) ÷ 4 = 3 m/s²

Slowing gives acceleration opposite to the motion.

For constant acceleration:

v² − u² = 2as

Example: an object starts from rest and accelerates at 2.0 m/s² over 25 m.

v = √(2 × 2.0 × 25) = 10 m/s

This equation is not for changing acceleration.

Moving in a circle at constant speed still changes velocity, because direction changes. A resultant force towards the centre is needed.

Average speed is not usually the average of two speeds

A cyclist travels 60 m at 3 m/s, then 60 m at 6 m/s.

  1. First time = 60 ÷ 3 = 20 s.
  2. Second time = 60 ÷ 6 = 10 s.
  3. Total distance = 120 m; total time = 30 s.
  4. Average speed = 120 ÷ 30 = 4 m/s, rather than 4.5 m/s.

The slower stage takes longer. A simple average of speeds works only in appropriate circumstances, such as equal time spent at each speed.

Negative acceleration can mean speeding up

Choose right as positive. A trolley changes from −2 m/s to −6 m/s in 2 s.

a = (−6 − (−2)) ÷ 2 = −2 m/s²

It is moving left and becoming faster. Both velocity and acceleration point left. Negative acceleration therefore does not always mean slowing down. An object slows when acceleration points opposite to its velocity.

A displacement–time line can slope down as an object moves back towards the chosen origin. A graph of total distance travelled cannot decrease. Check exactly what “distance” labels in a question describe.

Newton's laws and terminal velocity

First law: if the resultant force is zero, a stationary object stays at rest and a moving object keeps a constant velocity. A car travelling steadily has a driving force that balances resistance. It does not need a forward resultant force to keep moving. Inertia is the tendency to stay at rest or keep the same velocity.

Newton's second law

F = ma

  • F: the resultant force, in N.
  • m: mass, in kg.
  • a: acceleration, in m/s².

For the same mass, a greater resultant force produces greater acceleration. For the same force, a greater mass produces less acceleration.

Inertial mass = F/a

This measures how difficult it is to change velocity.

Third law: when two objects interact, they exert equal and opposite forces of the same type on each other. The forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel on one free-body diagram.

For example, a book's weight and the table's upward force both act on the book. They are therefore not a third-law pair. The partner to Earth's gravitational pull on the book is the book's gravitational pull on Earth.

Falling through air

  1. Initially, weight exceeds drag, so the object accelerates downwards.
  2. Increasing speed increases drag. Resultant force and acceleration decrease.
  3. When drag = weight, resultant force is zero: the object falls at terminal velocity.
  4. A parachute increases drag; immediately after opening, upward drag may exceed weight, so it slows. A new, lower terminal velocity is reached when forces balance again.

Free fall with negligible air resistance gives acceleration about 9.8 m/s² near Earth. At terminal velocity there are still forces; they balance.

Practical: force, mass and acceleration

Use a low-friction trolley pulled by a string over a pulley by hanging masses. Light gates or a motion sensor measure velocities and acceleration. Keep the string taut and measure before the hanging mass hits the floor.

Vary force at constant mass

  • Transfer small masses from the trolley to the hanger. Total moving mass of trolley, hanger and added masses stays constant, while the driving weight increases.
  • For the whole moving system, driving force is approximately the hanging weight when friction is negligible. The string tension on the trolley alone is not equal to the hanging weight while it accelerates.
  • Measure acceleration for each driving force. Repeat and plot a against F; expect approximate direct proportionality.

Vary mass at constant force

  • Keep hanging mass fixed and add mass to the trolley. Total moving mass increases while the driving weight remains the same.
  • Measure acceleration and repeat. A graph of a against 1/total mass should be approximately straight if force is constant.
  • Measure and allow for friction; keep track and pulley conditions consistent. Secure the pulley, catch the trolley safely and keep clear of falling masses.

Use Δv/Δt for acceleration when measured velocities and their time interval are available. A single speed measurement is not an acceleration.
