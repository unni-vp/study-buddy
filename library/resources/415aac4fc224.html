Atomic structure questions & answers

1 mark2 marks3 marks4 marks6 marks

1 mark

1. Which particle has no electric charge and is found in the nucleus?

Answer:

Neutron.

2. What is the atomic number?

Answer:

The number of protons in a nucleus.

3. What is the SI unit of activity?

Answer:

Becquerel (Bq).

4. What is an alpha particle?

Answer:

A helium nucleus: two protons and two neutrons.

2 marks

5. Explain what is meant by isotopes of an element.

Answer:

Atoms with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

6. An atom has atomic number 11 and mass number 23. State its neutron number and the number of electrons in its singly positive ion.

Answer:
  • Neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12.
  • The ion has lost one electron: 11 − 1 = 10 electrons.

7. Describe what happens when an electron absorbs radiation and then returns to its original energy level.

Answer:
  • It moves to a higher energy level after absorption.
  • It emits electromagnetic radiation when returning to a lower level.

8. State how the mass number and atomic number change during beta-minus decay.

Answer:
  • Mass number does not change.
  • Atomic number increases by 1 because a neutron becomes a proton.

9. How does irradiation differ from contamination?

Answer:
  • Irradiation is exposure to radiation from a source.
  • Contamination is radioactive material being present where it is unwanted.

10. Give one natural source and one human-made source of background radiation.

Answer:
  • Natural: rocks/radon gas.
  • Human-made: fallout from nuclear weapons tests.

11. Why is radioactive decay described as random even though a sample has a predictable half-life?

Answer:
  • The decay time of an individual nucleus cannot be predicted.
  • Large numbers follow a predictable overall halving pattern.

3 marks

12. An isotope has activity 960 Bq and half-life 4 hours. Calculate its activity after 12 hours.

Answer:
  • 12/4 = 3 half-lives.
  • 960 → 480 → 240 → 120.
  • Activity = 120 Bq.

13. An isotope undergoes four half-lives. State the fraction remaining, the fraction decayed and the ratio of decline to initial activity.

Answer:
  • Remaining = (½)⁴ = 1/16.
  • Decayed = 1 − 1/16 = 15/16.
  • Decline : initial = 15 : 16.

14. A detector reads 110 counts/s with a source present and 10 counts/s without it. What total reading is expected after two half-lives?

Answer:
  • Initial source count rate = 110 − 10 = 100 counts/s.
  • After two half-lives, source rate = 100/4 = 25 counts/s.
  • Add unchanged background: 35 counts/s.

15. A nucleus with mass number 226 and atomic number 88 emits an alpha particle. Find both numbers for the daughter nucleus and explain the change.

Answer:
  • Daughter mass number = 226 − 4 = 222.
  • Daughter atomic number = 88 − 2 = 86.
  • An alpha particle removes two protons and two neutrons.

16. Explain why most alpha particles pass through thin metal foil but very few are deflected backwards.

Answer:
  • Most of the atom is empty space.
  • Positive alpha particles near the positive nucleus are repelled.
  • Very large deflections are rare because the dense nucleus occupies a tiny fraction of the atom.

17. Explain why a gamma-emitting isotope with a suitable short half-life can be useful as a medical tracer.

Answer:
  • Gamma can leave the body and reach an external detector.
  • The source lasts long enough to complete the investigation.
  • A suitably short half-life reduces continuing activity and exposure afterwards.

4 marks

18. Explain why an alpha-emitting contaminant can be much more dangerous inside the body than outside it.

Answer:
  • Alpha has low penetrating power and is largely stopped by outer skin.
  • Inside the body it is close to living cells without that protection.
  • It is highly ionising, depositing energy over a short distance.
  • This can damage cells and DNA, increasing the risk of mutations and cancer.

19. Compare nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.

Answer:
  • Fission splits a large unstable nucleus, usually after neutron absorption.
  • It releases smaller nuclei, neutrons, gamma radiation and energy; neutrons can continue a chain reaction.
  • Fusion joins light nuclei into a heavier one.
  • Fusion also releases energy as some mass is converted; it needs conditions allowing positive nuclei to overcome repulsion.

20. A detector measures a source for 60 s and records 3,600 counts. A background test records 300 counts in 60 s. Calculate the corrected source count rate and give two ways to improve the measurement.

Answer:
  • Background rate = 300/60 = 5 counts/s.
  • Corrected rate = 3,600/60 − 5 = 55 counts/s.
  • Count for longer or repeat to reduce relative random variation.
  • Keep distance and shielding fixed so geometry does not change the detection rate.

6 marks

21. Explain how neutron absorption can lead to a chain reaction and how controlling the chain differs from an uncontrolled reaction.

Answer:
  • A large unstable nucleus absorbs a neutron.
  • It splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy, gamma and two or three neutrons.
  • The products carry kinetic energy, which can transfer to thermal stores through collisions.
  • Released neutrons can be absorbed by other unstable nuclei, causing more fissions.
  • In a reactor, neutron absorption by control rods helps keep the chain and energy release at a controlled rate.
  • An uncontrolled multiplying chain allows very rapid energy release. It is the nuclei that split, not ordinary chemical bonds.

22. Two gamma tracers have half-lives of 10 minutes and 10 days. An organ investigation lasts 30 minutes. Explain how a scientist should choose between them; do not assume the shorter half-life is automatically suitable.

Answer:
  • Gamma is useful because it can leave the body and be detected externally.
  • The 10-minute isotope undergoes three half-lives, leaving only 1/8 of its initial activity after 30 minutes.
  • It may be suitable if enough signal remains; otherwise useful images could be lost or a larger initial activity might be needed.
  • The 10-day isotope changes little during the test but may expose the patient for longer unless removed quickly from the body.
  • Compare starting activity, biological removal, detector sensitivity and total dose, as well as half-life.
  • Choose a source that provides an adequate signal with the smallest justified dose, balancing the medical benefit and exposure rather than choosing on half-life alone.
