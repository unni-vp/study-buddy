Characters

Arthur Birling

  • Role: wealthy manufacturer, father and spokesman for self-interest. He hopes the marriage will improve his business and social standing.
  • Choices: dismisses Eva for seeking higher wages, resists responsibility and worries about scandal.
  • Development: his authority is weakened by the investigation, but he does not show Sheila's lasting change.
  • Quotation: “lower costs” and “higher prices” — the paired commercial aims reduce relationships to profit. Language: the economic vocabulary contrasts with the Inspector's concern for human welfare.

Arthur's wrong predictions also weaken trust in his wider opinions. Explain this connection rather than merely calling him foolish.

Sybil Birling

  • Role: upholds class boundaries and the family's respectable appearance.
  • Choices: rejects Eva's appeal to the charity partly because she dislikes her manner and use of the Birling name.
  • Development: condemns the father before discovering Eric's involvement; still resists responsibility afterwards.
  • Quotation: “girls of that class” — the distancing phrase turns individuals into an inferior social category. Language: the demonstrative and class label expose prejudice.

A charity role does not make her automatically compassionate. Priestley tests what she actually does with her power.

Sheila Birling

  • Role: begins as a sheltered daughter but becomes increasingly perceptive.
  • Choices: admits that jealousy and anger led her to misuse influence at the shop.
  • Development: accepts guilt, questions Gerald and understands that the lessons remain important even if Goole's identity is doubtful.
  • Quotation: “these girls aren't cheap labour” — she challenges the reduction of workers to a business expense. Language: the contrast between people and their economic value signals a change in outlook.

Her remorse does not erase the harm. Her willingness to face it gives the play a possibility of change.

Eric Birling

  • Role: uneasy son whose drinking and strained relationship with his parents conceal serious wrongdoing.
  • Choices: uses pressure in his encounter with Eva, then steals money to help her. Neither attempted help nor later remorse excuses the earlier harm.
  • Development: admits his actions and continues to acknowledge responsibility when others become relieved.
  • Quotation: “in that state” — his indirect description of drunken conduct avoids saying clearly what he did. Language: the evasive phrase exposes shame and discomfort.

Eric is a flawed agent of change, not a simple moral hero. Priestley allows regret while keeping the consequences visible.

Gerald Croft

  • Role: socially desirable fiancé and link to a more prestigious business family.
  • Choices: rescues Daisy from a threatening situation, gives her somewhere to live and begins an unequal affair.
  • Development: initially admits the relationship, but later investigates Goole's identity and joins the older Birlings' relief.
  • Quotation: “young and fresh and charming” — the three admiring descriptions foreground Daisy's attraction to him. Language: the listing helps expose the personal desire within his account of rescue.

He is neither wholly cruel nor wholly selfless. A stronger answer recognises both help and control.

Inspector Goole and Eva Smith

The Inspector

The Inspector controls the pace of disclosure, challenges evasions and temporarily displaces Arthur's authority. He acts as a moral investigator, although his precise identity is unresolved.

Quotation: “one body” — the metaphor presents society as interconnected: harm to one part matters to the whole. His speeches broaden the enquiry from one household to everyone affected by selfish choices.

Eva Smith / Daisy Renton

Eva never appears on stage. The audience learns about her through others, which reflects how powerful people control the telling of a vulnerable person's story.

She seeks fair pay, loses work, experiences unequal relationships and refuses stolen money. These details give her moral agency; she is not simply a passive object of pity. The common surname can suggest that her situation represents many ordinary people.
