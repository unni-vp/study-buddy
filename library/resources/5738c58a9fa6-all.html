Using resources questions & answers

1 mark

1. What is potable water?

Answer:

Water safe to drink.

2. Name a method that removes dissolved salt from seawater.

Answer:

Distillation.

3. What is the solid formed by sewage sedimentation called?

Answer:

Sludge.

4. Which organisms carry out bioleaching?

Answer:

Bacteria.

5. What two substances must be present for iron to rust?

Answer:

Oxygen and water.

6. Which metal commonly galvanises iron?

Answer:

Zinc.

7. Which two metals form bronze?

Answer:

Copper and tin.

8. Which two metals form brass?

Answer:

Copper and zinc.

9. What percentage gold is in 18-carat gold?

Answer:

75%.

10. What catalyst is used in the Haber process?

Answer:

Iron.

11. What does NPK stand for?

Answer:

Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.

12. Which polymer type has covalent cross-links and does not melt on heating?

Answer:

Thermosetting.

2 marks

13. Distinguish renewable from finite resources.

Answer:
  • Renewable resources can be replenished on useful timescales if managed appropriately.
  • Finite resources are used far faster than they are replaced, so available reserves decline.

14. Define sustainable development.

Answer:
  • Development meeting current generations’ needs.
  • Without compromising future generations’ ability to meet theirs.

15. Why is potable water not necessarily chemically pure?

Answer:
  • It is safe because microbes and harmful substances are sufficiently low.
  • It still contains dissolved substances, so it is not only H₂O.

16. Give one purpose of filtering water and one purpose of sterilising it.

Answer:
  • Filtering removes suspended solid particles.
  • Sterilising kills microorganisms.

17. Give two sterilisation methods for freshwater supplies.

Answer:

Chlorine and ultraviolet light.

18. Why does filtering seawater not usually make it potable?

Answer:
  • Ordinary filters do not remove the dissolved salts.
  • Desalination, such as distillation or reverse osmosis, is needed to reduce salt levels sufficiently.

19. Describe how distillation removes dissolved salts.

Answer:
  • Water evaporates, leaving dissolved salts behind.
  • Vapour is cooled and condensed into collected water.

20. Distinguish sewage-sludge treatment from effluent treatment.

Answer:
  • Sludge undergoes anaerobic digestion.
  • Effluent undergoes aerobic biological treatment.

21. Describe bioleaching of copper compounds.

Answer:
  • Bacteria help produce a leachate solution.
  • The leachate contains dissolved copper compounds for further metal recovery.

22. Why can scrap iron recover copper from copper-salt solution?

Answer:
  • Iron is more reactive than copper.
  • It displaces copper from its compound, producing copper metal.

23. Give two reasons recycling copper can be better than extracting more ore.

Answer:
  • It conserves finite ore resources.
  • It can require less energy and cause less mining damage than fresh extraction.

24. Why can painting fail to protect scratched iron?

Answer:
  • A scratch breaks the barrier.
  • Water and oxygen can then reach the iron and cause rusting.

25. Explain why zinc can protect scratched galvanised iron.

Answer:
  • Zinc is more reactive and oxidises preferentially.
  • It gives sacrificial protection while enough connected zinc remains.

26. Why does aluminium resist further corrosion after its surface reacts?

Answer:
  • It forms an adherent oxide coating.
  • The coating acts as a barrier to further reaction of the underlying metal.

27. State a use of stainless steel and a property supporting it.

Answer:
  • Cutlery or kitchen equipment.
  • It is hard and corrosion-resistant.

28. State a use of low-carbon steel and a supporting property.

Answer:
  • Car body panels.
  • It is comparatively soft and easily shaped.

29. Give a use of high-carbon steel and its relevant limitation.

Answer:
  • Cutting tools need its hardness/strength.
  • It is brittle, which can limit uses requiring bending or impact resistance.

30. Explain why aluminium alloys suit aircraft structures.

Answer:
  • They have low density, reducing mass.
  • Suitable alloys combine this with useful strength.

31. Name the three main starting materials for soda-lime glass.

Answer:

Sand, sodium carbonate and limestone.

32. What two roles make up a composite material?

Answer:
  • A matrix/binder holds the structure together.
  • A reinforcement, such as fibres, supplies useful strength or other properties.

33. State a usual source of each Haber-process reactant.

Answer:
  • Nitrogen from air.
  • Hydrogen from natural gas.

34. What happens to ammonia and unused gases after the Haber reactor?

Answer:
  • Ammonia is cooled so it liquefies and is removed.
  • Unreacted nitrogen/hydrogen are recycled.

35. Why is untreated phosphate rock not an effective direct fertiliser?

Answer:
  • It is poorly soluble.
  • Nutrients cannot readily dissolve and become available for plant uptake.

36. Name the fertiliser products associated with sulfuric and phosphoric acid treatment of phosphate rock.

Answer:
  • Sulfuric acid: single superphosphate, including calcium sulfate and soluble phosphate compounds.
  • Phosphoric acid: triple superphosphate, mainly calcium dihydrogenphosphate.

3 marks

37. Describe phytomining and the form of the recovered material.

Answer:
  • Plants absorb metal compounds from soil.
  • Harvest and burn the biomass.
  • Ash contains metal compounds, requiring further processing to obtain metal.

38. An empty basin is 24.50 g; after evaporating 50 cm³ water it is 24.65 g. Calculate residue mass and concentration in g dm⁻³.

Answer:
  • Residue = 24.65−24.50 = 0.15 g.
  • Volume = 50/1000 = 0.050 dm³.
  • Concentration = 0.15/0.050 = 3.0 g dm⁻³.

39. A 16 g jewellery alloy is 18 carat. Calculate gold mass and explain why alloying is useful.

Answer:
  • Gold fraction = 18/24 = 0.75.
  • Gold mass = 16 ×0.75 = 12 g.
  • Alloying increases hardness/wear resistance compared with soft pure gold.

40. Explain why alloys are usually harder than the pure metal.

Answer:
  • Pure metal has relatively regular layers of similar-sized atoms.
  • Other elements introduce different sizes and distort the layers.
  • Layers slide less easily.

41. Describe manufacturing clay ceramics.

Answer:
  • Shape wet clay into the required form.
  • Dry as appropriate and heat strongly in a furnace.
  • The resulting hard material can form pottery or bricks, though it is brittle.

42. Explain how LDPE and HDPE can both be made from ethene yet have different properties.

Answer:
  • Different conditions/catalysts produce different chain arrangements.
  • LDPE’s greater branching prevents close packing.
  • HDPE packs more closely, giving higher density and greater rigidity.

43. Explain why thermosetting polymers do not melt like thermosoftening polymers.

Answer:
  • Thermosetting chains have covalent cross-links.
  • These prevent chains moving freely on heating.
  • Thermosoftening chains are separate, so overcoming intermolecular attractions allows movement and melting.

44. State the Haber equation and explain the gas ratio.

Answer:
  • N₂+3H₂ ⇌2NH₃.
  • One nitrogen molecule reacts with three hydrogen molecules.
  • Feed gases therefore use a 1:3 nitrogen:hydrogen molar ratio.

45. Explain why high pressure favours ammonia in the Haber process.

Answer:
  • There are four gas moles on the reactant side and two on the product side.
  • High pressure favours fewer gas moles.
  • Equilibrium shifts towards ammonia, increasing its proportion.

46. Name the products from nitric acid and phosphate rock, and link one to fertiliser use.

Answer:
  • Products include calcium nitrate.
  • Phosphoric acid is also produced.
  • Calcium nitrate supplies plant nutrients in a soluble salt; phosphoric acid can support further phosphate-fertiliser manufacture.

4 marks

47. Describe a controlled experiment showing rust needs both oxygen and water.

Answer:
  • Use comparable clean iron nails, same time and temperature.
  • One nail has air and water and should rust.
  • One has dry air with a drying agent, preventing water exposure.
  • One has boiled water covered with oil, removing/excluding oxygen; little/no rust in the latter two supports both requirements.

48. Explain the four major life-cycle stages and why transport matters.

Answer:
  • Raw-material extraction/processing.
  • Manufacture and packaging.
  • Use over the product’s useful lifetime.
  • Disposal/reuse/recycling, with transport between stages adding energy use and emissions.

49. Explain why a reusable glass bottle can have lower impact per use despite higher production energy.

Answer:
  • Manufacture impact is spread over many uses.
  • Reuse avoids making a new bottle for every use.
  • Washing and return transport still consume resources/energy.
  • Actual reuse count, breakage and travel determine whether the overall comparison is favourable.

50. Describe the main wastewater treatment sequence.

Answer:
  • Screen/remove grit.
  • Sediment to separate sludge and effluent.
  • Digest sludge anaerobically.
  • Treat effluent aerobically, with extra chemical treatment if required by contaminants.

6 marks

51. Evaluate a biological method for extracting copper from low-grade material.

Answer:
  • Phytomining concentrates compounds in plant ash; bioleaching produces a solution of metal compounds.
  • Copper still requires recovery, for example iron displacement or electrolysis.
  • These methods can reduce excavation of huge rock volumes and use deposits unsuitable for conventional extraction.
  • They may be slow, require land/controlled conditions and risk harmful leachate.
  • Compare measured yield, time, energy, cost and pollution with mining/recycling alternatives.
  • A justified choice depends on the deposit and controls, rather than biological methods being automatically harmless.

52. Evaluate paper and plastic shopping bags using a fair LCA.

Answer:
  • Compare the same load-carrying service and realistic reuse numbers.
  • Include raw materials, manufacture, transport, use and disposal/recycling.
  • Paper may use renewable feedstock but considerable water/energy; plastic can be light but often uses finite feedstock.
  • Durability, litter, disposal and collection affect the practical outcome.
  • Use reliable quantitative energy/resource data while recognising value judgements in weighting impacts.
  • Give a conclusion tied to the evidence and assumptions, avoiding a selective single-stage advertisement.

53. Explain why the Haber process uses about 450°C, 200 atmospheres and an iron catalyst.

Answer:
  • Forward ammonia formation is exothermic, so lower temperature favours equilibrium yield.
  • Very low temperature gives too slow a rate; about 450°C balances yield and useful production speed.
  • Pressure favours ammonia because four reactant gas moles become two product gas moles.
  • Excessive pressure costs compression energy and stronger safe equipment; around 200 atmospheres is a practical compromise.
  • Iron lowers activation energy and speeds both directions without changing equilibrium yield.
  • Cooling removes liquid ammonia and unused gases are recycled, improving overall production.

54. Compare preparing an ammonium fertiliser salt in a laboratory and in an integrated industrial process.

Answer:
  • Both use appropriate chemical reactions and controlled quantities, such as ammonia neutralisation with acid.
  • Laboratory titration can determine proportions, followed by an indicator-free mixture and crystallisation.
  • Industrial manufacture uses larger-scale equipment, often continuous flow, monitoring and heat management.
  • Raw-material and acid production can be integrated, with useful streams recycled.
  • Final NPK composition must meet plant nutrient needs and purity requirements.
  • Evaluate supplied yield, energy, safety and cost data rather than assuming classroom apparatus simply scales up unchanged.
