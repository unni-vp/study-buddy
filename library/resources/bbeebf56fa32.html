Creative prose writing questions & answers

1 mark2 marks4 marks8 marks40 marks

1 mark

1. What is narrative viewpoint?

Answer:

The perspective through which events are presented.

2. What is a motif?

Answer:

A significant detail or image that recurs and develops meaning.

3. What is a narrative turning point?

Answer:

An event or discovery that changes the direction or understanding of the story.

4. What is a comma splice?

Answer:

Two complete clauses joined only by a comma.

5. Which form does the creative prose task require?

Answer:

A narrative or recount.

6. How many titles or prompts are offered?

Answer:

Four.

2 marks

7. Why can a limited time span help a short story?

Answer:

It gives space to develop one important event and its effects, rather than rushing through a large plot.

8. Explain why description alone may not fulfil this task.

Answer:

The response needs narrative development: events, a recount or a meaningful change. A static description alone does not fully meet that purpose.

9. What is wrong with a first-person narrator stating an unseen character's thoughts as certain facts?

Answer:

The narrator does not automatically know those thoughts. Show observable behaviour or make clear that the narrator is inferring.

10. Correct this sentence in two ways: The room was empty, the marks remained.

Answer:
  • The room was empty. The marks remained.
  • The room was empty, but the marks remained.

A semicolon between the complete clauses would also work.

11. Explain one useful reason to repeat an object near the ending.

Answer:

Its meaning can change after the event, connecting the ending to the opening and revealing the character's changed understanding.

12. Why is “show, never tell” too rigid?

Answer:

Summary can move through uneventful time efficiently. Closely showing the important moment then gives that moment more space and emphasis.

4 marks

13. Improve “He was very worried” by showing behaviour, then explain the choices.

Answer:

He searched the same pocket twice. When his hand came out empty again, he checked the chair as if it might have moved.

The repeated search makes the worry visible. The uncertain second action suggests that he cannot settle on what to do next. The detail expresses anxiety without simply renaming it.

14. Plan a short story for the title The Small Decision using a want, an obstacle, a choice and a consequence.

Answer:
  • Want: a café worker wants to catch the bus after a tiring shift.
  • Obstacle: a customer leaves the glasses he needs.
  • Choice: leave them in the drawer or run after him.
  • Consequence: returning them reveals why the customer struggled, changing the worker's judgement.

The stages connect causally. The event creates the change, rather than unrelated incidents being placed next to one another.

15. Write an opening that introduces a significant object and a question for the reader. Explain it.

Answer:

The last customer had gone, but his table looked as though he was still there. A spoon rested across the saucer. Beside the sugar bowl lay a pair of glasses with one arm wrapped in brown tape.

The contrast between absence and traces of presence creates curiosity. The damaged glasses suggest a particular owner and provide an object that can drive the event.

16. Correct the tense, agreement, apostrophe and sentence boundary: She walk to the door, the boys coat is still on the hook. (one boy; past-tense narrative)

Answer:

She walked to the door. The boy's coat was still on the hook.

Walked and was maintain past tense. Boy's shows singular possession. The full stop separates the complete clauses.

17. Write two lines of dialogue in which one character wants to leave and the other delays. Make the delay visible without naming the emotion.

Answer:

“Are you ready?” she asked, holding the door.

“Nearly.” He folded the letter again, although it already fitted inside the envelope.

The new speaker gets a new paragraph. The unnecessary folding suggests hesitation, while the held door creates pressure to move.

18. Give four editing priorities for a complete creative response.

Answer:
  • The exact title or prompt is fulfilled.
  • Events and viewpoint remain coherent.
  • The central moment and ending are developed.
  • Sentences, tense, punctuation and spelling are accurate.

A useful edit improves meaning as well as checking individual words.

8 marks

19. Develop a paragraph in which an empty room changes from feeling merely bare to feeling emotionally significant. Explain the paragraph afterwards.

Answer:

Without the wardrobe, a pale rectangle showed on the wall. At first I noticed only the dust beneath it. Then I saw the pencil marks: a little ladder of dates rising beside the door, the lowest decorated with a crooked star. I had forgotten how carefully we used to measure a year. The room was empty, but it had not stopped keeping count.

The focus moves from a practical detail to a meaningful discovery. The ladder image makes growth visible; the final personification links the room to memory. The paragraph develops a change in perception rather than adding a detached description.

20. Write an ending that echoes an earlier object but gives it a changed meaning. Use a character leaving home. Explain the structural effect.

Answer:

At the doorway I looked back once. The pencil marks were still on the wall, too faint to see from here. In my pocket, my phone held three photographs: my sister, me, and the crooked ladder between us. I picked up the last box. The marks would stay; the years were coming with us.

The repeated marks connect the ending to the earlier discovery. Photographs provide a plausible action through which the character accepts leaving. The last contrast resolves the emotional problem without pretending the house itself can be taken away.

21. Compare these openings and explain which better establishes a controllable story: “It was a nice day and I woke up” / “My bus was due in six minutes when I found the glasses.”

Answer:

The second opening establishes a deadline and a problem immediately. The glasses provide a specific object whose owner may need help, while the bus gives the narrator a competing priority. That conflict can develop within a short episode.

The first opening is not automatically wrong, but it gives little direction by itself. Unless the weather or waking matters to the event, it delays the story without adding much. A stronger version would connect the ordinary beginning to a relevant disturbance.

The judgement explains the effects of the choices. It does not claim that every story must begin with action or that an opening sentence alone determines its quality.

40 marks

22. Write a story for the title The Small Decision. Aim for about 450–600 words.

Answer:

The last customer had gone, but his table looked as though he was still there. A spoon rested across the saucer. His chair pointed towards the window. Beside the sugar bowl lay a pair of glasses with one arm wrapped in brown tape.

I glanced at the clock. Six minutes until my bus.

All afternoon I had carried plates between tables, answering questions about things printed clearly on the menu. The man by the window had asked me to read it to him twice. When I explained that the kitchen was closing, he had looked at me as though I had announced something unreasonable. I had been polite. Mostly.

Now I wanted my coat, my wages and the seat upstairs on the bus where nobody would ask me for anything.

I lifted the glasses by their good arm. Outside, the evening had turned the pavement silver. Through the window I could see a dark coat moving towards the bus stop. It might be his. It might not.

“Lost property box,” my manager said, counting coins at the till.

I opened the drawer. There were two scarves, a child's glove and a key without a label. The glasses would fit easily on top. Tomorrow, somebody else could deal with them.

My phone vibrated. My sister had sent a photograph of the dog asleep on my bed, followed by a question about when I would be home. I began to type. Then I looked again at the glasses. The lenses were thick enough to bend the edge of the counter behind them.

“I'll be a minute,” I said.

The pavement was slippery. I ran awkwardly, holding the glasses inside my fist, and reached the stop as the bus pulled in. The man was searching his pockets. He checked one, then the other, then started again.

“You left these.”

He looked at my closed hand before he looked at me. When I opened it, his shoulders dropped. For a moment he did not take them.

“I thought I'd lost them on the road.”

He put them on carefully, first one ear, then the other. Behind the lenses his expression changed, as if the world had moved back into its proper place.

“I couldn't read the numbers,” he said, nodding towards the buses. “Thank you.”

There was no reason for him to explain anything else. Still, I heard again the impatient brightness of my own voice when I had read out the menu. I had assumed he was being difficult. It was an uncomfortable thing to carry, that small certainty I had mistaken for knowledge.

The driver tapped the open door. “Either of you coming?”

We climbed aboard. I let the man go first and watched him read the number above the windscreen before he found a seat. Upstairs, my usual place was empty. I sat down, opened my phone and deleted the message I had started.

On my way, I wrote. Tell the dog to move over.

Then I put the phone away and looked properly out of the window.

Development: the deadline creates a choice; returning the glasses changes the narrator's judgement.

Organisation: a single episode moves from the café to the bus stop and back to the narrator's reflection. The final act of looking connects to the glasses motif.

Style: concrete objects, short dialogue and selective reflection support the voice. Sentence lengths change for emphasis without losing control.

23. Write a story beginning “I had almost walked away when I noticed the line.” Aim for about 450–600 words.

Answer:

I had almost walked away when I noticed the line.

It was half-hidden by a pale rectangle where the wardrobe had stood. A pencil mark, no longer than my thumb, with a date beside it. Underneath, in my father's square handwriting, was my name.

The room had looked smaller once we emptied it. Without the bed and its familiar heap of clothes, there was nowhere for my eyes to settle. Dust lay in the corners. On the landing, somebody pulled another strip of tape across a box. The sound was sharp enough to make me turn.

“Ready?” my sister called.

“Nearly.”

I went back to the wall. Below the first mark was another, then a crowded little ladder of them reaching towards the skirting board. The lowest had a crooked star beside it. I remembered insisting that being measured counted as an achievement. I had wanted a sticker. My father had found a pencil instead.

At the top, the last mark stopped just below my shoulder. We had not made a new one for years. At some point, growing had become something I expected rather than something worth announcing.

My sister appeared in the doorway carrying a box labelled KITCHEN. A wooden spoon stuck out through the flaps.

“We really have to go.”

I pointed. She came closer and bent to read the smallest date.

“That's mine,” she said.

I had forgotten that her marks were there too. They ran beside mine, always trying to catch up. She traced one with a finger, stopping before she touched the pencil.

Downstairs, the front door banged against its stop. Someone called for the last box. The house was becoming a place with echoes, each familiar sound returning from the wrong direction.

“Can we take it?” I asked.

My sister looked at the wall, then at me. For a second I thought she might laugh. Instead, she put the box down.

We considered the problem seriously. The marks could not be peeled away like a poster. Painting over them would not make them travel with us. Leaving them felt unexpectedly like forgetting something important, although we had already checked every cupboard twice.

Then she took out her phone.

“Stand there.”

I stood beside the top mark. She moved back, frowned at the screen and told me to stop looking as though I was waiting for a school photograph. I laughed, and she took the picture before I could arrange my face again.

We exchanged places. I photographed her with the crooked star just visible below her elbow. Then we took one picture of the whole ladder, dates and smudges and all.

Outside, the car was waiting. I picked up the kitchen box while she checked that the photographs were saved. At the doorway I looked back once, not to search for another forgotten object, but to see the room as it was.

The marks stayed on the wall. For the first time that morning, this did not seem the same as leaving them behind.

Development: discovering the height marks creates a small but genuine problem; the siblings' response allows the narrator to accept leaving.

Organisation: attention narrows from the empty room to the marks, opens into memory, then returns to a practical action. The ending revisits the idea of leaving something behind.

Style: the ladder image and changed echoes make the setting meaningful. Dialogue and paragraph shifts keep the story moving.
