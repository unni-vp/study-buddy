How do bacteria and viruses cause illness?

Answer
  • Bacteria can multiply rapidly and release toxins that damage tissues.

Which four groups of pathogen can cause communicable disease?

Answer
  • Bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists.
QuestionPathogens and transmission

What is a vector?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • An organism that carries a pathogen between hosts.
  • For malaria, the mosquito is the vector and the protist is the pathogen.
QuestionPathogens and transmission

What are common routes of pathogen transmission?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Direct contact, water and air.
  • Contaminated food and animal vectors can also spread pathogens.
QuestionHuman infections

What causes measles, how does it spread, and what are its main symptoms?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Measles is caused by a virus spread in droplets from coughs and sneezes.
  • It causes fever and a red skin rash; serious complications can be fatal.
  • Vaccination reduces illness and spread.
QuestionHuman infections

What causes HIV, and how does it spread?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • HIV is a virus spread through sexual contact or exchange of body fluids, including blood from shared needles.
  • Condoms and avoiding shared needles reduce transmission.
QuestionHuman infections

What is the difference between HIV infection and AIDS?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • HIV attacks immune cells and can initially cause a flu-like illness.
  • AIDS is the late stage when the immune system is severely damaged, making other infections and some cancers more likely.
QuestionHuman infections

What causes Salmonella food poisoning, and how can it be controlled?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Salmonella bacteria are swallowed in contaminated food.
  • They and their toxins cause fever, cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea.
  • Food hygiene, thorough cooking and poultry vaccination reduce spread.
QuestionHuman infections

What causes gonorrhoea, and how is it controlled?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Gonorrhoea is a bacterial sexually transmitted infection.
  • It can cause pain when urinating and a thick yellow or green discharge.
  • Condoms reduce spread; suitable antibiotics treat it, but resistant strains are harder to treat.
QuestionHuman infections

What causes malaria, and how is it controlled?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • A protist causes malaria; mosquitoes transfer it between people.
  • Mosquito nets reduce bites, and removing breeding sites reduces vector numbers.
  • A common symptom is recurrent fever.
QuestionBody defences

How do the nose, trachea and bronchi defend against pathogens?

  • Nasal hairs and mucus trap particles and pathogens.

How does the stomach defend against pathogens?

Answer
  • Hydrochloric acid kills many pathogens that have been swallowed.
QuestionBody defences

What is phagocytosis?

Answer

QuestionBody defences

How do antibodies defend against pathogens?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Antibodies bind to specific antigens on a pathogen.
  • The complementary binding helps the immune system destroy the pathogen.
QuestionBody defences

What do antitoxins do?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • They neutralise toxins released by bacteria.
  • They do not directly kill the bacteria.
QuestionVaccination

How does vaccination produce immunity?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • A vaccine introduces dead or inactive pathogens, or their antigens.
  • White blood cells make specific antibodies, and some become memory cells.
  • Later exposure causes a faster, larger antibody response that destroys the pathogen before significant illness develops.
QuestionVaccination

What is the role of memory cells?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • They remain after the first exposure and recognise the same antigen later.
  • They allow the correct antibodies to be produced more quickly and in greater amounts.
QuestionVaccination

How can high vaccination uptake protect people who are not vaccinated?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • When many people are immune, an infected person has fewer susceptible people to infect.
  • Transmission falls, indirectly protecting people who cannot be vaccinated.
QuestionVaccination

What should be considered when evaluating a vaccination programme?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Reduction in disease and transmission, reliable evidence about risks, cost, access and vaccine uptake.
  • Use the data provided; no vaccine protects every individual.
QuestionMedicines and resistance

What do antibiotics treat?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Antibiotics kill bacteria or stop their growth.
  • Different bacteria may require different antibiotics.
QuestionMedicines and resistance

Why do antibiotics not kill viruses?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Viruses reproduce inside body cells.
  • It is difficult to target the virus without also damaging the body's own cells.
QuestionMedicines and resistance

What is the difference between an antibiotic and a painkiller?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • An antibiotic targets the cause of a suitable bacterial infection.
  • A painkiller reduces symptoms such as pain but does not kill the pathogen.
QuestionMedicines and resistance

How does antibiotic resistance arise and why is it a problem?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Resistant bacteria survive treatment and reproduce.
  • The infection becomes harder to treat, while new treatments take time and money to develop.

What happens during clinical drug trials?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Very low doses are first given to healthy volunteers to investigate safety.
  • Further trials use patients to test efficacy and find the optimum dose.
QuestionDrug development

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Toxicity: whether it causes harm.
  • Efficacy: whether it works.
  • Dose: the amount that gives useful effects with acceptable risk.
QuestionDrug development

What is a placebo, and why is it used?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • A placebo resembles the treatment but contains no active drug.
  • It helps separate the drug's effect from expectations and other changes.
QuestionDrug development

What is a double-blind trial, and why is it useful?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Neither patients nor the researchers assessing them know who receives the drug until results are analysed.
  • This reduces bias.
QuestionDrug development

How do random allocation and large comparable groups improve a clinical trial?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Random allocation reduces selection bias.
  • Large samples reduce the effect of chance.
  • Comparable groups make differences in outcomes more likely to be caused by the treatment.

QuestionMonoclonal antibodies

What is a monoclonal antibody?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • One of a batch of identical antibodies made by a single clone of cells.
  • Each binds to one specific binding site on one protein antigen.
QuestionMonoclonal antibodies

How is a hybridoma made?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • A mouse is exposed to an antigen, then antibody-producing lymphocytes are collected.
  • A lymphocyte is fused with a tumour cell to make a hybridoma.
QuestionMonoclonal antibodies

Why are lymphocytes fused with tumour cells when making monoclonal antibodies?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • The lymphocyte makes the specific antibody.
  • The tumour cell can divide repeatedly.
  • The hybridoma combines both useful properties.
QuestionMonoclonal antibodies

What happens after a suitable hybridoma is formed?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Select a hybridoma making the required antibody, clone it and grow many identical cells.
  • Collect and purify the antibodies they release.

How can monoclonal antibodies be used in research?

Answer
  • Attach a fluorescent dye to an antibody.
  • The antibody binds to its target molecule, revealing the molecule's location in cells or tissues.
QuestionMonoclonal antibodies

How can monoclonal antibodies treat cancer, and what is a limitation?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • They can deliver a toxic drug, radioactive substance or growth-blocking chemical to cells with the target antigen.
  • They can still cause side effects, and not every cancer has a suitable target.

Answer
  • They feed on sugar-rich sap, removing substances needed for growth.

QuestionPlant disease

What do nitrate and magnesium deficiencies cause?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Nitrate deficiency causes stunted growth because nitrates are needed to make amino acids and proteins.
  • Magnesium deficiency causes yellowing called chlorosis because magnesium is needed to make chlorophyll.
QuestionPlant disease

How can plant disease be detected and identified?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Observe signs such as spots, decay, stunting, discolouration, malformed growth or pests.
  • Compare with reliable guides, send samples to a laboratory, or use a monoclonal-antibody testing kit.
  • Combine evidence because similar symptoms can have different causes.
QuestionPlant disease

What physical defences protect plants?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Cellulose cell walls, a tough waxy cuticle and bark resist pathogen entry.
  • Shedding dead bark can remove pathogens.
QuestionPlant disease

What chemical and mechanical defences protect plants?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Antibacterial chemicals act against bacteria; poisons deter herbivores.
  • Thorns and hairs deter feeding; touch-sensitive drooping or curling and mimicry can discourage animals.
