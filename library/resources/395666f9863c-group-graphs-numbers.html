Read the labels before explaining a graph

Start with what is measured, where, when and in which units. A chart of UK electricity generation cannot describe all energy consumption. A historical disaster map cannot automatically describe the present day. A population pyramid shows age and sex, but its shape alone does not establish why people migrated or births changed.

PresentationWhat to checkWhat you can reasonably conclude
Measured chartYear, source, units, scale and category definitions.Describe the pattern with values and keep the conclusion within that dataset.
Model diagramWhich relationships are simplified and what is left out.Explain the model’s proposed pattern, without treating it as measured data for a particular country.
Disaster mapTime span, event definition, reporting and whether values are totals or rates.Compare the recorded pattern, then consider exposure and vulnerability as well as the hazard.

Percentage points and percentage change are different. If one generation share is 50.4% and another is 31.8%, the difference is 18.6 percentage points. Percentage change instead divides the change by a starting value and multiplies by 100.

Scatter graphs

  • Put the possible cause on the x-axis and the possible effect on the y-axis.
  • A positive correlation rises from left to right.
  • A negative correlation falls from left to right.
  • No correlation means no clear relationship.
  • A line of best fit summarises the trend; an anomaly lies away from it.
  • Correlation suggests a relationship but does not prove causation.

Building a clear presentation

Use a suitable title, labelled axes, units, even scale and an accurate key. Choose intervals that use the available space without distorting the pattern.

To analyse, use pattern + evidence + exception:

  1. State the overall trend.
  2. Support it with values and locations.
  3. Identify and suggest a reason for an anomaly.

Numerical and statistical skills

Averages and spread

  • Mean: total of all values ÷ number of values.
  • Median: middle value after ordering the data.
  • Mode: most frequent value.
  • Modal class: group containing the greatest frequency.
  • Range: highest − lowest.
  • Interquartile range: upper quartile − lower quartile; it measures the spread of the middle 50%.

Worked example: 2, 3, 3, 6, 11

  • Mean = 25 ÷ 5 = 5
  • Median = 3
  • Mode = 3
  • Range = 11 − 2 = 9

The mean is raised by the extreme value 11, so the median may better represent a typical value.

Percentages

Percentage = part ÷ whole × 100

Percentage change = change ÷ original value × 100

Example: traffic rises from 80 to 100 vehicles. Change = 20. Percentage increase = 20 ÷ 80 × 100 = 25%.

Interpolation and extrapolation

  • Interpolation estimates within the known data range; it is usually safer.
  • Extrapolation estimates beyond the known range; it is less reliable because the trend may change.
