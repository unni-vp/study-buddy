Vowels and nasal sounds

From letters to sounds

French spelling works like a code: a group of letters can make one sound, and some written letters are silent. Learn the group, not an English sound for every letter.

The symbols between /slashes/ show sounds. Examples and mouth position help you connect them to real French. Use the pronunciation sound bank to hear words spoken.

Keep vowels steady rather than sliding between two sounds. French rhythm groups words into phrases; the final spoken syllable of a phrase often receives emphasis. Do not stress every word as you would in English.

Clear vowel contrasts

SpellingSound and useful example
a/a/: avec, arriver; open the mouth
i / y/i/: public, physique; a high front vowel
closed eu/ø/: deux, peu; tongue forward, lips rounded
e/ə/: le, de; a short, relaxed vowel that can sometimes disappear in connected speech
au / eau / closed o / ô/o/: beau, sauf; rounded lips, a steady vowel
ou/u/: sous, toujours; tongue towards the back, rounded lips
u/y/: dur, connu; tongue as for /i/, with rounded lips
silent final ejeune, chaque; final written e usually adds no syllable, but the preceding consonant is heard
é / infinitive -er / verb -ez/e/: été, parler, parlez; do not pronounce -er as two separate letters
è / ê / ai/ɛ/: collège, fête, vrai; more open than /e/
oi / oy/wa/, often /waj/ for oy before another vowel: mois, moyen
open eu / œu/œ/: leur, cœur; more open than the vowel in deux
open o/ɔ/: porte, alors; more open than the vowel in beau

Ou and u are different: sous /su/ and su /sy/ do not sound the same. For u, keep your tongue forward and round your lips. For ou, move the tongue further back.

É and è differ: the vowel in été is closer; the vowel in mère is more open. An accent can change pronunciation and spelling.

Nasal vowels

A nasal vowel sends some air through the nose. Do not add a separate strong n or m after it. The spelling is a guide to the vowel, rather than another consonant to pronounce.

SpellingSoundExamples
en / an / em / am/ɑ̃/grand, immédiatement
on / om/ɔ̃/bon, tomber
ain / in / aim / im/ɛ̃/matin, faim
-ien/jɛ̃/rien, canadien

These combinations are commonly nasal before a consonant or at the end of a word. A following vowel or doubled consonant can change the pattern: bon has a nasal vowel; bonne has a pronounced n and a different vowel.

Avoid confusing an, on and in. The final sounds in grand, bon and matin are different.
