QuestionExplain and compare

Why is radioactive decay described as random even though a sample has a predictable half-life?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • The decay time of an individual nucleus cannot be predicted.
  • Large numbers follow a predictable overall halving pattern.
QuestionApply and calculate

An isotope has activity 960 Bq and half-life 4 hours. Calculate its activity after 12 hours.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • 12/4 = 3 half-lives.
  • 960 → 480 → 240 → 120.
  • Activity = 120 Bq.
QuestionApply and calculate

An isotope undergoes four half-lives. State the fraction remaining, the fraction decayed and the ratio of decline to initial activity.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Remaining = (½)⁴ = 1/16.
  • Decayed = 1 − 1/16 = 15/16.
  • Decline : initial = 15 : 16.
QuestionApply and calculate

A detector reads 110 counts/s with a source present and 10 counts/s without it. What total reading is expected after two half-lives?

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Initial source count rate = 110 − 10 = 100 counts/s.
  • After two half-lives, source rate = 100/4 = 25 counts/s.
  • Add unchanged background: 35 counts/s.
QuestionApply and calculate

A nucleus with mass number 226 and atomic number 88 emits an alpha particle. Find both numbers for the daughter nucleus and explain the change.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Daughter mass number = 226 − 4 = 222.
  • Daughter atomic number = 88 − 2 = 86.
  • An alpha particle removes two protons and two neutrons.
QuestionApply and calculate

Explain why most alpha particles pass through thin metal foil but very few are deflected backwards.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Most of the atom is empty space.
  • Positive alpha particles near the positive nucleus are repelled.
  • Very large deflections are rare because the dense nucleus occupies a tiny fraction of the atom.
QuestionApply and calculate

Explain why a gamma-emitting isotope with a suitable short half-life can be useful as a medical tracer.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Gamma can leave the body and reach an external detector.
  • The source lasts long enough to complete the investigation.
  • A suitably short half-life reduces continuing activity and exposure afterwards.
QuestionMulti-step application

Explain why an alpha-emitting contaminant can be much more dangerous inside the body than outside it.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Alpha has low penetrating power and is largely stopped by outer skin.
  • Inside the body it is close to living cells without that protection.
  • It is highly ionising, depositing energy over a short distance.
  • This can damage cells and DNA, increasing the risk of mutations and cancer.
QuestionMulti-step application

Compare nuclear fission and nuclear fusion.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Fission splits a large unstable nucleus, usually after neutron absorption.
  • It releases smaller nuclei, neutrons, gamma radiation and energy; neutrons can continue a chain reaction.
  • Fusion joins light nuclei into a heavier one.
  • Fusion also releases energy as some mass is converted; it needs conditions allowing positive nuclei to overcome repulsion.
QuestionMulti-step application

A detector measures a source for 60 s and records 3,600 counts. A background test records 300 counts in 60 s. Calculate the corrected source count rate and give two ways to improve the measurement.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Background rate = 300/60 = 5 counts/s.
  • Corrected rate = 3,600/60 − 5 = 55 counts/s.
  • Count for longer or repeat to reduce relative random variation.
  • Keep distance and shielding fixed so geometry does not change the detection rate.
