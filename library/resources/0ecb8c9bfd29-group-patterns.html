Models & periodic patterns

How scientific models changed

A scientific model is an explanation supported by evidence. New evidence can force it to change, like updating a map after discovering a road runs somewhere else.

Evidence or ideaChange to the atomic model
Early ideasAtoms were tiny, indivisible spheres.
Discovery of the electronThe plum pudding model: negative electrons embedded in a ball of positive charge.
Alpha-particle scatteringMost particles passed through; some deflected; a tiny number bounced back. This supported a small, dense, positive nucleus and mostly empty space.
Bohr's modelElectrons occupy specific shells/energy levels at particular distances from the nucleus.
Later nuclear experimentsPositive nuclear charge was assigned to protons.
Chadwick's experimentsEvidence showed neutrons exist in the nucleus.

Link observation to conclusion: most pass through → mostly empty space; some deflect → positive charge repels positive alpha particles; very few return → mass and positive charge occupy a tiny, dense nucleus. This could not be explained by positive charge spread throughout a plum pudding atom.

Development of the periodic table

  • Early tables followed atomic weight. Some chemically similar elements ended up in different groups.
  • Mendeleev left gaps for undiscovered elements and sometimes changed the strict mass order to keep similar properties together.
  • He predicted missing elements' properties. Later discoveries matched these predictions, strengthening confidence in his table.
  • Discovery of isotopes explained why average atomic mass does not always follow atomic-number order. Modern tables use proton number.

Groups 0, 1 and 7

Group 0: noble gases

  • Examples: helium, neon, argon. Their outer electron shells are full and stable.
  • They are very unreactive and exist as individual atoms, not ordinary diatomic molecules.
  • Boiling points increase down the group as relative atomic mass increases. Use the trend to make predictions from data.

Group 1: alkali metals

Lithium, sodium and potassium have one outer electron and form +1 ions. They are soft, low-density metals compared with most transition metals.

  • With water: metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen. The hydroxide solution is alkaline.
  • Example: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂.
  • Lithium fizzes and moves slowly; sodium reacts faster and can melt into a ball; potassium reacts more vigorously and may burn with a lilac flame. These are teacher demonstrations, not home experiments.
  • With oxygen they form oxides; with chlorine they form white metal chlorides. For a specified simple oxide: 4Li + O₂ → 2Li₂O. With chlorine: 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl.
  • Reactivity increases down Group 1: more shells mean greater distance and shielding, so the attraction between nucleus and outer electron is weaker. The electron is lost more easily.

Group 7: halogens

Halogens have seven outer electrons and form −1 ions when they gain one electron. Their molecules are diatomic: F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂.

Halogen at room temperatureAppearance
ChlorineGreen gas
BromineRed-brown liquid
IodineGrey-black solid; purple vapour when heated
  • Melting and boiling points increase down the group.
  • Reactivity decreases down Group 7: more shells and shielding weaken attraction for an incoming electron.
  • With metals, halogens form ionic metal halides. With non-metals they generally form covalent compounds, such as hydrogen chloride.
  • A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from its aqueous halide solution.
  • Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂. Chlorine displaces bromine; iodine cannot displace chlorine from chloride solution.
  • Ionic equation: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂. Potassium ions are unchanged spectator ions.

Transition metals

Examples include Cr chromium, Mn manganese, Fe iron, Co cobalt, Ni nickel and Cu copper.

Compared with Group 1 metals, they are generally harder, stronger and denser, have higher melting points, and react less vigorously with oxygen, water and halogens. Iron reacts with oxygen slowly when rusting; copper does not react with cold water.

Many transition metals:

  • Form ions with different charges, such as Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺.
  • Form coloured compounds, such as blue copper(II) sulfate and green iron(II) compounds.
  • Act as catalysts, including iron in ammonia manufacture and nickel in hydrogenation.

These are general trends, not rules without exceptions. Use the particular element and evidence in the question.
