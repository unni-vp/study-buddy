Structures & materials

Polymers and giant structures

Polymers are very large molecules made from repeating units. Their atoms are joined by strong covalent bonds. They have relatively strong forces between the large molecules, so they are usually solids at room temperature.

  • A repeating unit in brackets with n outside represents a long chain; bonds through the brackets show the chain continues.
  • A polymer molecule is not the same as a giant covalent network. Individual polymer chains can be separated from other chains by overcoming intermolecular forces.
  • Silicon dioxide is a giant covalent network, not separate SiO₂ molecules. Strong bonds throughout the structure give it a very high melting point.
  • Dot-and-cross models show electron sharing but not realistic scale or 3D shape. Ball-and-stick models show connections and shape, but atoms are not coloured balls joined by literal sticks. A small section of a giant structure cannot show its full extent.

Carbon structures

The same element can form very different materials because its atoms join in different ways.

StructureArrangementProperties explained
DiamondEach carbon forms four covalent bonds in a giant 3D networkVery hard and very high melting point; many strong bonds. No delocalised electrons, so no electrical conduction
GraphiteEach carbon forms three bonds in hexagonal layers; one electron per atom is delocalisedConducts electricity. Layers slide because only weak forces act between them, making graphite soft/slippery. Strong bonds within layers give high melting point
GrapheneOne layer of graphite, one atom thickStrong, light and electrically conducting; useful in composites and electronics
FullerenesHollow carbon molecules with rings, mainly hexagons but sometimes pentagons/heptagonsC₆₀ is approximately spherical. Hollow shapes can carry other molecules; large surface area can be useful in catalysts or drug delivery
Carbon nanotubesCylindrical fullerenes; very large length-to-diameter ratioHigh tensile strength and electrical conductivity suit reinforcement and electronics

Graphite is not held together by weak covalent bonds. The covalent bonds within each layer are strong; forces between layers are much weaker. Fullerenes are molecules, whereas diamond is an extended network.

Metals and alloys

Metal atoms form a regular giant structure. Their outer electrons are delocalised, meaning they are free to move through the whole structure.

  • Strong electrostatic attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons holds the metal together.
  • Electrons carry electrical charge and transfer thermal energy, so metals conduct electricity and heat.
  • In a pure metal, regular layers can slide over one another while metallic bonding remains. This makes metals malleable and ductile.
  • An alloy contains a metal mixed with other elements. Different-sized atoms distort the regular layers, making them harder to slide, so alloys are usually harder.

Imagine stacking equal-sized marbles in neat rows, then replacing some with larger marbles: the rows no longer slide neatly. This models distortion, though real metal bonding involves charges and electrons.

For material choices, compare the properties needed for the job with cost, density, strength, conductivity and resistance to corrosion. The best conductor is not automatically the best material for a long overhead cable.

Nanoparticles

Nanoparticles are 1–100 nm in size. Their small size gives them a large surface area to volume ratio, so a large fraction of their atoms are at the surface.

  • Fine particles: about 100–2,500 nm in diameter.
  • Coarse particles/dust: about 2,500–10,000 nm.
  • 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m. A 20 nm particle is 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ m across, much larger than a typical atom but far smaller than dust.

Surface-area calculations

For a cube of side length l:

Surface area = 6l²; volume = l³; SA:V = 6/l.

A 10 nm cube has area 600 nm² and volume 1,000 nm³: ratio 0.6 nm⁻¹. A 1 nm cube has area 6 nm² and volume 1 nm³: ratio 6 nm⁻¹. Reducing the side by ten makes SA:V ten times greater, even though each cube's total area is smaller.

Uses and risks

Applications include medicine, electronics, cosmetics, sun creams, deodorants and catalysts. For the same effect, a smaller quantity of nanoparticle material may be needed because more surface is available.

Possible disadvantages depend on the material, dose and exposure: particles may enter the body or environment, and long-term effects may be uncertain. Evaluate supplied evidence rather than assuming all nanoparticles are harmful or all are safe. Compare benefit, exposure route, effectiveness, cost and evidence about toxicity.
