Language and structure

Analyse words and images

Start with the meaning, then identify a method if the term helps explain it.

ChoiceWhat to explore
VerbWhat kind of action, force or pace does it suggest?
AdjectiveWhat quality or judgement does it add?
Simile/metaphorWhat qualities are transferred between the things compared?
PersonificationWhat human action or intention is given to something non-human?
Sound or sensory detailWhat can be heard, seen or felt, and why does it matter here?
DialogueWhat does the wording reveal beyond the literal statement?

“The clock swallowed another minute.” Personification turns time into something consuming a limited opportunity. Swallowed suggests an irreversible loss, reflecting Ada's sense that her decision is getting closer. The clock is not merely telling us the time.

“A small, crooked bridge.” The simile connects the stitch on the glove to a bridge. The repaired object becomes an image of a relationship that is imperfect but still capable of connection. Explain that link rather than simply identifying a simile.

Analyse structure and viewpoint

Structure means how events and information are arranged. Follow the passage's movement, then explain why the order matters.

  • Opening focus: the nearly empty station isolates Ada and makes small sounds noticeable.
  • Delayed information: footsteps arrive before their owner's purpose is revealed, sharing Ada's fear with the reader.
  • Turning point: the parcel contains gloves, overturning the expectation of a confrontation.
  • Ending: the raised hand and sketchbook develop a possibility of connection rather than erasing every disagreement.

The third-person narration stays close to Ada's perceptions. We know her expectations before we know the father's intentions. Do not claim that every third-person narrator reveals everyone's thoughts.

Sentence length can affect pace, but explain the particular use. “She turned.” isolates the action at the moment of uncertainty. Longer descriptive sentences earlier slow our progress and keep us waiting with her. There is no rule that every short sentence creates tension.

Build an analytical paragraph

Use a flexible thinking route: point → evidence → explain the choice → connect to the question.

The father's arrival initially seems threatening. His footsteps are “hard, quick beats”, making the sound abrupt and forceful. Because Ada recognises the rhythm before seeing him, the writer keeps us within her anxious viewpoint. Her thought that there is “nowhere ... to hide” makes a familiar parent seem like an approaching danger, increasing uncertainty about whether she will be allowed to leave.

The paragraph explains a specific effect from specific evidence. A technical label is useful only if it helps explain the words or their arrangement. Do not attach a fixed number of paragraphs to every mark value.
