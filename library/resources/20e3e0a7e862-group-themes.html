Themes and dramatic methods

Responsibility and social inequality

Responsibility: the Inspector links decisions made at different times. Sheila and Eric accept the moral lesson; others focus on avoiding consequences. The final call forces the issue back into view.

Class and power: Arthur controls employment; Sheila influences a shop; Sybil controls charitable help. Their different kinds of power all affect Eva's options. Contrast their secure dining room with the precarious life described outside it.

Money and individualism: Arthur treats success as personal achievement. The investigation reveals that his choices have effects beyond his family. Gerald's financial help is valuable, but also gives him power in the relationship.

Use a specific action to explain each theme. Do not replace the play with a general speech about rich and poor people.

Gender, generations and appearance

Gender: Eva's low-paid work and dependence on more powerful men make her vulnerable. Sheila's initial focus on the engagement changes as she challenges male accounts. Do not claim that every woman responds in the same way: Sybil defends the hierarchy.

Generations: Sheila and Eric become more willing to accept responsibility than their parents. The younger characters still begin with serious faults. Gerald's position complicates a simple young-good, old-bad reading.

Appearance and reality: respectable roles conceal selfishness, betrayal and coercion. The important contrast is between the family's reputation and its behaviour.

Guilt and change: feeling ashamed is only a start. The ending tests whether a lesson changes somebody's values when a legal penalty seems avoidable.

Dramatic methods and context

  • Stage directions and lighting: the opening is “pink” and “intimate”. The lighting becomes harsher when the Inspector arrives. The visual change suggests comfortable appearances being exposed.
  • Single setting: the dining room keeps the characters together, like a pressure cooker. Eva's story enters this protected space through the investigation.
  • Entrances and exits: arrivals interrupt speeches and alter who controls a scene.
  • Cliff-hangers: the breaks after Gerald's recognition and before Eric's confession encourage the audience to anticipate revelations.
  • Dramatic irony: Arthur's predictions and Sybil's condemnation reveal limits to their judgement.
  • Detective form: evidence is disclosed gradually, but the central mystery becomes a moral one.
  • Morality-play features: the visitor confronts wrongdoing and offers a lesson. This is an interpretation of the form, not proof that Goole is a ghost.

The 1912 setting foregrounds class divisions, limited opportunities and complacency before major conflict. Writing after two world wars gave Priestley a perspective from which to challenge that confidence. Use context to illuminate a particular choice, rather than adding unrelated history.
