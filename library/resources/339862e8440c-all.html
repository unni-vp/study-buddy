Organic chemistry

Carbon compounds and chemical families

Carbon atoms can join into chains and rings. A homologous series is a family of compounds with a general formula and similar chemical reactions. Neighbouring members differ by CH₂.

A functional group is the part of a molecule that largely determines its reactions. Think of tools with the same working end but different handle lengths. Alcohols share the –OH functional group, while alkenes share C=C.

A hydrocarbon contains carbon and hydrogen only. Alcohols and carboxylic acids also contain oxygen. They are organic compounds, but they are not hydrocarbons.

A molecular formula counts atoms. A displayed structural formula shows every atom and bond. Check each atom's bonds: carbon forms four, oxygen two and hydrogen one.

Recognise the working part of a molecule

FamilyRecognising featureUseful reaction
AlkaneIt is a hydrocarbon with only single carbon–carbon bonds.Complete combustion forms carbon dioxide and water.
AlkeneIt contains a carbon–carbon double bond, C=C.Addition opens that double bond; bromine water is decolourised.
AlcoholAn –OH group is attached to carbon.Suitable primary alcohols can be oxidised to carboxylic acids.
Carboxylic acidIt contains –COOH, including a C=O and an O–H.It reacts with carbonates to produce carbon dioxide.
EsterIt contains a –C(=O)–O– link between carbon groups.It can be formed from a carboxylic acid and an alcohol.

Look for the complete group, not one isolated atom or bond. A carboxylic acid contains O–H, but the attached C=O makes it a different family from an ordinary alcohol.

Crude oil and alkanes

Crude oil is a finite mixture, mainly hydrocarbons, formed from ancient biomass such as plankton buried in mud over millions of years. It is non-renewable on human timescales. Much of it consists of alkanes, saturated hydrocarbons with only single carbon–carbon bonds.

Alkane formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. The first four are:

NameMolecular formulaCondensed structure
MethaneCH₄CH₄
EthaneC₂H₆CH₃CH₃
PropaneC₃H₈CH₃CH₂CH₃
ButaneC₄H₁₀CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃

“Saturated” means the chain has no carbon–carbon double bond and has the maximum hydrogen content for that acyclic chain. Formula recognition should use the supplied structure where available, because the same molecular formula can sometimes fit other types of compound.

Fractional distillation and uses

Fractional distillation separates a mixture; it does not break molecules or make new substances.

  1. Heat crude oil so many hydrocarbons evaporate.
  2. Vapours enter a column that is hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top.
  3. Different boiling ranges mean substances condense at different heights.
  4. Long-chain, high-boiling fractions collect lower down. Short-chain, low-boiling fractions rise further and collect higher up; some remain gases.

A fraction is a mixture of molecules with similar carbon-chain lengths/boiling ranges, not necessarily a single pure compound. Fractions supply fuels such as petrol, diesel, kerosene, heavy fuel oil and LPG. Petrochemical feedstocks produce solvents, lubricants, polymers and detergents.

As molecular size increases, boiling point and viscosity increase, while flammability decreases. Longer molecules have stronger intermolecular attractions and are harder to separate; covalent bonds within the molecules are not broken by boiling. Viscous liquids resist flowing more.

Complete combustion and balancing

With sufficient oxygen, hydrocarbons burn to carbon dioxide and water, releasing energy. Carbon and hydrogen are oxidised.

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Balance carbon first, hydrogen second, oxygen last. Change coefficients, never subscripts. For ethane, start C₂H₆ + 3½O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O, then double all coefficients:

2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O.

Insufficient oxygen can produce carbon monoxide and soot through incomplete combustion. Alkenes often burn with smokier flames in air, but complete combustion still produces carbon dioxide and water when oxygen is sufficient.

Cracking and demand

Cracking breaks large hydrocarbons into smaller molecules, usually including alkanes and alkenes. It is a chemical change, unlike distillation.

  • Catalytic cracking: vaporise the hydrocarbon and pass vapour over a hot catalyst.
  • Steam cracking: mix hydrocarbon vapour with steam and heat strongly to a high temperature.
  • Products supply high-demand smaller fuels and reactive alkenes for polymers/other chemicals.

Example: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄. Count each element on both sides. Cracking does not require one unique product pair; use the given formulas and conservation of atoms.

Work out a missing cracking product

Suppose C₁₂H₂₆ → C₈H₁₈ + unknown.

  1. Carbon remaining = 12 − 8 = 4 atoms.
  2. Hydrogen remaining = 26 − 18 = 8 atoms.
  3. The missing product is C₄H₈, consistent with an alkene in this context.

No atoms disappear. A cracking reaction can form different combinations, so the known product and atom balance determine what can be inferred.

Alkenes and their displayed structures

An alkene contains C=C, its functional group. It is unsaturated. For the acyclic single-double-bond series, CₙH₂ₙ, starting at two carbons:

NameFormulaStraight-chain example
EtheneC₂H₄CH₂=CH₂
PropeneC₃H₆CH₂=CHCH₃
ButeneC₄H₈CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃
PenteneC₅H₁₀CH₂=CHCH₂CH₂CH₃

The diagram uses but-1-ene and pent-1-ene as the examples; the double bond can occur elsewhere in longer chains. A double bond counts as two bonds when checking carbon’s total of four.

Addition reactions and the bromine test

Addition opens the C=C into a single C–C bond, attaching new atoms to its two carbons.

Added substanceMain conditionsProduct type
HydrogenHeated with a nickel catalystAlkane
Water/steamElevated temperature and pressure, suitable acid catalystAlcohol
Chlorine, bromine or iodineSuitable halogen contact, usually without a catalyst for a simple additionHalogenoalkane with a halogen on each former double-bond carbon

Adding hydrogen to ethene forms ethane. The C=C becomes C–C, and one hydrogen attaches to each carbon.

Adding steam to ethene forms ethanol. The double bond opens, with H added to one carbon and –OH to the other.

Adding bromine to ethene forms CH₂BrCH₂Br. One bromine attaches to each former double-bond carbon. Chlorine and iodine give corresponding additions in a structural model.

For a larger molecule, preserve the rest of the chain and add across the actual double bond. An unsymmetrical molecule can give more than one position product. Follow the supplied structure and conditions rather than assuming that an unspecified product must be unique.

For the alkene test, shake with bromine water: orange becomes colourless. An alkane does not decolourise it under this test’s ordinary conditions. This test supports the presence of unsaturation; do not treat it as identifying the exact alkene name.

Alcohols: structures, reactions and uses

Alcohols contain –OH attached to carbon.

AlcoholFormula
MethanolCH₃OH
EthanolC₂H₅OH
PropanolC₃H₇OH
ButanolC₄H₉OH

Straight-chain examples end in –CH₂OH. Keep the oxygen–hydrogen bond visible when displaying them.

  • With sodium, alcohols produce hydrogen and the corresponding sodium compound.
  • They burn as fuels, with complete combustion producing carbon dioxide and water.
  • These small alcohols dissolve in water to differing extents, forming neutral solutions; solubility generally falls as the hydrocarbon part gets longer.
  • With a suitable oxidising agent, primary examples can form carboxylic acids, such as ethanol to ethanoic acid.
  • Uses include fuels, solvents and ethanol-containing alcoholic drinks. Not every alcohol is safe to drink: methanol is toxic.

Ethanol combustion: C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O. Include the oxygen already present in ethanol when balancing. You need to describe the other reactions accurately without memorising extra symbol equations for every member.

Making ethanol and comparing methods

Fermentation: yeast enzymes convert sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide in warm, anaerobic conditions, commonly around 30°C. Too-cold conditions slow enzyme activity; too-hot conditions kill yeast/denature enzymes. Oxygen exclusion favours fermentation rather than aerobic respiration.

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂

Fermentation produces a dilute aqueous solution, so distillation is needed for more concentrated ethanol. It can use renewable crop material, but needs land, growing time and energy, and can compete with food production.

Hydration of ethene reacts ethene with steam using a suitable acid catalyst, high temperature and pressure. It can operate continuously and make a more concentrated product rapidly, but usually uses a finite petroleum feedstock and energy-intensive conditions. Compare renewable input, speed, purity/separation, land use, yield and energy with supplied evidence.

Renewable feedstock does not mean zero impact

Fermentation can use sugars from crops that can be regrown. Growing, harvesting, transporting and distilling them still require land and energy. Clearing habitats for crop land can add further impacts. The comparison with hydration therefore needs the whole production route, not just whether the original feedstock is renewable.

Fermentation is usually a batch process and the ethanol must be separated from water. Hydration can operate continuously, but uses high-temperature, high-pressure equipment and a suitable catalyst. Compare conditions and separation needs alongside yield.

Carboxylic acids and weak acidity

Carboxylic acids contain –COOH: carbon is double-bonded to one oxygen and single-bonded to an –OH group. The first four are:

NameCondensed formula
Methanoic acidHCOOH
Ethanoic acidCH₃COOH
Propanoic acidC₂H₅COOH
Butanoic acidC₃H₇COOH

They dissolve in water to form acidic solutions and react with carbonates to produce a salt, water and carbon dioxide. For example ethanoic acid with sodium carbonate produces sodium ethanoate.

Carboxylic acids are weak acids, meaning partial ionisation in water. At the same concentration a weak acid has fewer hydrogen ions and a higher pH than a strong acid. “Weak” refers to degree of ionisation; “dilute” refers to amount per volume. A concentrated weak acid is possible.

Esters and condensation

A carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol to make an ester and water, commonly with an acid catalyst. Ethanoic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl ethanoate + water. This is a condensation reaction because a small molecule is eliminated.

Esters often have characteristic fruity smells and are useful in perfumes/flavourings and solvents. Ethyl ethanoate is the named ester to remember. Its ester link is –C(=O)–O–, distinct from an alcohol’s –OH and an acid’s –COOH. Do not confuse a product’s name with the reactant alcohol name.

Work out where an ester name comes from

For ethyl ethanoate, the ethyl part comes from ethanol and the ethanoate part comes from ethanoic acid. The reaction also forms water. Do not confuse ethanoic acid with ethanol just because both names refer to two carbon atoms.

The ester link is –C(=O)–O–. The double-bonded oxygen stays attached to the acid-derived carbon, while the other oxygen connects to the alcohol-derived carbon group.

Addition polymers and repeat units

A monomer is a small molecule that joins many others.

A polymer is a very large molecule built from repeating units.

In addition polymerisation, alkene double bonds open and form a long carbon backbone.

To draw a repeat unit:

  1. Keep the two former double-bond carbons and all attached groups.
  2. Change C=C to C–C.
  3. Draw one outward chain bond from each end through the brackets.
  4. Put n outside the brackets, below the chain.

No small by-product forms, so the repeat unit contains the same atoms as the monomer. Poly(propene) retains the CH₃ side group: its unit is –CH₂–CH(CH₃)–. Reverse the steps to identify a monomer from a polymer: restore a double bond between the backbone carbons, retaining side groups. Do not strip off chlorine or methyl groups as if they disappeared.

Reverse a repeat unit without losing side groups

For the repeat unit –CH₂–CHCl–, identify the two carbon atoms in the backbone. Remove the outward chain bonds and restore a C=C between those carbons. Keep their attached H and Cl atoms. The monomer is CH₂=CHCl, not ethene: the chlorine is part of the original monomer.

The symbol n counts repeating units. It does not mean a new element, a fixed chain length or an extra atom.

Condensation polymers and polyesters

Condensation polymerisation uses monomers with two functional groups, enabling chains to extend at both ends. They join while losing small molecules, usually water.

  • A diol has two –OH groups.
  • A dicarboxylic acid has two –COOH groups.

Reaction between them makes a polyester containing repeated ester links, –C(=O)–O–.

In the named example, ethanediol and hexanedioic acid can join to make a polyester. Focus on functional groups and linking, rather than memorising additional organic names.

Compare the methods:

AdditionCondensation
Usually alkene monomers with C=CMonomers with two reactive functional groups
Double bond opensFunctional groups join
No small molecule eliminatedSmall molecule such as water eliminated
Same atoms in monomer and repeat unitSome atoms leave in the by-product

Monomers need two joining sites for an extended chain; a single alcohol reacting with a single carboxylic acid makes a small ester rather than a long polyester.

Amino acids, proteins and natural polymers

Amino acids contain both –NH₂ and –COOH functional groups. Glycine, H₂NCH₂COOH, is one example.

Amino acids join by condensation. An amino group reacts with a carboxyl group, forming a link and releasing water. Repeating this process produces a polypeptide.

Proteins contain chains of amino acids. The order of the amino acids affects how a chain folds, which helps determine the protein's function.

Natural polymerMonomer type
Protein/polypeptideAmino acids
StarchGlucose
CelluloseGlucose
DNANucleotides

DNA carries genetic instructions for development/functioning of living organisms and viruses. Most DNA consists of two polymer chains in a double helix, built from four types of nucleotide. A nucleotide is not an amino acid, and glucose is not the monomer of protein. Different arrangements can give starch and cellulose different properties despite both using glucose.

Common structure and reaction mistakes

  • Hydrocarbon means only C and H; do not include oxygen-containing families.
  • Distillation separates; cracking breaks molecules.
  • Boiling separates molecules by overcoming intermolecular forces, not C–C bonds.
  • Check carbon’s four bonds and preserve every atom in addition products.
  • Alkenes start with ethene, not a one-carbon “methene”.
  • Show –OH, –COOH and ester links distinctly.
  • Addition polymer units have single backbone bonds, brackets, outward links and n.
  • Weak acid does not mean dilute acid.
