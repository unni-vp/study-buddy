Read and use evidence

Read the question and its range

Fiction reading means working out what happens, what it suggests and how the writing shapes your response. Treat the passage like a film scene: notice the action, the viewpoint and where your attention is directed.

Read the question before choosing evidence. Identify its focus, command and line range. A point from outside the requested range may be interesting but may not answer that question.

The reading section uses one unseen twentieth-century prose extract. It rewards accurate retrieval, supported interpretation, analysis and evaluation. It is not an essay on the author's biography or the history of the novel.

Retrieve facts and make inferences

Explicit information is stated directly. If the clock shows ten minutes to seven, the time is 6:50. For a list question, give distinct facts rather than five versions of the same point.

Inference means working out something suggested by the evidence. Ada repeatedly counts the benches and knows the timetable by heart: these details suggest anxiety and repeated preparation, rather than proving she has never used a train.

What the text saysSupported inferenceUnsupported leap
Her hand tremblesShe may be nervousShe has a particular illness
He will not meet her eyesHe seems uncomfortableHe definitely hates her
The door is lockedAccess is restrictedSomebody has committed a crime

Use suggests, implies, seems when the detail allows more than one interpretation. An inference needs a reason, not just a confident label.

Select evidence that does a job

A quotation is a piece of evidence, not the explanation itself. Choose a short phrase whose words you can discuss.

Ada grips the portfolio until the string marks her hand. The physical pressure suggests that she is using it to steady herself and hold on to her decision. Her art matters enough for her to endure discomfort.

You can also refer accurately to an action or sequence without quoting every sentence. Avoid copying a large chunk and then adding only “this makes the reader interested”.

For impressions, name the quality and support it: the father initially seems intimidating because Ada recognises his heavy approach and thinks there is nowhere to hide. Later evidence complicates this impression.
