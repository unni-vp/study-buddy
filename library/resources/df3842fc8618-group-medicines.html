Medicines & drug development

Antibiotics and other medicines

Antibiotics treat bacterial diseases by killing bacteria or preventing their growth. Different bacteria may need different antibiotics, so the treatment must match the infection.

Viruses are different: antibiotics do not kill them. Viruses reproduce inside body cells, making it difficult to target the virus without also damaging the body's own tissues.

Painkillers relieve symptoms such as pain. They do not kill the pathogen causing the infection. A person may therefore feel better while still carrying and spreading a pathogen.

Antibiotic resistance: resistant bacteria survive an antibiotic that previously worked. This makes infections harder to treat. Developing new treatments also takes time and money.

The key distinction is treating a cause versus relieving a symptom. An appropriate antibiotic targets a bacterial cause. A painkiller targets a symptom.

How resistance spreads through a population

  1. Bacteria vary. A random mutation may make a bacterium resistant before treatment begins.
  2. An antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria or prevents them reproducing.
  3. Resistant bacteria are more likely to survive the treatment and reproduce.
  4. Their offspring inherit resistance, increasing the resistant proportion of the population.

The antibiotic selects resistant bacteria; it does not make each bacterium deliberately adapt. It is the bacteria, rather than the patient's body, that become resistant. Avoiding unnecessary antibiotic use reduces this selection pressure. Follow the prescribed treatment instructions rather than treating a viral infection with an antibiotic.

Developing new drugs

Where drugs come from

  • Digitalis: a heart drug originally obtained from foxgloves.
  • Aspirin: developed from chemicals found in willow.
  • Penicillin: discovered by Alexander Fleming from the Penicillium mould.
  • Most new drugs are now synthesised by chemists. A natural substance may still provide the starting point.

Testing sequence

A promising chemical must pass several checks, like a new car being tested before passengers ride in it.

StageWhat happens and why
Preclinical testingTest on cells, tissues and live animals to investigate effects and possible harm.
Early clinical trialsGive very low doses to healthy volunteers to investigate safety.
Further clinical trialsTest on patients to see how well the treatment works and find the optimum dose.
ReviewOther scientists examine the methods and results through peer review before publication.

The three key checks are toxicity (harmful effects), efficacy (whether it works) and dose (how much gives a useful effect with acceptable risk).

Placebo control: a placebo resembles the treatment but has no active drug. Comparing it with the drug helps separate the drug's effect from expectations and other changes. Where appropriate, a trial may instead compare a new drug with an existing treatment.

Double blinding: neither patients nor the researchers assessing them know who receives the drug until the results are analysed. This reduces the chance that expectations influence reporting or assessment.

Fair comparison: random allocation helps distribute other patient differences between groups. A large sample and comparable groups also strengthen the evidence. Compare recovery rates and side effects, rather than raw numbers alone when group sizes differ.

Example: comparing a drug with a placebo

GroupPatients who improvePercentage
Drug60 out of 10060%
Placebo35 out of 10035%

Difference = 60 − 35 = 25 percentage points.

Consider sample size, side effects and whether the groups were comparable before judging the drug.

What makes trial evidence convincing?

Random allocation and a large sample reduce the chance that differences such as age or severity explain the result. Blinding addresses a different problem: knowledge of the treatment might influence a patient's reporting or a researcher's judgement.

A placebo can still be associated with improvement because of expectations, recovery over time or other care. “No active drug” does not mean no participant can improve. Compare improvements and adverse effects in both groups, using percentages when the group sizes differ.

Peer review checks the reasoning, methods and analysis. It does not mean another scientist automatically repeats every trial or proves that a treatment has no risk.
