Environmental threats to our planet questions & answers

1 mark2 marks3 marks4 marks6 marks

1 mark

1. What is climate change?

Answer:

A long-term change in average climate conditions.

2. Name one source of evidence for past climate.

Answer:

Ice cores.

3. Name the greenhouse gas released in large quantities by burning fossil fuels.

Answer:

Carbon dioxide.

4. What sea temperature is normally needed for a tropical storm to form?

Answer:

About 27°C or above.

5. What is agricultural drought?

Answer:

A shortage of soil moisture for crops and grazing.

6. Name the ocean-atmosphere event linked to Australia's 2002–03 drought.

Answer:

El Niño.

2 marks

7. Explain how ice cores provide climate evidence.

Answer:
  • Trapped air bubbles preserve past greenhouse-gas concentrations.
  • Oxygen isotopes in the ice indicate past temperature.

8. Give two human causes of increased greenhouse-gas emissions.

Answer:
  • Burning fossil fuels.
  • Deforestation, farming, landfill or fertiliser use.

9. Explain why tropical storms weaken over land.

Answer:
  • The storm loses its supply of warm ocean water and moisture.
  • Friction over land also disrupts its rotating winds.

10. Give two effects of climate change on people.

Answer:
  • Greater heat-related illness or water shortage.
  • Damage to homes, food supply or infrastructure from extremes and sea-level rise.

11. Give two effects of Australia's 2002–2009 drought.

Answer:
  • Farm production and income fell.
  • Reservoirs and river flows declined, or bushfire risk increased.

12. Give two Australian adaptations to drought.

Answer:
  • Water restrictions or recycling.
  • Desalination, efficient irrigation, water trading or changing farm practice.

3 marks

13. Explain how volcanic eruptions can cause short-term cooling.

Answer:
  • Large eruptions send ash and sulphur aerosols high into the atmosphere.
  • These reflect part of the Sun's energy back to space.
  • Less solar energy reaches the surface, causing temporary cooling.

14. Explain the enhanced greenhouse effect.

Answer:
  • Earth gives out long-wave heat radiation.
  • Extra greenhouse gases absorb and re-radiate more of this heat.
  • Less energy escapes to space, so average temperature rises.

15. Explain why many hot deserts occur around 30° north and south.

Answer:
  • Air that rose near the Equator moves poleward and cools.
  • It sinks around 30°, creating high pressure.
  • Sinking air prevents clouds and rainfall, producing dry conditions.

16. Explain how El Niño can contribute to Australian drought.

Answer:
  • Warm Pacific water and rising air shift eastwards.
  • Rainfall is displaced away from the western Pacific.
  • Eastern and northern Australia therefore face a higher chance of dry winter and spring conditions.

4 marks

17. Explain one strength and one limitation of tree rings as climate evidence.

Answer:
  • Strength: annual rings can be accurately dated and their width records favourable or difficult growing seasons.
  • Limitation: growth is affected by rainfall, disease and local conditions as well as temperature.

18. Explain two conditions needed for a tropical storm to develop.

Answer:
  • Sea temperature above about 27°C supplies rapid evaporation and latent heat when water condenses.
  • Low wind shear allows a tall rotating storm structure to build instead of being torn apart.

19. Explain two environmental effects of prolonged drought.

Answer:
  • Low river flow and shrinking wetlands destroy aquatic habitats and reduce water quality.
  • Dry vegetation and exposed soil increase wildfire risk and erosion.

20. Explain why Australia's Millennium Drought became severe even though not every year was an El Niño year.

Answer:
  • Repeated failures of cool-season rainfall produced a cumulative shortage in soil, rivers and reservoirs.
  • High temperatures and evaporation increased water loss, so occasional rain did not fully restore long-term stores.

6 marks

21. Assess the usefulness of different evidence for reconstructing past climate.

Answer:
  • Instrument records directly measure temperature and rainfall but cover only the recent past.
  • Ice cores extend much further back and preserve gases and temperature indicators, though they mainly come from polar or mountain sites.
  • Tree rings give annual detail but are influenced by rainfall, disease and local growing conditions.
  • Paintings and diaries add human detail but may be subjective and local.
  • The strongest reconstruction combines several independent sources that show the same trend.

22. Assess the causes of modern climate change.

Answer:
  • Orbital, solar and volcanic changes can alter climate naturally and explain many past variations.
  • Orbital cycles act too slowly to explain the rapid recent rise, while large eruptions usually cool climate temporarily.
  • Burning fossil fuels, deforestation and agriculture have rapidly increased carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide.
  • These gases strengthen the greenhouse effect by retaining more outgoing heat.
  • Natural factors still influence individual years, but human emissions are the main cause of current long-term warming.

23. Assess how successfully Australia adapted to the 2002–2009 drought.

Answer:
  • Restrictions reduced non-essential demand quickly, while recycling and conservation changed behaviour.
  • Desalination and linked supply systems made cities less dependent on rainfall.
  • Efficient irrigation, forecasting and changed farm practice improved resilience.
  • Water trading moved water towards higher-value uses, but smaller farmers could lose and ecosystems still needed flows.
  • Desalination was costly and energy-intensive, and no response could fully replace the missing rain.
  • Adaptation reduced vulnerability, but heavy La Niña rain in 2010–11, rather than management alone, ended the worst drought conditions.
