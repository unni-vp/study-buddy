River landscapes

River landscapes

The drainage basin and river profiles

A drainage basin is the land drained by a river and its tributaries. Its boundary is the watershed, often following high ground. A tributary joins another river at a confluence. The source is where the river begins; the mouth is where it enters the sea, a lake or another river.

The long profile shows height along the river from source to mouth. A cross-profile is a slice across the channel or valley. Do not confuse a river's width with the width of its whole valley.

FeatureTypical upper courseTypical lower course
Gradient and valleyA steeper gradient and a narrow, steep-sided valley are common.A gentler gradient and broader valley are common, although resistant rock can interrupt the pattern.
Channel and dischargeThe channel is usually narrower and shallower, carrying less water.Tributaries add water, so discharge, width and depth generally increase.
Average velocityRoughness and a shallow channel can make friction important.A larger, more efficient channel often has a higher average velocity despite a gentler slope.
ProcessesVertical erosion is important in cutting down into the bed.Lateral erosion, transport and deposition shape bends and floodplains. All can occur together.

These are general patterns, not a claim that every downstream site is deeper or faster than the one before it. Geology, obstacles, flow conditions and management cause local differences.

V-shaped valleys

  1. The river cuts down into its bed through vertical erosion, including hydraulic action and abrasion.
  2. Weathering breaks material from the valley sides.
  3. Gravity moves loosened material downslope.
  4. The river carries it away, allowing continued downcutting and leaving a narrow valley with a V-shaped cross-profile.

Waterfalls and gorges

Where resistant rock lies above less resistant rock, differential erosion can create a step:

  1. The less resistant rock erodes more rapidly. Hydraulic action and abrasion deepen a plunge pool below the fall.
  2. Erosion undercuts the resistant layer, leaving an unsupported overhang.
  3. Gravity causes the overhang to collapse. Fallen rock provides further material for abrasion.
  4. Repeated undercutting and collapse make the waterfall retreat upstream.
  5. The retreat can leave a narrow, steep-sided gorge.

This sequence explains one way to form a gorge. It does not mean every gorge was produced by waterfall retreat: the lower Wye also illustrates river incision into resistant rock.

Meanders and oxbow lakes

At a bend, faster flow against the outside bank promotes lateral erosion, including hydraulic action and abrasion. Undercutting creates a river cliff. Shallower, slower flow on the inside deposits material, building a slip-off slope. Erosion and deposition gradually move and enlarge the bend.

  1. Continued erosion narrows the neck between neighbouring bends.
  2. During high discharge, water can cut through the neck and take a straighter route.
  3. Flow through the old loop decreases. Deposition blocks its entrances.
  4. The isolated former bend becomes an oxbow lake.

Floodplains and natural levees

A floodplain is the broad, relatively flat land beside a river. Lateral erosion and migrating meanders widen the valley floor. When the river overflows, water spreads across this land, slows and deposits alluvium. Repeated floods build layers of fertile sediment.

The abrupt loss of velocity beside the channel causes coarser material to settle first. Finer particles travel farther across the floodplain. Repeated near-channel deposition can build raised banks called natural levees. These are different from artificial embankments built by people.
