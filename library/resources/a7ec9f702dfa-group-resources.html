Food, water & energy

Food, water and energy

Food production

Mechanisation means using machinery for tasks such as ploughing, sowing and harvesting. It increases the area that can be worked and reduces labour needed per unit of output. Larger fields make machinery easier to use, but removing hedgerows reduces habitats and wildlife connections.

Intensive farming raises output through inputs such as fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation. Fertilisers supply nutrients, but runoff can enrich rivers, encouraging algal growth and oxygen depletion. Pesticides can affect non-target organisms. Heavy machinery may compact soil, reducing infiltration and increasing runoff.

Commercial fishing uses boats and equipment to catch fish for sale. Powerful vessels, sonar and large nets improve catches, but taking fish faster than stocks reproduce causes overfishing. Some methods also damage the seabed or catch unwanted species. Catch limits, suitable mesh sizes, protected areas and monitoring can help stocks recover, although restrictions affect fishers' short-term income.

Water supply

UK rainfall and demand do not match neatly. Wetter northern and western uplands can have a surplus, while some more densely populated southern and eastern areas face pressure on supplies, especially during dry periods. A wet national climate does not guarantee that every place has enough usable water at the right time.

StrategyHow it helpsEnvironmental and practical trade-off
ReservoirA dam stores water during wetter periods so it can be released when needed.Flooding the valley changes habitats and may displace land uses. The dam also alters downstream flow and sediment movement.
Water transferPipelines, canals or connected rivers move water from a source area to an area of demand.Construction and pumping cost money and energy. Removing too much water can harm the source river and its users.
Leak reductionRepairing pipes makes more of the treated supply available to consumers.Finding and replacing damaged infrastructure can be expensive and disruptive.
Demand managementEfficient fittings, metering and suitable behaviour reduce the amount required.Savings depend on uptake, affordability and how people use the equipment.

These choices can be combined. Reducing unnecessary demand can delay the need for a new reservoir or reduce the amount transferred.

Energy choices

Renewable sources are naturally replenished, such as wind, solar, hydroelectric, tidal and biomass. Non-renewable sources are finite, such as coal, oil, natural gas and uranium.

The shares refer to UK primary energy consumption in 2025, covering transport, heating and energy used to generate electricity. Net electricity imports account for about 1.6%.

Renewable describes whether a resource is replenished. Low carbon describes its emissions. They are different tests: nuclear is low carbon during operation but its uranium fuel is non-renewable. Biomass regrowth can replace feedstock, but production and transport can still cause emissions.

Insulating a home reduces the energy it needs; building a power station increases supply. Comparing both avoids assuming that every future shortage must be solved by producing more energy.

Solar planning example: Hacheston, Suffolk: An appeal for a 25 MW solar park on about 50 hectares of agricultural land was dismissed in May 2014. Renewable electricity was a benefit, but landscape, heritage and the loss of productive farmland weighed against it. The example shows that a renewable source can still have local environmental costs.

Wind planning example: Kirkby Moor, Cumbria: A 2015 proposal would have replaced 12 turbines with six turbines up to 115 m high. Cumbria County Council objected because of landscape, visual and cumulative effects near sensitive habitats. Fewer turbines do not automatically mean a smaller visual impact if the replacements are much taller. This describes that proposal and objection, rather than the site's current planning status.

Changing UK energy supply

Since 1950, the UK's energy mix has shifted as coal declined, oil and gas expanded, nuclear developed and renewable electricity grew.

Changes are influenced by:

  • resource availability and price;
  • technology and infrastructure;
  • energy security;
  • climate targets and environmental rules;
  • government subsidies, planning and international agreements;
  • public acceptance.

Demand also changes with population, industry, transport, household technology and energy efficiency.

DUKES 2025 figures for 2024: renewables generated 50.4% of UK electricity, compared with 31.8% from fossil fuels. This is a snapshot of electricity generation, not all the energy used for heating, transport and industry.

The long-term pattern is a move away from coal and towards renewable electricity. Gas still matters because it can provide flexible generation when weather-dependent output is low.

Energy security and decision making

Energy security means having a dependable supply that meets needs at an affordable cost. Producing more energy within the UK can reduce reliance on imported fuels, but domestic production can still depend on weather, equipment, investment or imported components.

FactorHow it influences the UK's choices
EconomicsConstruction costs, fuel prices, maintenance and network upgrades affect affordability. A plant with a low running cost may still need expensive investment first.
PoliticsPlanning rules, support payments and agreements influence which projects are built. Producer decisions and conflict abroad can change imported fuel prices.
EnvironmentCarbon emissions, habitat change, visual effects and waste affect whether a project is acceptable. Benefits and costs occur at different scales.
ReliabilityA varied mix, stronger grid connections, storage and controllable output help meet demand when one source is unavailable.

Organisations such as OPEC, a group of oil-producing countries, can influence world supply through production decisions. International climate agreements encourage lower-carbon choices. These influences affect the UK even when the decisions are made elsewhere.

Fracking: obtaining gas changes the environment

Fracking, or hydraulic fracturing, releases gas trapped in shale. A well is drilled into the rock, and a mixture containing water and sand is pumped in at high pressure. The rock fractures, and sand helps keep openings available so gas can flow towards the well.

Potential benefits include an additional domestic gas supply, jobs and local contracts. Costs include water use, wastewater handling, heavy vehicle movements, noise and changes to the landscape. Poor control can create contamination risks, and operations can trigger small earthquakes. Burning the extracted gas releases carbon dioxide, while methane leakage adds to climate effects. An additional domestic fossil-fuel supply is therefore not the same as a renewable or zero-carbon supply.

Reduce demand as well as increasing supply

Energy efficiency provides a similar service using less energy: insulation reduces the energy needed to maintain a comfortable home temperature. Conservation reduces unnecessary use, such as turning off lighting in an empty room.

Local households, businesses and councils can improve buildings and travel choices. National decisions shape building standards, funding, large infrastructure and the energy mix. Upfront costs can prevent some households from making improvements, so the availability of support affects how fairly benefits are shared.
