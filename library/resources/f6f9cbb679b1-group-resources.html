Energy resources

Energy resources and decisions

A renewable resource is replenished as it is used. Fossil fuels and nuclear fuels are non-renewable: their usable supplies are finite.

ResourceUses and strengthsLimitations and impacts
Coal, oil and gasControllable heating and electricity; oil fuels transportCO₂ contributes to climate change; pollution; extraction damage; finite fuels
Nuclear fuelReliable large-scale electricity, low operational CO₂Radioactive waste, high building/decommissioning costs; finite fuel
BiofuelHeating, electricity or transport; can be regrownLand competes with food/habitats; burning produces CO₂; carbon balance depends on regrowth and processing
WindRenewable electricity without burning fuelVariable wind; visual/noise impacts; wildlife and site constraints
SunSolar electricity and water heatingDaylight/weather vary; large area may be needed
HydroelectricElectricity; stored water can respond quickly to demandReservoirs flood habitats/settlements; drought can reduce supply
TidesPredictable timing for electricityOutput is not continuous; high cost and estuary/ecosystem changes
WavesRenewable electricityVariable sea conditions; difficult maintenance; coastal impacts
GeothermalHeating and electricity; steady at suitable sitesSuitable sites limited; drilling costs

Reliable does not always mean renewable, and renewable does not mean impact-free. A mix of resources, storage and flexible generation can help match variable supply to demand.

When evaluating a choice, use the given data: output, reliability, cost, land use and emissions. State which factor matters most for the situation, then give a justified conclusion. Scientists can measure emissions and risks; policy also depends on economic, social, ethical and political decisions.

For trends, distinguish an increase in total energy from an increase in percentage share. New technology, cost, fuel availability and environmental policy can change the mix. Avoid inventing current percentages when the question supplies a graph.
