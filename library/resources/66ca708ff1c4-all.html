20th-century fiction reading

Read the question and its range

Fiction reading means working out what happens, what it suggests and how the writing shapes your response. Treat the passage like a film scene: notice the action, the viewpoint and where your attention is directed.

Read the question before choosing evidence. Identify its focus, command and line range. A point from outside the requested range may be interesting but may not answer that question.

The reading section uses one unseen twentieth-century prose extract. It rewards accurate retrieval, supported interpretation, analysis and evaluation. It is not an essay on the author's biography or the history of the novel.

Retrieve facts and make inferences

Explicit information is stated directly. If the clock shows ten minutes to seven, the time is 6:50. For a list question, give distinct facts rather than five versions of the same point.

Inference means working out something suggested by the evidence. Ada repeatedly counts the benches and knows the timetable by heart: these details suggest anxiety and repeated preparation, rather than proving she has never used a train.

Use suggests, implies, seems when the detail allows more than one interpretation. An inference needs a reason, not just a confident label.

Select evidence that does a job

A quotation is a piece of evidence, not the explanation itself. Choose a short phrase whose words you can discuss.

Ada grips the portfolio until the string marks her hand. The physical pressure suggests that she is using it to steady herself and hold on to her decision. Her art matters enough for her to endure discomfort.

You can also refer accurately to an action or sequence without quoting every sentence. Avoid copying a large chunk and then adding only “this makes the reader interested”.

For impressions, name the quality and support it: the father initially seems intimidating because Ada recognises his heavy approach and thinks there is nowhere to hide. Later evidence complicates this impression.

Analyse words and images

Start with the meaning, then identify a method if the term helps explain it.

ChoiceWhat to explore
VerbWhat kind of action, force or pace does it suggest?
AdjectiveWhat quality or judgement does it add?
Simile/metaphorWhat qualities are transferred between the things compared?
PersonificationWhat human action or intention is given to something non-human?
Sound or sensory detailWhat can be heard, seen or felt, and why does it matter here?
DialogueWhat does the wording reveal beyond the literal statement?

“The clock swallowed another minute.” Personification turns time into something consuming a limited opportunity. Swallowed suggests an irreversible loss, reflecting Ada's sense that her decision is getting closer. The clock is not merely telling us the time.

“A small, crooked bridge.” The simile connects the stitch on the glove to a bridge. The repaired object becomes an image of a relationship that is imperfect but still capable of connection. Explain that link rather than simply identifying a simile.

Analyse structure and viewpoint

Structure means how events and information are arranged. Follow the passage's movement, then explain why the order matters.

  • Opening focus: the nearly empty station isolates Ada and makes small sounds noticeable.
  • Delayed information: footsteps arrive before their owner's purpose is revealed, sharing Ada's fear with the reader.
  • Turning point: the parcel contains gloves, overturning the expectation of a confrontation.
  • Ending: the raised hand and sketchbook develop a possibility of connection rather than erasing every disagreement.

The third-person narration stays close to Ada's perceptions. We know her expectations before we know the father's intentions. Do not claim that every third-person narrator reveals everyone's thoughts.

Sentence length can affect pace, but explain the particular use. “She turned.” isolates the action at the moment of uncertainty. Longer descriptive sentences earlier slow our progress and keep us waiting with her. There is no rule that every short sentence creates tension.

Build an analytical paragraph

Use a flexible thinking route: point → evidence → explain the choice → connect to the question.

The father's arrival initially seems threatening. His footsteps are “hard, quick beats”, making the sound abrupt and forceful. Because Ada recognises the rhythm before seeing him, the writer keeps us within her anxious viewpoint. Her thought that there is “nowhere ... to hide” makes a familiar parent seem like an approaching danger, increasing uncertainty about whether she will be allowed to leave.

The paragraph explains a specific effect from specific evidence. A technical label is useful only if it helps explain the words or their arrangement. Do not attach a fixed number of paragraphs to every mark value.

Evaluate a statement

Evaluation means making a judgement about an interpretation and supporting it. A question asking “To what extent do you agree?” needs a view about the text, not just whether you personally like the character.

For “Ada is simply selfish”, consider her secret departure and her guilt, preparation and later response. A nuanced judgement might be: she makes a choice that can hurt her father, but the passage presents her as conflicted rather than uncaring.

Choose evidence that supports your judgement and evidence that qualifies it, when the text warrants that. Do not invent a disagreement just to sound balanced. Explain how the writer encourages your response.

Cover the passage and manage time

Give a longer question enough range: follow important developments across its specified section rather than analysing only the first sentence. Keep returning to the question's focus.

The sample paper recommends roughly an hour for reading and its questions, leaving about 45 minutes for creative prose writing. Use the marks and the actual instructions to control your time. A five-mark retrieval task does not need a long introduction or conclusion.

Common mistakes

  • Retelling events when asked how the writing works.
  • Using evidence from outside the specified range.
  • Identifying a method without explaining its meaning or effect.
  • Giving an interpretation that the words do not support.
  • Claiming all readers must feel exactly the same thing.
  • Importing another board's fixed question numbers, marks or paragraph formulas.
  • Writing about structure as a disconnected checklist rather than changes in this passage.
