Unseen poetry questions & answers

1 mark

1. What is the speaker in a poem?

Answer:

The voice presenting the poem.

2. What is a metaphor?

Answer:

A comparison that presents one thing as another.

3. What is a simile?

Answer:

A comparison using like or as.

4. What is personification?

Answer:

Giving human qualities to something non-human.

5. What is enjambment?

Answer:

A sentence continuing across a line ending.

6. What is a caesura?

Answer:

A pause within a line.

7. What is end-stopping?

Answer:

A line ending with punctuation that closes or pauses the sense.

8. What is a volta?

Answer:

A turn in thought or attitude.

9. What is a semantic field?

Answer:

A group of words related in meaning.

10. How many lines does a sonnet normally have?

Answer:

Fourteen.

11. What is free verse?

Answer:

Verse without a fixed regular metre or rhyme pattern.

12. Which unseen-poetry task is worth more marks: the single-poem response or the comparison?

Answer:

The comparison.

2 marks

13. Explain why the speaker should not automatically be identified as the poet.

Answer:
  • A poet can invent a voice or situation.
  • Claims about the speaker's experience therefore do not prove facts about the poet's life.

14. Explain the difference between literal and deeper meaning.

Answer:
  • Literal meaning concerns what is directly described.
  • Deeper meaning concerns the ideas or implications suggested by those details.

15. Give two useful questions to ask on the first reading.

Answer:
  • Who is speaking, and what is happening?
  • What attitude or feeling is expressed, and does it change?

16. Explain how a stanza break could contribute to meaning.

Answer:
  • It can separate stages such as different times, settings or attitudes.
  • Explain the actual change across that break rather than assuming every break has the same effect.

17. Explain why identifying alliteration alone is not a developed analysis.

Answer:
  • Naming the sound pattern identifies a method.
  • Analysis must explain how the particular sounds or words support meaning or atmosphere in that poem.

18. Explain why a poem's ending is useful evidence.

Answer:
  • It may resolve, reverse or complicate the opening idea.
  • Comparing it with earlier details reveals the poem's overall development.

19. Explain how an unfamiliar word can be approached without guessing a whole interpretation.

Answer:
  • Use the surrounding sentence and related images to infer a plausible meaning.
  • Check the interpretation against other evidence instead of relying entirely on that one word.

20. Explain how a title can affect a reader's interpretation.

Answer:
  • It can frame the subject or introduce an expectation.
  • Reading the whole poem may reveal a contrast or change in what the title means.

21. Explain a useful personal response to a poem.

Answer:
  • Offer an interpretation of its meaning or effect.
  • Support it with a precise detail, rather than saying only that you like or dislike it.

22. Give two ways to sustain comparison.

Answer:
  • Organise paragraphs around shared ideas, with evidence from both poems.
  • Explain relevant differences as well as similarities in meaning and methods.

4 marks

23. In Waiting, the speaker lays a second place and then puts it away. Analyse this action.

Answer:

The extra place makes the absent person visible through a familiar domestic object. Preparing it suggests hope or an established habit, but removing it immediately introduces doubt. The contrast between the two actions expresses hesitation without directly stating an emotion. It helps make loneliness an experience of repeated preparation and disappointment.

24. Analyse the final statements from Waiting: The room stays ready. No one arrives.

Answer:

The two short statements place hopeful preparation directly beside absence. The full stop creates a pause between what the speaker provides and what actually happens. Their contrast leaves the waiting unresolved, so the ending offers neither reunion nor clear emotional release. The reader can therefore recognise the continuing cost of hope.

25. Analyse the image from Waiting: their windows carry other lives.

Answer:

The metaphor makes bus windows glimpses of people and experiences elsewhere. Movement outside contrasts with the speaker's stationary waiting inside. Other lives seem available to see but difficult to share, supporting the speaker's isolation. The image therefore turns an ordinary street into a reminder of connection beyond reach.

26. Analyse the opening of Departure, where rinsing a cup and turning a key lead into an opening morning.

Answer:

The completed domestic actions suggest a deliberate departure rather than unfinished waiting. The morning that opens gives the street a welcoming quality through personification. This movement from enclosure to possibility supports a reading of leaving as chosen freedom. The practical verbs make the emotional change concrete before the final reflection confirms it.

27. Compare how the two poems use the house or room in their endings.

Answer:

In Waiting, a ready room remains empty, so contrast makes the home a place of unresolved attachment. In Departure, finding needed space makes the loss of familiar objects feel liberating. Both endings concern absence, but its value differs: one speaker keeps preparing for another person, while the other gains independence through leaving.

6 marks

28. Explain how to develop a paragraph about an image rather than merely identify it.

Answer:

Begin with a claim linked to the question, then choose short evidence supporting that claim. Examine the particular word or comparison and explain the qualities it gives to the subject. Consider what those qualities suggest about the speaker's attitude or the poem's ideas. Connect the image to a relevant change elsewhere if that develops the argument. For example, a warm cup in a poem about waiting can make attachment tangible; the empty place later complicates that comfort. The method's label should support this explanation rather than replace it.

29. Explain how to compare two poems that both discuss absence but express different attitudes.

Answer:

Identify a shared idea, then distinguish each speaker's response. Choose evidence from both poems showing those attitudes and explain the methods creating them. A useful paragraph might compare an unresolved ending with a chosen departure, showing why one absence feels painful and the other freeing. Follow with another connected comparison, such as their different uses of domestic objects or the outside world. Keep bringing both poems together instead of writing two separate accounts. The overall judgement should explain what the shared subject reveals through those differences.

30. Explain how language and structure can work together to create a change in attitude.

Answer:

Language can establish an initial mood through particular images or descriptions. A structural turn, such as a stanza break or a change of viewpoint, can then introduce a contrasting response. Analyse the evidence on both sides of that turn. For example, enclosed domestic images might give way to open street imagery as a speaker chooses to leave. The placement makes the changed vocabulary part of a developing argument, rather than a collection of disconnected techniques. Explain the particular shift instead of claiming that any stanza break automatically signals hope.

15 marks

31. Write about how Waiting presents the experience of absence.

Your cup still warms the kitchen shelf.
The clock repeats its patient beat.
I lay a second place at six,
then move it back beneath my seat.
Outside, the buses empty out;
their windows carry other lives.
I leave one small lamp by the door.
The room stays ready. No one arrives.
Answer:

Waiting presents absence as an unresolved attachment, expressed through ordinary routines rather than direct explanation. The speaker keeps preparing a domestic space for somebody who does not appear. This makes hope seem persistent but increasingly painful.

The opening cup is still warm, suggesting recent presence or a feeling that has not faded. The word “still” also connects the object with continuation. The clock's “patient beat” gives time a calm quality through personification, yet that calm contrasts with the uncertainty of waiting. The regular return of sound can make the speaker's lack of progress feel more noticeable.

Laying “a second place” turns the missing person into an empty position at a meal. The speaker then moves it away, so the paired actions reveal a conflict between expectation and doubt. The first stanza develops loneliness through this small reversal instead of stating that somebody is sad. Its rhyme supports a repeated routine, but does not make the situation happy.

The second stanza turns towards the street. Windows carrying “other lives” create a metaphor for connection outside the speaker's own room. Public movement contrasts with private stillness: the speaker can watch other people's lives continue while remaining emotionally fixed. The shift of setting therefore broadens the sense of exclusion.

The lamp by the door leaves welcome available, but the final two statements separate readiness from reality. “No one arrives” denies the outcome repeatedly prepared for, and its place at the end leaves no later resolution. The poem makes absence feel like continued waiting rather than accepted departure. Because the relationship is not explained, the reader can recognise the emotion without assuming a specific biography or cause.

32. Write about how Departure presents the experience of leaving.

I rinse the cup and turn the key.
The morning opens down the street.
A bus lifts sunlight from the road;
I choose the window for my seat.
The house grows smaller as we turn.
Its darkened panes no longer stare.
I carry nothing from the shelf,
but find the space I needed there.
Answer:

Departure presents leaving as a deliberate movement towards independence. The speaker begins with practical actions and ends by recognising an emotional benefit. The familiar house becomes smaller rather than remaining the centre of attention.

The opening verbs “rinse” and “turn” finish a routine and secure the house, making departure seem purposeful. The first-person voice gives the action personal ownership. The morning that “opens” then personifies the outside world as welcoming, so the transition beyond the door creates possibility rather than immediate threat.

The bus imagery extends that movement. Lifting “sunlight” gives the journey brightness, while “I choose” makes the speaker's agency explicit. The window is a chosen position from which to see the world, rather than a barrier keeping the speaker from it. The first stanza therefore combines practical movement with a hopeful attitude.

After the stanza break, attention returns to the house from increasing distance. The dark panes “no longer stare”, personifying the building as something that previously seemed watchful or oppressive. The change in scale reduces its hold on the speaker. This does not prove a particular event inside the house; it suggests a relationship with the place from which the speaker wants freedom.

The final contrast makes that freedom more reflective. Carrying “nothing” from the shelf might initially sound like loss, but the conjunction “but” introduces a different judgement. The needed “space” can mean emotional room as well as physical distance. The ending therefore interprets leaving as gaining something rather than simply abandoning belongings.

The poem's movement from domestic completion through travel to reflection supports this change. It makes departure feel chosen and constructive, while leaving the exact past circumstances open. The speaker's clearer sense of agency distinguishes the ending from a narrative of helpless separation.

25 marks

33. Compare how Waiting and Departure present absence and separation. Use the poems printed in the two questions above.

Answer:

Both poems use an ordinary domestic cup and movement outside a home to explore separation, but their speakers respond differently. Waiting presents continuing attachment to an absent person, whereas Departure presents leaving as a chosen route towards independence. Their similar objects make the difference in attitudes especially clear.

In Waiting, the cup remains warm and the speaker prepares “a second place”. These details make the missing person present through objects that are ready for them. Removing the extra place introduces doubt, so the action swings between hope and disappointment. By contrast, Departure begins by rinsing the cup and turning the key. Its completed actions make the domestic routine something the speaker can leave behind. Both poems connect emotion with small gestures, but one gesture remains unfinished in purpose while the other supports a decision.

The outside world develops the contrast. Waiting's bus windows carry “other lives”, a metaphor that places connection beyond the speaker's own room. The speaker watches movement but does not participate in it. In Departure, the speaker chooses a seat and travels. The personified morning that “opens” makes the street seem inviting, while sunlight gives the journey a positive quality. Movement therefore emphasises exclusion in one poem and opportunity in the other.

Both poems use a stanza break to shift attention, but the directions differ. Waiting looks outside before returning to the prepared room; Departure looks back at a house that is becoming smaller. These structural movements reinforce each speaker's relationship with home. One returns to attachment, while the other gains distance from a place whose windows once seemed to watch them. The personification of panes that “no longer stare” gives that distance emotional significance.

The endings finally interpret absence in opposing ways. Waiting places readiness beside “No one arrives” in two short statements, making expectation and disappointment coexist without resolution. Departure uses “but” to turn apparent loss into the needed “space”. That word can suggest freedom as well as a physical gap. Neither poem explains every circumstance, so the interpretations depend on the details provided rather than invented biography.

Together, the poems show that separation is not one fixed feeling. Similar rooms, cups and buses can express either painful waiting or constructive departure, depending on agency, viewpoint and the direction of the ending.
