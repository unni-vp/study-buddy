Language, structure and form

Language and imagery

Language concerns the words and patterns chosen. Imagery creates an imagined experience, often through the senses or comparison.

Useful methods

  • Simile: compares using like or as. Explain the specific qualities transferred by the comparison.
  • Metaphor: presents one thing as another. Ask what the new image implies.
  • Personification: gives human qualities to something non-human. Explain whether it seems comforting, powerful, indifferent or threatening.
  • Sensory imagery: sound, sight, touch, smell or taste makes a scene vivid. Link the sensation to meaning.
  • Semantic field: related words build a pattern, such as confinement or growth.
  • Repetition: returns to a word or idea. It may intensify concern, suggest persistence or show obsession, depending on context.
  • Sound: alliteration, sibilance or other repeated sounds can reinforce an image or rhythm. Do not claim one sound always creates one effect.

A strong comment moves beyond naming the technique: the choice suggests something particular about this speaker, situation or idea.

Structure and form

Structure is how the poem develops and organises ideas. Form is its overall kind or shape: for example a sonnet, an elegy, a dramatic monologue or free verse.

Structure you can explain

  • Stanza change: may mark a new time, place, viewpoint or emotional stage.
  • Enjambment: a sentence continues over a line ending. Consider the words temporarily separated, the movement of thought or an expectation delayed.
  • End-stopping: punctuation closes a line. It may create a firm statement, a pause or a controlled sequence.
  • Caesura: a pause within a line. Explain what the interruption separates or makes prominent.
  • Volta: a turn in thought or attitude. It is not restricted to sonnets.
  • Contrast: an earlier image is challenged by a later one.
  • Circular structure: the ending returns to the opening, perhaps with changed meaning.

Avoid automatic equations such as enjambment equals speed or regular rhyme equals happiness. Look at the sentence, subject and rhythm together.

Form without guessing

A sonnet usually has 14 lines, but identify its organisation and features before making claims about a particular sonnet type. Free verse has no fixed regular metre or rhyme pattern; it can still use deliberate repetition and organisation.

If a specialist form is unclear, analyse the features you can see accurately. The speaker's movement from confidence to doubt is more useful than an incorrect label.

A short poem to practise with

Waiting

Your cup still warms the kitchen shelf.
The clock repeats its patient beat.
I lay a second place at six,
then move it back beneath my seat.

Outside, the buses empty out;
their windows carry other lives.
I leave one small lamp by the door.
The room stays ready. No one arrives.

Overview: a speaker waits for somebody whose absence remains unexplained. Familiar routines create hope, but the ending exposes continuing loneliness.

Language / imagery: “their windows carry other lives” turns the buses into glimpses of connection elsewhere. The metaphor contrasts public movement with the speaker's private waiting.

Structure: laying and removing the second place makes hope and disappointment visible. The stanza break moves from the kitchen to the wider world, then returns indoors.

Ending: “The room stays ready. No one arrives.” uses two short statements and a pause to place preparation beside absence. The contrast leaves the waiting unresolved.

Worked paragraph

The poem presents loneliness through repeated acts of preparation. The speaker lays “a second place”, so absence becomes visible through an ordinary object intended for somebody else. Moving it away immediately afterwards reveals doubt within the apparent routine. At the end, the room remains ready but nobody arrives. The pause between those final statements separates hope from reality, leaving the reader with a sense of emotional suspension rather than closure.

Selecting evidence and building paragraphs

Choose short evidence that supports your argument. Look across the poem, including the ending, instead of writing about only the first stanza.

A developed paragraph can include:

  1. A point directly answering the question.
  2. A short quotation or precise reference.
  3. Analysis of a particular word, image or structural choice.
  4. Explanation of its meaning and effect.
  5. A connection to the poem's wider development.

Personal response means an informed interpretation supported by the text. “I think it is nice” gives no reason. “The unresolved ending makes the speaker's hope seem painful” identifies a meaningful response.

An introduction needs an overview, not a dictionary definition of poetry. A conclusion can explain the poem's overall development without repeating every technique.
