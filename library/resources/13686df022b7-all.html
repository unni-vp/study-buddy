Creative prose writing

Choose a story you can control

A story needs something to happen and something to change. Think of it as a short journey: the important part is not how many places you visit, but what is different when you arrive.

Choose one of the four offered titles or prompts. Make its connection to your response clear. The task is narrative/recount, so purely describing a scene without events or development will not fulfil it fully.

The task carries 40 marks: 24 for communication and organisation, 16 for vocabulary, sentence structure, spelling and punctuation. The sample paper suggests about 450–600 words. This is a length guide, not a reason to add an unrelated event or cut off an ending.

A small time span, a limited setting and a few characters can help you stay in control. They are useful choices, not compulsory rules.

Build a meaningful plot

Plan want → obstacle → choice → consequence → change. The change may be emotional or practical. An ordinary event can work if the narrator notices something important through it.

For a story called The Small Decision:

  • Want: a tired café worker wants to catch the bus and go home.
  • Obstacle: a customer has left something he needs.
  • Choice: put it in lost property or go after him.
  • Consequence: returning it reveals a detail the worker misunderstood.
  • Change: the worker becomes less certain of a quick judgement.

Every event contributes to that direction. A burglary, a sudden accident and a secret twin would not automatically make this story better; extra events can prevent the main moment from developing.

Open with a useful focus

An opening can begin with action, a significant object, a telling observation or a problem. Establish enough of who, where and what matters for the reader to follow.

The last customer had gone, but his table looked as though he was still there.

The sentence introduces an absence that needs explaining. A spoon, a displaced chair and the abandoned glasses make the situation concrete. The opening is connected to the event that follows, rather than a decorative weather paragraph.

Withholding information can create curiosity, but do not hide everything. A reader who cannot work out who is doing what is confused rather than intrigued.

Give characters choices

Character emerges through actions, thoughts, speech and responses. A useful contradiction makes a person feel more real: the café worker is polite but impatient, then acts kindly while still wanting the bus.

I opened the drawer. There were two scarves, a child's glove and a key without a label. The glasses would fit easily on top.

The convenient drawer makes the easy option visible. The reader understands the temptation without needing a paragraph announcing that the narrator faces a moral dilemma.

Use dialogue when it changes the situation or reveals character. Avoid a long conversation in which characters explain things both already know. Actions can qualify speech: a person may say they are fine while repeatedly checking a pocket.

Control time and viewpoint

Choose first person or third person and keep the viewpoint clear. A first-person narrator can report another person's expression and infer feelings; they cannot automatically know that person's unspoken thoughts.

Past tense is often convenient for a recount. Maintain it unless a change is purposeful. A brief memory can explain the present moment, but signal the shift and return clearly.

I remembered insisting that being measured counted as an achievement. This connects the height marks to a childhood memory. The present moving-day problem remains the main event.

You can summarise uneventful time and slow down a decisive moment. Showing every action in equal detail makes the writing feel mechanical. A clear sentence telling us about an afternoon's work can create room to show the important encounter closely.

Make description serve the story

Choose details that fit the viewpoint and mood. You do not need a separate example of all five senses.

  • “The lenses were thick enough to bend the edge of the counter.” A physical detail helps the narrator understand why the glasses matter.
  • “A crowded little ladder” of pencil marks. The image makes growth visible and prepares the emotional difficulty of leaving.
  • The house's echoes. The changed sound makes a familiar place feel newly empty.

Precise nouns and verbs often work better than several vague adjectives. He checked one pocket, then the other, then started again reveals worry more convincingly than he was extremely, terribly worried.

An image should clarify the moment. Combining a storm, a monster, a volcano and a broken mirror in one description creates competing pictures rather than intensity.

Shape paragraphs and endings

Change paragraph when time, place, speaker, action or focus changes. Paragraph length can affect emphasis, but a one-sentence paragraph needs a purpose.

“Six minutes until my bus.” Its shortness isolates the deadline. Later, a longer reflective passage slows the narrator's reassessment of the customer. The contrast serves the story's priorities.

Link paragraphs through a recurring object, a continued action or a developing question. A motif is a significant repeated detail: the glasses or height marks gain meaning as the story develops.

A satisfying ending shows the result of the central event. It may echo the opening with a changed meaning, reveal a small new action or leave a considered uncertainty. It need not explain a moral in a final lecture.

Avoid a sudden unrelated twist or a dream ending that cancels everything unless it has been carefully prepared and serves the story. Finishing one meaningful episode is better than beginning another plot in the final paragraph.

Control sentences and punctuation

Vary sentence structures for meaning. A longer sentence can connect thought and action; a short sentence can emphasise a realisation. Accuracy matters as well as range.

ProblemRepair
The bus arrived, I ran.The bus arrived. I ran. / When the bus arrived, I ran.
She look at the mark.She looked at the mark.
Its getting late.It's getting late.
The girls coat was wet.The girl's coat was wet.

A semicolon can link two closely related complete clauses: The room was empty; the marks were still there. A colon can introduce an explanation after a complete clause: One thing remained: the pencil marks. Do not insert complex punctuation at random to display it.

For dialogue, place punctuation inside the speech marks where it belongs, and start a new paragraph for a new speaker:

“Ready?” my sister called.

“Nearly,” I said.

Dialogue punctuation should help the reader hear and follow the exchange.

Edit for meaning, then accuracy

Check the whole story first:

  • Does it respond to the exact prompt?
  • Can the reader follow the event and viewpoint?
  • Is the central moment developed rather than rushed?
  • Does the ending follow from the choice or change?

Then check sentence boundaries, tense, agreement, spelling, apostrophes and dialogue punctuation. Remove a repeated idea or an image that does not fit. Replace an imprecise word rather than automatically choosing a longer one.

Use a short plan and leave checking time. The sample paper's writing allowance is about 45 minutes, including planning. Practice producing a complete, controlled episode within that time rather than memorising a story that may not fit the exam prompt.
