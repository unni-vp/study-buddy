Network types and connections

Wired and wireless networks

FeatureWired networkWireless network
ConnectionA cable connects each device.Radio signals connect devices without a cable to each one.
MobilityMovement is restricted by cables.Users can move around within coverage.
InstallationCabling can cost money and disrupt a building.Convenient where cables are difficult; mobile devices are easier to add.
PerformanceUsually stable, with low latency and less radio interference.Distance, obstacles, interference and congestion can reduce signal quality.
SecurityPhysical access makes interception harder, but a cable does not guarantee security.Signals may extend beyond a building, so authentication and encryption are important.
Suitable useFixed devices needing a stable connection.Phones and tablets needing mobility.

Copper and fibre-optic cable

FeatureCopper cableFibre-optic cable
SignalCarries electrical signals.Carries light.
Suitable useCommon, relatively inexpensive shorter links.High-bandwidth links over long distances.
Trade-offCan be affected by electromagnetic interference.Resists electromagnetic interference, but installation can cost more.

Choose a connection for the stated situation. Do not assume one option is always faster or more secure in every installation.

Making a justified connection choice

A fixed desktop used for large transfers may benefit from a stable wired connection. A tablet carried around a classroom needs mobility, so wireless access is more practical. Wireless performance can fall when walls weaken the signal or many nearby devices compete for access.

Copper carries electrical signals and is often economical for shorter connections within a building. Fibre carries light, supports high bandwidth over longer distances and resists electromagnetic interference. Fibre installation and the equipment at its ends may cost more.

Neither connection automatically makes transmitted information private. Wired access can still be compromised, and wireless signals may be received outside the intended room. Authentication and encryption address risks that a speed comparison alone does not cover.

Check the whole connection

A connection can only perform as well as its limiting part. Replacing a classroom's local cable will not remove a slow internet link outside the school. Many users sending large files can also compete for the same capacity.

Bandwidth describes how much data a connection can carry per second. Latency is the delay in communication. A video call can suffer from delay even when a connection can transfer a large file quickly. For a fixed workstation, stability may matter most; a moving tablet may need wireless access despite changing signal quality.

Choose using the actual distance, mobility, cost and workload. A label such as “wired” or “wireless” is not enough to guarantee a particular performance.
