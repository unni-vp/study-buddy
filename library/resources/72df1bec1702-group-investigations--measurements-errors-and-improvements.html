Measurements, errors and improvements

Choose equipment appropriate to the quantity and required resolution: balance for mass, ruler for length, thermometer for temperature, measuring cylinder/pipette for volume, timer for time and calibrated probe for pH. Check zero, read scales at eye level and record units in table headings.

TermMeaningUseful response
AccuracyClose to the true valueCalibrate and correct systematic offsets
PrecisionRepeated values close togetherImprove measurement resolution and consistency
RepeatabilitySame investigator/method gives similar resultsRepeat under the same conditions
ReproducibilityDifferent investigators/equipment give similar resultsCompare independent investigations
Random errorUnpredictable scatterRepeat and calculate a mean
Systematic errorConsistent bias or offsetFind the cause and recalibrate/correct it

Systematic bias: if a balance always reads 0.2 g too high, taking more readings does not remove that offset. Check and correct its zero or calibration.

Anomalies: investigate an unexpected result and repeat the measurement. Exclude it only with a justified reason, such as a recorded measurement failure.

Uncertainty: report how much the readings vary, for example using their range. Consider the instrument's resolution as well as the spread of repeated readings.

Resolution does not remove other errors

A thermometer reading to 0.1°C can still lag behind the true maximum temperature. A more finely divided scale does not remove heat loss or slow response. Match the improvement to the problem: insulation limits heat loss, prompt mixing improves consistency, and an appropriate probe can record changes more frequently.

For a difference between two readings, consider uncertainty in both. If each burette reading has a stated uncertainty of ±0.05 cm³, a titre has a simple maximum uncertainty of ±0.10 cm³. At a titre of 20.00 cm³, this is 0.10 ÷ 20.00 × 100 = 0.5%. Use the uncertainty convention specified in the question.
