Magnetism and electromagnetism questions & answers

1 mark2 marks3 marks4 marks5 marks6 marks

1 mark

1. Name the magnetic material commonly used as a transformer core.

Answer:

Iron.

2. What happens when two north poles are brought together?

Answer:

They repel.

3. What is the unit of magnetic flux density?

Answer:

Tesla (T).

4. Which device uses the generator effect to convert sound vibrations into an electrical signal?

Answer:

A microphone.

5. Name the effect that makes a current-carrying coil move in a loudspeaker.

Answer:

The motor effect.

6. Does a stationary magnet beside a stationary coil produce a sustained induced potential difference?

Answer:

No.

7. What does the north-seeking end of a compass show?

Answer:

The direction of the local magnetic field.

8. Which ring arrangement connects a simple alternator coil to its external circuit?

Answer:

Two slip rings.

9. Both the magnetic field and the current through a perpendicular wire are reversed. What happens to the force direction? Choose one: A it reverses; B it stays the same; C force becomes zero; D it becomes parallel to the field.

Answer:

B: it stays the same.

2 marks

10. Distinguish a permanent magnet from an induced magnet.

Answer:
  • A permanent magnet retains its magnetism and produces its own field.
  • An induced magnet becomes magnetic in an external field and usually loses most or all magnetism when the field is removed.

11. Why does attraction alone not prove that an iron object is a permanent magnet?

Answer:
  • An initially unmagnetised iron object can become an induced magnet.
  • It is then attracted to the magnet, even though it was not a permanent magnet.

12. Give two changes that increase the strength of an electromagnet.

Answer:
  • Increase the current through its coil.
  • Add an iron core or increase turns per unit length.

13. Describe the field inside a long solenoid, away from its ends.

Answer:
  • It is strong and approximately uniform.
  • Draw parallel, evenly spaced field lines with the same direction.

14. Why is a compass needed as well as iron filings when mapping a magnetic field?

Answer:
  • Filings show the field’s pattern.
  • A compass shows its direction, using the north-seeking end of its needle.

15. State the directions shown by Fleming’s first finger and second finger.

Answer:
  • First finger: magnetic field, from north to south.
  • Second finger: conventional current, positive to negative.

16. A wire carries 4.0 A perpendicular to a 0.20 T field. A length of 0.15 m is within the field. Calculate the force.

Answer:

F = BIl = 0.20 × 4.0 × 0.15 = 0.12 N.

17. What happens to the force on a wire if only the current is reversed? What if both current and field reverse?

Answer:
  • Reversing current alone reverses force.
  • Reversing both leaves force in its original direction.

18. Explain why a split-ring commutator is used in a simple DC motor.

Answer:
  • It reverses current in the coil every half-turn.
  • This reverses the side forces at the appropriate position, keeping the turning effect in the same rotational direction.

19. A magnet is moved into a coil connected to a voltmeter. Give two ways to increase the peak induced potential difference.

Answer:
  • Move the magnet faster.
  • Use a stronger magnet or more turns in the coil, with otherwise comparable conditions.

20. Why can a voltmeter show induced potential difference even when the coil does not carry a significant current?

Answer:
  • A changing linked field induces potential difference across the coil.
  • A substantial current requires a complete conducting circuit; potential difference does not require current to flow.

21. What happens to the induced signal if a magnet moves out of a coil instead of into it? Explain the comparison.

Answer:
  • Its polarity reverses for the same pole orientation.
  • The field change is reversed. Magnitude depends on speed and the other conditions.

22. Explain why a transformer normally needs AC rather than steady DC.

Answer:
  • AC creates a continuously changing magnetic field in the core.
  • This induces secondary potential difference. Steady DC does not provide the sustained field change needed.

23. Why is iron suitable for a transformer core?

Answer:
  • It is easily magnetised.
  • Its magnetisation can change and reverse with the alternating primary current, linking the changing field to the secondary.

24. A transformer has 500 primary turns and 100 secondary turns. Is it step-up or step-down? Explain.

Answer:
  • Step-down.
  • Fewer secondary turns mean lower secondary potential difference.

3 marks

25. Explain how a moving-coil loudspeaker makes sound.

Answer:
  • Changing current flows in a coil within a magnetic field, producing a changing motor-effect force.
  • The attached coil and cone vibrate as the current changes.
  • The cone creates pressure variations in air, producing sound waves.

26. Explain how a moving-coil microphone makes an electrical signal.

Answer:
  • Sound pressure variations vibrate a diaphragm and attached coil.
  • The coil moves in a magnetic field, changing the field linked with it.
  • The generator effect induces a changing potential difference that represents the sound.

27. A perpendicular wire experiences a 0.018 N force in a 60 mT field while carrying 2.0 A. Calculate the length within the field.

Answer:
  • B = 0.060 T.
  • l = F/(BI) = 0.018/(0.060 × 2.0).
  • l = 0.15 m.

28. A transformer has 300 primary turns at 240 V and 30 secondary turns. Calculate secondary voltage and state the type.

Answer:
  • Vₛ = Vₚ × nₛ/nₚ.
  • Vₛ = 240 × 30/300 = 24 V.
  • It is step-down.

29. Explain how AC in a primary coil leads to potential difference in a secondary coil.

Answer:
  • AC makes a changing magnetic field around the primary.
  • The iron core links the changing field with the secondary.
  • This induces alternating potential difference across the secondary.

30. Explain how an induced current opposes a magnet being pushed into a coil.

Answer:
  • Movement changes the magnetic field linked with the coil and induces current.
  • The current produces a field that opposes the original change.
  • An opposing magnetic force means work must be done to continue pushing, supplying the electrical energy.

31. Compare the potential-difference graphs of a simple alternator and a simple dynamo.

Answer:
  • Alternator AC crosses zero, changing sign each half-turn.
  • Dynamo output remains in one external direction because the split ring swaps connections.
  • Its output can pulse, so DC does not necessarily mean constant size.

32. An ideal transformer supplies 18 V at 2.0 A from a 240 V source. Calculate primary current.

Answer:
  • Secondary power = VₛIₛ = 18 × 2.0 = 36 W.
  • Ideal primary power = secondary power.
  • Iₚ = 36/240 = 0.15 A.

33. For the same transmitted power, a grid voltage is doubled. State the new current and heating loss as fractions of their original values, with resistance fixed.

Answer:
  • P = VI, so current becomes one half.
  • Cable heating follows I²R.
  • Heating loss becomes (1/2)² = one quarter.

4 marks

34. Describe a compass method for plotting the field around a bar magnet.

Answer:
  • Trace the magnet on paper and keep it fixed.
  • Place a small compass nearby and mark both ends of its needle.
  • Move it to continue from a previous mark, repeating to trace a line. Join marks smoothly.
  • Add arrows using the north-seeking end and repeat from other starting points. Keep other magnetic objects away.

35. An 80% efficient transformer supplies 12 V at 3.0 A from a 240 V source. Calculate input power and input current.

Answer:
  • Output power = 12 × 3.0 = 36 W.
  • Efficiency = output/input, so input power = 36/0.80 = 45 W.
  • Iₚ = Pₚ/Vₚ = 45/240 = 0.1875 A, about 0.19 A.
  • Input power exceeds output because some energy is dissipated.

36. Explain how opposite forces on the two sides of a motor coil can cause rotation rather than canceling the turning effect.

Answer:
  • The sides carry current in opposite directions within the same field.
  • They experience opposite motor-effect forces.
  • The forces act at different positions and create moments in the same rotational sense.
  • In a simple DC motor, a split-ring commutator reverses coil current each half-turn to maintain rotation.

37. Sketch the magnetic field around a bar magnet. Show the poles, field direction and how the drawing indicates stronger field near the poles.

Answer:

Label the magnet N and S. Draw curved field lines connecting the poles, with arrows from N to S outside the magnet. Draw lines closer together near the poles to show a stronger field. Field lines must not cross.

5 marks

38. A transformer has 1000 primary turns and 50 secondary turns. Its primary potential difference is 240 V. It supplies a secondary current of 3.0 A and is 80% efficient. Calculate the secondary potential difference, input power and primary current.

Answer:
  • Turns ratio: Vₛ = Vₚ × nₛ/nₚ = 240 × 50/1000 = 12 V.
  • Output power: P = VI = 12 × 3.0 = 36 W.
  • Efficiency = 0.80. Input power = 36 ÷ 0.80 = 45 W.
  • Primary current: Iₚ = Pinput/Vₚ = 45 ÷ 240 = 0.1875 A, or about 0.19 A.
  • Input power exceeds output power because some energy is dissipated. The ideal equal-power equation cannot be used unchanged here.

6 marks

39. Explain how transformers make long-distance electricity transmission more efficient and still allow consumers to use a lower voltage.

Answer:
  • The power station’s supply enters a step-up transformer with more secondary turns. AC creates a changing field that induces a higher secondary potential difference.
  • For a given transmitted power, P = VI means the higher voltage allows a lower current.
  • Heating in cables is I²R, so reducing current greatly reduces heating losses for the same cable resistance.
  • A step-down transformer near consumers has fewer secondary turns and reduces the potential difference.
  • Transformer output cannot exceed its input power. Real equipment dissipates some energy; the high voltage reduces transmission loss rather than eliminating every loss.
  • The comparison needs the same delivered power. Saying only “high voltage is more efficient” misses the connection through current.

40. Compare how a moving-coil speaker and a moving-coil microphone transfer information between electrical signals and sound.

Answer:
  • In a speaker, changing current passes through a coil in a permanent magnetic field.
  • The motor effect gives a force whose direction and size change with the signal, moving the attached cone back and forth.
  • Cone motion makes air compressions and rarefactions. The vibrations follow the electrical signal’s frequency.
  • In a microphone, sound pressure variations first move a diaphragm and its coil.
  • Motion in a magnetic field changes field linkage and induces potential difference by the generator effect. With a connected circuit, this gives a varying electrical signal.
  • Thus a speaker uses electrical input to make motion and sound; a microphone uses sound-driven motion to make an electrical signal. Both need the magnetic field, but their input and output differ.
