Coastal landscapes

Coastal landscapes

Waves supply energy

Wind transfers energy to the sea surface. Wave size is influenced by wind strength, how long it blows and the fetch: the distance of open water over which the wind blows.

Swash carries water up the beach after a wave breaks. Backwash returns it downhill under gravity.

Wave typeTypical balanceEffect on the beach
ConstructiveSwash is stronger than backwash. Waves are usually lower and less frequent.More material is moved up than removed, helping build the beach.
DestructiveBackwash is stronger than swash. Waves are usually steeper and more frequent.More material is removed from the beach, increasing exposure to erosion.

A coastline can experience different wave conditions at different times. A storm can remove material from a beach that built up during calmer weather.

Longshore drift connects places

  1. Waves approach at an angle, influenced by the prevailing wind.
  2. Swash carries sediment diagonally up the beach.
  3. Gravity returns the backwash more directly down the slope.
  4. Repetition moves sediment along the coast in a zigzag.

The direction must be checked for the particular stretch of coast. A bend in the coastline or a change in wave conditions can change the pattern. Defences can interrupt sediment movement and affect beaches farther along that local route.

Headlands and bays

Where bands of rock with different resistance meet the sea, less resistant rock can erode faster to form a bay. More resistant rock remains projecting into the sea as a headland. Cracks and layers influence the rate as well as the rock type.

As waves approach shallow water around a headland, their direction changes through wave refraction. Energy becomes concentrated on the headland, while the bay is more sheltered. This helps maintain erosion at the headland and deposition within the bay.

Caves, arches and stacks

  1. Hydraulic action and abrasion enlarge a crack in a headland to form a cave.
  2. Continued erosion deepens the cave until it breaks through, forming an arch.
  3. Erosion at the base and weathering above weaken the arch. Its unsupported roof eventually collapses.
  4. A detached pillar remains as a stack. Further erosion can eventually reduce it to a low stump.

The collapse is a consequence of gravity acting on weakened, unsupported rock. Naming the landforms in order is only part of the explanation; the processes connect the stages.

Beaches and spits

A beach is a deposit of sand or shingle. It builds where sediment supply and deposition exceed removal. Sheltered water and constructive-wave conditions often favour accumulation.

A spit is a narrow ridge attached to land at one end. Longshore drift carries material along the coast. At a change in coastline direction or an estuary, sediment continues beyond the land and is deposited as transport weakens. Further supply extends the ridge. Changes in wind and wave direction can produce a recurved end, while river flow or currents may prevent it closing the entrance.

The sheltered water behind a spit has less energy. Fine sediment settles to form mudflats; salt-tolerant plants trap more sediment and help a salt marsh develop. The habitat and the landform therefore depend on both physical deposition and living vegetation.
