Series and parallel circuits

FeatureSeries: one loopParallel: separate branches
CurrentSame through every componentTotal current = sum of branch currents
Potential differenceShared; component values add to supply pdSame across each branch connected to the supply
ResistanceRtotal = R₁ + R₂ + …Total is smaller than the smallest branch resistance
Adding a resistorMore opposition in the route; current falls for fixed supply pdExtra route for charge; total current rises for fixed supply pd
Broken componentBreaks the whole loopOther complete branches can still work

Series example

Two resistors, 4 Ω and 8 Ω, are connected in series across 12 V.

  1. Add the resistances: R = 4 + 8 = 12 Ω.
  2. Find the current: I = V/R = 12 ÷ 12 = 1 A.
  3. The potential differences are 4 V and 8 V, adding to the 12 V supply.

Parallel example: two branches across 6 V have currents 0.30 A and 0.20 A.

Total current is 0.50 A.

Both branches have 6 V across them.

Charge is conserved at a junction; it does not collect there.

A household circuit uses parallel branches so appliances receive the same supply voltage and can be switched independently. You do not need a reciprocal-resistance formula to explain why adding a parallel path reduces the total resistance.

Predict what happens to an existing branch

With an ideal fixed 6 V supply, adding another resistor in parallel leaves 6 V across the original branch. Its current stays the same if its resistance is unchanged. The supply current increases because it now feeds an extra branch.

Adding a resistor in series increases the total resistance and reduces the current through the whole loop. The original resistor then has a smaller share of the same supply voltage. State the supply assumption: a real battery's terminal voltage can fall under a heavy load.
