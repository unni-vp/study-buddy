QuestionApply and calculate

Explain how meiosis and fertilisation maintain chromosome number across generations.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Meiosis halves the chromosome number, producing gametes with 23 chromosomes in humans.
  • Fertilisation joins two gamete nuclei.
  • The fertilised egg has 46 chromosomes, restoring the usual body-cell number.
QuestionApply and calculate

Describe chromosome changes during meiosis without naming its phases.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • DNA is copied once, giving joined copies of each chromosome.
  • The cell divides twice, producing four gametes with half the original chromosome number.
  • The gametes are genetically different because information from the two parents is reshuffled.

QuestionApply and calculate

Two cystic-fibrosis carriers have genotypes Cc. Determine the probability their child is affected.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Each parent produces gametes C or c.
  • The combinations are CC, Cc, Cc, cc.
  • Only cc is affected: 1/4 = 25% for each pregnancy.
QuestionApply and calculate

A heterozygous parent has polydactyly, Pp. The other parent is pp. Determine the chance of an affected child.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Parent gametes are P or p, and p only.
  • Offspring combinations are Pp, Pp, pp, pp.
  • P is dominant, so 2/4 = 50% are predicted to be affected.
QuestionApply and calculate

Use the usual XX/XY model to explain equal probabilities of female and male offspring.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer

  • Fusion produces XX or XY with equal chances in the model.
  • Each outcome has probability 1/2, not a guarantee of equal numbers in a family.
QuestionApply and calculate

In 200 children from carrier-by-carrier crosses, calculate the expected number of affected children and explain why observed numbers might differ.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer

  • Expected number = 200 ×1/4 = 50.
  • Inheritance is random, so observed counts can differ from the expectation.

Describe the main steps linking a DNA base sequence to a functional protein.

QuestionMulti-step application

Explain how natural selection can make a camouflaged insect colour more common.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • A population contains inherited variation in colour.
  • Better-camouflaged insects are less likely to be eaten.
  • They are more likely to survive and reproduce.
  • Their offspring inherit the alleles, so these alleles become more common over generations.
QuestionMulti-step application

Explain how isolation can lead to a new species.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Populations become isolated, preventing breeding between them.
  • Different conditions and mutations produce different variation and selection pressures.
  • Over many generations different alleles accumulate.
  • Eventually the populations cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so they are different species.
QuestionMulti-step application

Describe selective breeding to produce wheat with high yield and disease resistance.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • Choose parents showing the desired characteristics.
  • Breed them together.
  • Select offspring with the best combination and breed those offspring.
  • Repeat selection and breeding over many generations, rather than simply reusing the original parents.

QuestionMulti-step application

Compare plant cuttings, tissue culture and embryo splitting.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • A cutting grows into a plant from a portion of a parent plant.

QuestionMulti-step application

Explain how antibiotic-resistant bacteria become more common during treatment.

Select the card or press Space to reveal the answer.

Answer
  • A random mutation can make some bacteria resistant.
  • Antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria while resistant ones survive.
  • Survivors reproduce rapidly and pass on resistance.
  • Resistant bacteria form a larger proportion of the population; antibiotic does not deliberately create a useful mutation.
