19th and 21st-century non-fiction reading

Understand the text and the task

Non-fiction presents information, experiences or arguments about the real world. A writer still selects and shapes what you see. Think of two people describing the same journey: one might complain about the delays, while another celebrates the scenery.

Read the introduction and identify who is writing, when, for whom and why. An advice book may instruct householders; an opinion article may challenge readers' habits. Use the information supplied rather than inventing a biography or historical belief.

This reading section uses one nineteenth-century and one twenty-first-century text. There are 40 marks. The complete paper lasts two hours and also contains two writing tasks. Allow roughly an hour for reading and its questions, then protect time for both writing tasks.

Before each answer, check:

  • Command: find, explain, analyse, evaluate, synthesise or compare?
  • Focus: which particular idea, impression or attitude?
  • Range: the whole text, specified lines, or both texts?

The sample paper includes short retrieval tasks, a 4-mark synthesis and longer 10-mark responses. Follow the marks and instructions on the actual paper rather than expecting another board's question pattern.

Form, purpose and audience work together

A form is the kind of text, such as a letter, report or article. Purpose is what the writer wants to achieve. Audience is the intended readership. The same article form can entertain, advise or challenge an opinion, so identifying its form does not settle its tone.

Read any supplied date and introduction, but test an interpretation against the writing itself. A nineteenth-century writer may use humour or intimacy. A modern writer may sound formal or authoritative. Explain the actual choice rather than assigning attitudes by century.

Retrieve information and infer carefully

Retrieval means finding what the text states. For several requested details, give several different relevant facts. Repeating the same fact in different words adds nothing.

Inference means working out a supported meaning. Child's instruction to buy only enough at first suggests that experience should guide later spending. It does not prove that she opposes every purchase.

For an unfamiliar word, read the sentence and its neighbours. Economy in the household extract means careful use of resources; avarice means greed. The contrast helps explain both words. A nineteenth-century text can have long sentences, but follow its main claim and examples before worrying about every unfamiliar word.

Distinguish what the writer claims from a proven fact. An example can make an argument persuasive without establishing that it applies to everyone. Use precise attribution: *the writer argues*, *reports* or *suggests*.

Explain how the argument works

For a how question, connect idea → evidence → choice → effect in context. The choice might involve language, an example, tone or the order of the argument. Naming a device is only the start.

Child writes “Nothing should be thrown away”. The absolute “Nothing” makes avoiding waste sound like a firm duty. Her later phrase “however trifling” extends that duty even to things with very little remaining use. This supports her strict approach to household economy.

The modern article opens with a missing button. This small personal incident makes replacement feel like an everyday decision. It prepares the wider argument that convenience can hide the resources used to make an object.

Select a short quotation that proves your point. Explain the exact words rather than attaching a generic sentence such as *this makes the reader read on*. Develop several relevant points across the required range, including a shift or qualification where useful.

Analyse language, tone and organisation

Language includes precise word choices, images, commands, questions, contrasts and pronouns. Explain what they do here.

ChoiceWhat to investigate
ImperativeWhat action is requested, and how forceful or practical is the instruction?
Inclusive weWho is invited into a shared responsibility?
AnecdoteHow does one experience introduce or illustrate the wider claim?
ContrastWhich competing ideas become clearer when placed together?
Rhetorical questionWhat answer is implied, and what assumption is challenged?
Repetition or listWhat idea gains emphasis or builds through the examples?

Tone is the attitude conveyed by the voice: for example, earnest, critical, conversational, impatient or reassuring. Support the label with evidence. Tone can change within a text.

Organisation means how the argument develops. The modern article moves from a personal mistake to a wider concern, acknowledges a difficulty, then proposes manageable action. That concession makes its final recommendation more realistic. Explain the development instead of simply listing beginning, middle and end.

Do not assume that an older writer is automatically formal or a modern writer always informal. Judge the actual words and presentation.

Evaluate a view with evidence

Evaluation is a reasoned judgement about a view or its presentation. You may agree, disagree or give a qualified response, but support it from the text.

For *What do you think and feel about Child's advice?*:

Her advice is practical when she urges readers to buy only enough at first. The explanation that needs become clear through experience makes caution seem sensible. However, the absolute “Nothing” and the demand that “every member” earn or save money can feel inflexible. The extract leaves little room for rest or for possessions that cannot usefully be kept. Her distinction between economy and “avarice” nevertheless complicates the idea that she simply values money above people: she presents saving as a way to remain generous.

This weighs different parts of the argument. A judgement about the writer's ideas is stronger than a personal story loosely related to the topic. When discussing credibility, explain the strength or limitation of the evidence actually provided; do not invent contradictory statistics.

Synthesise information from both texts

Synthesis brings together relevant information from two sources. Imagine sorting two shopping bags onto the same labelled shelf: take only the items that belong to the question's focus.

For *Why do both writers encourage avoiding waste?*:

  • Child: objects may still have a useful purpose; careful spending helps a household meet its needs.
  • Modern article: repair can save replacement money and make better use of materials and labour.

Use both sources and make their identities clear. Concise paraphrase is often enough. Synthesis mainly selects and combines information and ideas; it does not automatically need a paragraph about metaphors or tone.

Do not turn *why* into a list of *how*. Knitting stockings is a method; making them last longer is a reason. Keep every selected detail relevant to the shared focus.

Separate the job of each two-text task

TaskMain jobWhat a response needs
SynthesisBring together relevant information from both sources.Clear attribution and the information requested by the shared focus.
ComparisonExplain similarities or differences in ideas and their presentation.Connected points, evidence from both writers and explanation of relevant methods.

For a question about reasons for avoiding waste, use the reasons in each source. Do not spend that answer explaining every metaphor. For a question about how attitudes are conveyed, the absolute commands in one source and the accommodating concession in the other become important.

Use a small plan with one shared issue on each row. If a point can only be supported from one text, either find a relevant connection or choose a better shared issue. Do not manufacture agreement that the sources do not support.

Compare ideas and how they are conveyed

Comparison explains a meaningful similarity or difference between the writers. Compare both their ideas or perspectives and how they communicate them when the question asks for both.

A useful planning table has a shared issue down the left and one evidence column for each text. Choose issues such as responsibility, reasons for saving, flexibility and the reader's role. This produces connections rather than two unrelated mini-essays.

Both writers encourage useful action instead of careless replacement, but they differ in flexibility. Child's absolute “Nothing” and instruction that “every member” should contribute present economy as a household duty. The modern writer instead says repair must not become “a test of moral purity” and acknowledges limits of time, money and safety. This concession gives the modern argument a more accommodating tone, whereas Child's commands create a firmer expectation of obedience.

The paragraph makes one connected comparison, uses evidence from each source and explains how the attitudes are conveyed. A comparison word alone, such as *whereas*, cannot replace that explanation.

Build a complete response

Use a quick plan suited to the task. A 10-mark analysis or evaluation needs developed understanding across the relevant text, not ten disconnected device labels. A comparison needs sustained connections between both texts.

  • Make a point that answers the precise question.
  • Select brief, accurate evidence from the right range.
  • Explain the relevant wording, organisation or implication.
  • Develop or qualify the point where the text supports it.
  • For comparison, connect the other writer within the same line of argument.

Check that each quotation belongs to the writer named. Avoid claims that every reader must feel the same emotion. Use *invites*, *encourages* or *may suggest* when discussing a plausible effect.
