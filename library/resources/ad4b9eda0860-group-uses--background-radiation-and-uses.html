Uses, risks and nuclear energy

Background radiation and uses

Background radiation is always present. Natural sources include rocks, radon gas, cosmic rays and substances in food/living things. Human-made sources include fallout from weapons tests and nuclear accidents. Medical procedures can add to a person's dose.

Location and occupation affect dose: rock type, altitude and work around radiation can matter. Radiation dose measures biological effect;

1 Sv = 1,000 mSv.

Compare data in the same units.

UseSuitable choice and reason
Medical tracerGamma can leave the body and reach an external detector; choose a half-life long enough for the investigation but short enough to limit continuing exposure
Destroying unwanted tissueRadiation damages cells; direct the dose at the target and limit exposure of healthy tissue
Thickness monitoringBeta can pass through a thin sheet but its detected count changes with thickness; alpha would be absorbed too easily and gamma may pass through too readily
Sterilising equipmentPenetrating gamma reaches microorganisms without leaving radioactive material on the equipment

For the same initial number of radioactive nuclei, shorter half-life means greater initial activity, but activity falls faster. A long-lived contaminant can remain a problem for much longer. “Longer half-life is always safer” is wrong: assess the whole situation.

When comparing benefits and risks, use the dose, expected benefit and available alternatives supplied in the question. Published research and peer review let other scientists check evidence about radiation effects.

Choose a half-life for the job

For an investigation lasting several hours, a tracer with a half-life of a few seconds may lose activity before measurements can be completed. One lasting many years may cause avoidable continuing exposure. Choose a half-life that allows transport and detection while limiting the period of exposure afterwards.

Half-life alone does not determine safety: amount, activity, emission type, location and exposure time also matter. Radioactive decay and removal of material from the body are separate ways of reducing continuing exposure.

Using radiation to destroy unwanted tissue is radiotherapy. Chemotherapy uses drugs. Both can affect healthy tissue, but they are different treatments.
