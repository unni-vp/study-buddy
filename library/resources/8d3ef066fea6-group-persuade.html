Persuade and adapt your form

Persuasion that grows from the idea

Persuasion is helping a reader see why a claim matters. Think of a bridge: reasons and examples are its supports. Attractive language cannot replace missing supports.

ChoiceExampleWhen it helps
Direct address“You only need to try one session.”Makes a manageable action feel personally relevant. Avoid accusing the reader.
Inclusive language“We all need somewhere to begin.”Builds a shared concern when the claim is fair.
Rhetorical question“Why should being new mean being unwelcome?”Sharpens an issue; follow with reasoning rather than more questions.
Contrast“A first attempt, not a final judgement.”Clarifies the difference that supports your argument.
Three-part pattern“Ask a question, try a session, make your own decision.”Gives a final recommendation rhythm and direction.
Anecdote or exampleA nervous newcomer completes a small task.Makes an abstract benefit concrete; keep it relevant and brief.
Carefully chosen emotion“No one should spend lunchtime feeling invisible.”Shows the human consequence, without exaggerated claims.

Use a technique because it helps the meaning. Repeating a device checklist can produce an artificial voice. Writing “This is a rhetorical question” in your article would break that voice completely.

Answer a counterargument fairly

A counterargument is a reasonable objection. A concession admits where it has some truth. A response then explains why your position still makes sense.

“An after-school club will not suit everyone, especially students with a long journey home. A lunchtime trial would offer another route in. The aim should be more choice, rather than another demand on everyone’s timetable.”

That is stronger than “Some people disagree, but they are wrong.” Not every advice piece needs a formal opposing argument; use one when it improves the task you are writing.

Change the form without losing the purpose

An article is only one possible form. Recognisable conventions help the reader, but developed prose does most of the work.

FormUseful shape and conventionsMain risk
ArticleRelevant headline; engaging opening; developed paragraphs; purposeful ending. Subheadings can help a guide.Writing an essay addressed to an examiner, or spending time drawing a magazine layout.
Formal letterAppropriate greeting; clear reason for writing; developed case; specific request or conclusion; suitable sign-off.Rudeness, vague complaints or asking the recipient to do something outside their control.
Speech or talkAddress listeners; clear opening; audible signposting; connected ideas; memorable close.Listing stage directions or forgetting that the writing must sound natural when spoken.
ReviewIdentify what is reviewed; select criteria; describe enough to support judgement; recommend for a particular audience.Retelling the whole plot instead of evaluating the experience.
ReportState purpose; group findings clearly, often with headings; explain what they imply; recommend if asked.Making unsupported claims sound like measured findings.
Guide or leafletOrganise advice around the reader’s needs; clear sections; practical detail and explanations.A decorative page or a bare checklist with too little developed writing.

Letters

For an unknown recipient: Dear Sir or Madam and Yours faithfully. For a named recipient, such as Dear Ms Patel, use Yours sincerely. A letter to an editor can use Dear Editor. Keep any address block brief; do not let formatting consume the writing time.

Firm and courteous: “I am concerned that removing the lunchtime club would leave students with fewer supervised activities.”

Too hostile: “Your ridiculous plan proves you do not care about anyone.”

Challenge the proposal, explain its consequences and offer an achievable alternative. A letter to a friend would need a different register.

Speeches and talks

Write the words you would actually say. A class talk can be warm and humorous; a speech to a formal panel may be more restrained. Link abstract ideas to familiar situations. Use short signposts such as “The first problem is ...” so listeners can follow without rereading.

Reviews and advice

A useful review combines detail + judgement + reason: “The clear beginners’ instructions make the game easy to learn, although experienced players may want a harder challenge.” End by explaining who would enjoy it and why.

Useful advice combines action + how + why: “Choose a club you can reach at lunchtime. That removes the need for another journey and makes a first visit easier to fit into your week.”
