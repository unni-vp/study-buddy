Landscapes of the UK

The UK landscape

The big pattern

Think of Britain as a tilted table. The north and west are generally higher, steeper and wetter. The south and east are generally lower, flatter and drier.

  • Upland landscapes have high relief, steep slopes and exposed rock. They are common in Scotland, Wales and northern England.
  • Lowland landscapes have gentler relief and wider valleys. They are common in southern and eastern England.
  • Glaciated landscapes were shaped by ice. They often contain steep-sided valleys, lakes and deposited material.
  • Geology matters because resistant rock erodes more slowly than softer rock.
  • Climate affects weathering, river discharge, waves and vegetation.
  • Human activity changes landscapes through farming, building, quarrying and management.

Must know: A landscape is produced by an interaction between geology, climate, geomorphic processes and people.

Processes that shape landscapes

Weathering and mass movement

Weathering breaks rock down in place. It does not move the material.

ProcessPlain-English meaning
Mechanical weatheringRock is physically broken, for example by repeated freezing and thawing.
Chemical weatheringMinerals in rock react and dissolve or change.
Biological weatheringLiving things break rock, for example roots widening cracks.
SlidingMaterial moves downhill along a flat surface.
SlumpingSaturated material rotates downhill along a curved surface.

Keep the process groups separate

Erosion, transport and deposition

  • Hydraulic action: water or air forces into cracks and breaks material away.
  • Abrasion: carried sediment scrapes the bed, banks or cliff.
  • Attrition: carried rocks collide, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: soluble minerals dissolve in water.
  • Traction: large stones roll along a river bed.
  • Saltation: small stones bounce along the bed.
  • Suspension: fine particles are carried within the water.
  • Deposition: sediment is dropped when water or wind loses energy.

River landscapes

From source to mouth

Upstream, a river usually has a steep gradient, a narrow channel and more vertical erosion. Downstream, tributaries add water, the channel becomes wider and deeper, and transport and deposition become more important.

V-shaped valleys

  1. The river cuts down by vertical erosion.
  2. Weathering weakens the valley sides.
  3. Material moves downhill by mass movement.
  4. The river removes it, leaving a narrow V-shaped valley.

Waterfalls and gorges

  1. Resistant rock lies above softer rock.
  2. Softer rock erodes faster, creating a step.
  3. Hydraulic action and abrasion deepen a plunge pool.
  4. An overhang forms and collapses.
  5. Repeated collapse moves the waterfall upstream and leaves a gorge.

Read the sequence: differential erosion creates the step; undercutting and collapse move it upstream.

Meanders and oxbow lakes

  • Faster water on the outside bend causes erosion and forms a river cliff.
  • Slower water on the inside bend causes deposition and forms a slip-off slope.
  • The neck becomes narrower. During high flow, the river may cut through it.
  • Deposition seals the old bend, producing an oxbow lake.

Formation: erosion narrows the neck, high flow cuts across it, then deposition seals the old bend.

Floodplains and levees

  • During floods, water spreads across the valley floor and loses energy.
  • Fine alluvium is deposited, gradually building a flat floodplain.
  • Coarser material is dropped first beside the channel. Repeated floods build raised banks called levees.

River Wye case study

Place and context

The River Wye is about 251 km long and descends roughly 700 m from upland Wales towards sea level. It rises on Plynlimon in the Cambrian Mountains of mid-Wales, flows through places including Hay-on-Wye, Hereford and Monmouth, and joins the Severn Estuary near Chepstow.

This is a useful whole-river case study because its landscape changes from wet uplands to broad lowlands and then to a deeply cut lower valley.

Geology and relief

Rock resistance: Much of the catchment is underlain by Old Red Sandstone. In the lower Wye, more resistant Carboniferous Limestone helps produce steep wooded gorges around places such as Symonds Yat. Softer alluvium on the floodplains is more easily eroded and deposited.

Slope and drainage: Steep upland slopes encourage rapid runoff and vertical erosion. Gentler downstream relief allows broader floodplains and meanders to develop. Geology can interrupt this general pattern, explaining why a steep gorge occurs in the lower course.

River processes and landforms

Upper Wye near Plynlimon: erosion and valley development: Heavy rainfall and steep slopes produce rapid runoff and energetic flow. Hydraulic action loosens material through the force of water, while abrasion occurs when transported stones scrape the bed. Vertical erosion cuts the channel down. Weathering loosens rock on the valley sides, and mass movement carries it downhill. The river removes this material, helping form a V-shaped valley.

Middle Wye around Hereford: meanders: Tributaries add water and increase discharge. Faster flow on the outside of a bend causes lateral erosion, producing a river cliff. Slower flow on the inside loses energy and deposits sediment, building a slip-off slope. Repeated erosion and deposition move the bends across the valley floor.

Floodplain deposition: When the Wye floods, water spreads beyond the channel and slows down. It deposits alluvium, gradually building the broad, fertile floodplain around places such as Hereford. This explains both the value of the land for farming and its exposure to flooding.

Lower Wye around Symonds Yat and Tintern: river incision: Over time, the Wye has cut down into the resistant Carboniferous Limestone, leaving steep gorge sides and deeply cut bends. This downward cutting is incision. Rock resistance helps the sides remain steep, so a lower-course landscape does not always become an open, gentle valley. Slightly acidic water can also dissolve limestone through solution.

Transport links the landscape: Large stones can move by traction along the bed or saltation in short hops. Fine sediment travels in suspension, while dissolved minerals move in solution. Where flow loses the ability to carry its load, deposition supplies features such as slip-off slopes and floodplains.

Human use and pressure

About 58% of the Wye Valley National Landscape is farmland and about 27% is woodland. Farming shapes field patterns and can compact soil or add sediment and nutrients to water. Settlements, roads, tourism, canoeing and walking bring income but add pressure to habitats and river access.

Water use and pollution: Farmers abstract river water for irrigation. Fertilisers, manure and sediment can enter the river, so farm management must protect water quality as well as maintain food production. Built surfaces speed runoff, while removing vegetation or compacting soil reduces interception and infiltration. Water then reaches channels faster.

Tourism and heritage: Canoeing, fishing and walking depend on the river and its scenery. The annual Hay-on-Wye literature festival brings visitors and spending. Historically, woodland supplied charcoal and timber for shipbuilding, changing vegetation and land use. Removing trees reduces interception and root binding, which can increase runoff, soil erosion and sediment entering the channel. Water abstraction for irrigation can reduce discharge, changing the river's capacity to transport sediment, especially during low flows.

Livox Quarry, south of Tintern, is a named example of limestone extraction in the lower Wye. Quarrying removes rock and vegetation, changes slopes and creates access and transport demands. It also provides useful stone and employment. Restoration can create exposed geological features and wildlife habitats, so the long-term effect depends on how the site is managed.

The physical change can be explained as a chain: rock extraction → altered relief and vegetation → different habitats, runoff and land use.

Flood risk and management

Communities such as Hereford and Monmouth can flood after prolonged rainfall. The published textbook case study estimates around 9,000 properties at particular flood risk in the area; this is an estimate of exposure, not a count of properties flooded in one event. Warnings and local defences reduce immediate risk, but hard defences may be costly and can move water downstream.

The Herefordshire Natural Flood Management pilot (2018–2021) worked with more than 140 landowners. Measures included restoring ponds, planting trees or hedges and using barriers that slow runoff. These store water in the landscape before it reaches the river. Slower runoff can reduce peak discharge, while roots and vegetation trap sediment and help stabilise slopes. Management changes the movement of water and sediment as well as the risk to buildings.

Evaluation

Natural flood management can reduce peak flow, improve habitats and trap sediment. It needs many landowners and a large area, and it cannot prevent every flood during exceptional rainfall. The strongest strategy combines upstream slowing and storage with warnings and targeted protection for settlements. Floodplain zoning also keeps new development away from high-risk land. Tree roots stabilise slopes and reduce erosion, while planting increases interception; neither measure guarantees that flooding will stop.

Exam pointer

Build the chain upland rainfall and steep slopes → rapid runoff and energetic flow → erosion, then show how tributaries, gentler relief and deposition change the landscape downstream. Add the limestone gorge as evidence that geology can interrupt the simple pattern.

Coastal landscapes

Waves and longshore drift

  • Destructive waves have a strong backwash and remove beach material.
  • Constructive waves have a strong swash and build beaches.
  • In longshore drift, swash carries sediment up the beach at the angle of the prevailing wind. Backwash returns straight downhill. The repeated zigzag moves material along the coast.

Two directions: swash follows the approaching wave; gravity brings backwash straight down the beach.

Headlands, bays, caves, arches and stacks

  • Softer rock erodes faster to form a bay; harder rock remains as a headland.
  • Wave refraction concentrates energy on headlands.
  • A crack is enlarged into a cave, then an arch.
  • The arch roof collapses, leaving a stack.

Beaches and spits

  • A beach forms where deposition is greater than removal.
  • A spit forms when longshore drift carries sediment beyond a bend or river mouth.
  • The end may curve as wind or wave direction changes.
  • Sheltered water behind the spit can develop mudflats and salt marsh.

North Norfolk coast case study

Place and context

The North Norfolk coast faces the North Sea. Named places include Cromer, Happisburgh, Blakeney Point and low-lying land beside the Wash. The coast contains cliffs, beaches, spits, salt marsh and areas at risk from both erosion and flooding.

Why the coast is vulnerable

Wave erosion at Happisburgh: The cliffs contain weak glacial deposits of sand, silt, clay and gravel. Hydraulic action uses the force of waves to loosen material, while abrasion occurs as waves throw sand and shingle against the cliff foot. Undercutting removes support from the material above, helping the cliff retreat.

Weathering and mass movement: Rainwater enters the cliff and saturates material, making it heavier and less stable. Slumping and sliding move it down the slope; waves then remove fallen debris and expose the cliff again. Marine erosion and mass movement therefore work together. Chalk, flint and glacial deposits differ in resistance, helping explain variation along the wider coast.

Storm surges and flooding: On 5–6 December 2013, a major North Sea surge affected the east coast, including Blakeney and Wells-next-to-Sea. Low pressure and strong winds raised sea level, and high tides increased the risk of water overtopping low land. Flooding covers land with water, whereas erosion removes material. One storm can cause both, but the processes are different.

Why the impacts matter: Homes, roads, fishing, tourism and farmland are exposed to change. Cromer's concentrated seafront assets help justify protection, while continuous defence is harder to justify on long, less developed stretches. The physical process creates the risk, but land use determines what can be damaged.

Sediment movement and depositional landforms

Longshore drift: Waves approaching at an angle carry sediment diagonally up the beach in the swash. Gravity returns it more directly downhill in the backwash. Repeated movement transports sediment along a stretch of coast. The direction varies around Norfolk: it is westwards along the Blakeney frontage, while material at Happisburgh is generally moved further south. These are not one simple route from Happisburgh to Blakeney.

Blakeney Point: spit growth: A shingle ridge extending from the Weybourne–Cley frontage has grown westwards to form Blakeney Point. Sediment is deposited where waves lose the capacity to carry it, extending the spit. Sheltered water behind the ridge has lower energy, allowing finer sediment to settle as mudflats. Salt-tolerant plants trap more sediment, helping salt marsh develop.

Holkham: wind and dune formation: A wide beach and shallow seabed expose sand at low tide. Wind transports dry sand inland. Marram grass traps it, and roots help bind it, allowing sand dunes to build. Trampling damages vegetation and exposes sand to renewed erosion, so visitor management affects the landform itself.

Sediment supply and protection: Beaches, dunes and salt marshes absorb some wave energy, but depend on sediment and vegetation. Eroding cliffs supply material; groynes and breakwaters can retain it locally and change what moves along the coast. Sediment supply, storm conditions and management therefore determine where erosion or deposition dominates. Stiffkey's salt marsh is another example of vegetation and sediment building a natural coastal buffer.

Management at Cromer

Cromer uses a sea wall, groynes and beach management. The wall protects the promenade, town and infrastructure; groynes retain beach sediment so the wider beach absorbs wave energy.

These defences protect a high-value settlement and tourism economy. They are expensive to maintain, can look intrusive and may reduce sediment moving along the coast.

Other named defences: At Sheringham, rock armour absorbs wave energy. Sea Palling combines a sea wall with offshore rock breakwaters that encourage sediment to collect behind them. These are different structures: the wider beach is linked to the breakwaters and beach management, rather than a sea wall stopping longshore drift by itself.

Adaptation at Happisburgh

Continuous hard defence is harder to justify where the coast is long, weak and less densely developed. At Happisburgh, parts of the coast continue to retreat despite local rock armour reducing wave energy. The rock sill has been repositioned as the cliff recedes. Adaptation includes moving access and facilities away from danger. Property, roads and community identity remain at risk.

This creates conflict: national policy considers cost and sustainability, while residents experience direct loss. Compensation, planning controls, warning and help with relocation can reduce harm but do not stop erosion.

Shoreline planning and evaluation

Shoreline Management Plans look ahead for roughly 100 years and select approaches such as hold the line, managed realignment or no active intervention.

Hard engineering is most defensible where many people, services and economic assets are concentrated. Allowing change elsewhere preserves sediment movement and avoids ever-rising defence costs, but the social cost to smaller communities can be severe. Management should therefore be judged by effectiveness, cost, environmental effects and fairness.

Holkham’s beach and dunes show how deposition and vegetation create a natural buffer. Wind moves dry sand inland; plants such as marram trap sand, and their roots help bind it. The dunes absorb some storm energy and support specialist habitats.

Visitors bring spending but trampling can damage vegetation, exposing loose sand to erosion. Boardwalks and steps concentrate access onto durable routes. This is a softer management approach than a sea wall: it supports natural processes, but needs visitor cooperation and cannot guarantee protection from every storm.

Exam pointer

Use named contrasts: Cromer—hold and protect; Happisburgh—adapt to retreat; Blakeney Point—deposition and habitat. Explain that all three are connected by sediment movement.
