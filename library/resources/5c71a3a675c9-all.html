Landscapes of the UK

The UK landscape

The broad pattern

Think of Britain as a table with a higher, rougher end. The north and west are generally higher, steeper and wetter. The south and east are generally lower, flatter and drier. Local exceptions matter: a lowland region can contain a resistant ridge, and an upland region can contain a broad valley.

Relief describes the height and shape of the land. Geology describes its rocks and their structure. Landscapes develop through the interaction of geology, climate, geomorphic processes and human activity.

LandscapeDistribution and exampleDistinctive characteristics and uses
UplandCommon in Scotland, Wales and northern England; examples include the Highlands, Eryri/Snowdonia, the Lake District and the Pennines. Dartmoor is an upland exception in southern England.High relief, steep slopes and exposed rock are common. Cooler, wetter conditions and thinner soils often favour grazing, forestry and recreation rather than extensive arable farming.
LowlandWidespread in southern and eastern England, including the Fens and the London Basin.Gentler slopes and deeper soils often favour arable farming, settlement and transport. Drainage has made some very low-lying land available for farming, but it can remain exposed to flooding.
GlaciatedIce modified much of Scotland, Wales and northern England, including the Cairngorms and Lake District.Ice widened and deepened valleys and left lakes and deposits. Steep-sided valleys and exposed scenery support tourism, while relief can restrict roads and farming.

Glaciated describes how an area was shaped, rather than a separate height category. An upland landscape can also be glaciated.

Rock type and resistance

Rock groupHow it formsExample and landscape connection
IgneousMolten rock cools and solidifies. Crystals can interlock tightly.Resistant granite helps explain Dartmoor's upland relief.
SedimentarySediments accumulate and become compacted and cemented; some rocks form from organic remains.Clay can be eroded readily, while limestone and sandstone vary in resistance. Limestone contributes to the steep lower Wye valley.
MetamorphicHeat and pressure change an existing rock without melting it.Rocks such as slate occur in upland areas. Their mineral arrangement can influence how they split.

Rock groups are useful background, but not every sedimentary rock is soft. Resistance also depends on cementation, cracks and layers. Water can enter cracks, and weak layers can provide surfaces along which material slips. These details explain a particular landscape more accurately than its rock-group name alone.

People change the physical system

Removing woodland can reduce interception and expose soil to runoff. Quarrying removes rock and changes slopes. Buildings and roads replace vegetation with hard surfaces, allowing rainfall to reach drains and rivers more quickly. Management then changes the system again: planting trees, restoring habitats or building defences can alter runoff, sediment supply and erosion.

Processes that shape landscapes

Weathering breaks rock down in place

Weathering happens where the rock is located. Material may move later through erosion or mass movement, but movement is not part of the definition of weathering.

TypeHow it worksWhat conditions matter?
Mechanical: freeze–thawWater enters a crack. It freezes and expands, putting pressure on the sides. Repeated freezing and thawing widens the crack until pieces break away.Water must be available, and temperature must cross the freezing point repeatedly. Continuous freezing alone does not provide repeated cycles.
ChemicalA reaction changes minerals in the rock. Slightly acidic water can dissolve calcium carbonate in limestone.Water, suitable rock and temperature affect the rate. Natural rainwater can be weakly acidic without industrial pollution.
BiologicalGrowing roots push into cracks and widen them; burrowing organisms can also disturb material.Plants or other organisms must be present. Their activity can prepare material for later removal.

Gravity causes mass movement

Mass movement moves rock or soil downslope under gravity. Rain can add weight and reduce stability; wave or river undercutting can remove support at the foot of a slope.

  • Sliding: a block moves along a relatively flat failure surface, such as a weak layer.
  • Slumping: a mass rotates along a curved failure surface, often leaving a stepped or tilted profile. Saturated weak material is especially vulnerable.

These processes can work together. A cliff is weakened by weathering, undercut by erosion and then collapses or slumps. Water removes the fallen material, exposing the cliff foot to further attack.

Moving water erodes and transports

ProcessWhat happens?Important distinction
Hydraulic actionThe force of moving water loosens material; pressure in cracks helps break it away.Water supplies the force.
AbrasionCarried stones scrape or strike the bed, bank or cliff.The load acts like sandpaper against a surface.
AttritionTransported particles collide, chip and become smaller and rounder.The particles change; this does not directly scrape away the channel itself.
SolutionSoluble minerals dissolve in water.Dissolved material can then be transported invisibly.
Transport processHow material moves
TractionLarge particles roll along the bed.
SaltationSmaller stones bounce along the bed.
SuspensionFine particles are carried within the flowing water.
SolutionDissolved minerals travel in the water.

The method depends on particle size and the strength of the flow. A particle that moves during a flood may be left on the bed when discharge falls.

Deposition lays material down

Deposition occurs when flowing water can no longer transport part of its load. This often happens where flow slows, spreads out or becomes sheltered. Coarser particles usually settle before fine sediment as flow weakens.

Erosion, transport and deposition can occur in different parts of the same river bend. They are not three jobs allocated exclusively to the upper, middle and lower courses.

River landscapes

The drainage basin and river profiles

A drainage basin is the land drained by a river and its tributaries. Its boundary is the watershed, often following high ground. A tributary joins another river at a confluence. The source is where the river begins; the mouth is where it enters the sea, a lake or another river.

The long profile shows height along the river from source to mouth. A cross-profile is a slice across the channel or valley. Do not confuse a river's width with the width of its whole valley.

FeatureTypical upper courseTypical lower course
Gradient and valleyA steeper gradient and a narrow, steep-sided valley are common.A gentler gradient and broader valley are common, although resistant rock can interrupt the pattern.
Channel and dischargeThe channel is usually narrower and shallower, carrying less water.Tributaries add water, so discharge, width and depth generally increase.
Average velocityRoughness and a shallow channel can make friction important.A larger, more efficient channel often has a higher average velocity despite a gentler slope.
ProcessesVertical erosion is important in cutting down into the bed.Lateral erosion, transport and deposition shape bends and floodplains. All can occur together.

These are general patterns, not a claim that every downstream site is deeper or faster than the one before it. Geology, obstacles, flow conditions and management cause local differences.

V-shaped valleys

  1. The river cuts down into its bed through vertical erosion, including hydraulic action and abrasion.
  2. Weathering breaks material from the valley sides.
  3. Gravity moves loosened material downslope.
  4. The river carries it away, allowing continued downcutting and leaving a narrow valley with a V-shaped cross-profile.

Waterfalls and gorges

Where resistant rock lies above less resistant rock, differential erosion can create a step:

  1. The less resistant rock erodes more rapidly. Hydraulic action and abrasion deepen a plunge pool below the fall.
  2. Erosion undercuts the resistant layer, leaving an unsupported overhang.
  3. Gravity causes the overhang to collapse. Fallen rock provides further material for abrasion.
  4. Repeated undercutting and collapse make the waterfall retreat upstream.
  5. The retreat can leave a narrow, steep-sided gorge.

This sequence explains one way to form a gorge. It does not mean every gorge was produced by waterfall retreat: the lower Wye also illustrates river incision into resistant rock.

Meanders and oxbow lakes

At a bend, faster flow against the outside bank promotes lateral erosion, including hydraulic action and abrasion. Undercutting creates a river cliff. Shallower, slower flow on the inside deposits material, building a slip-off slope. Erosion and deposition gradually move and enlarge the bend.

  1. Continued erosion narrows the neck between neighbouring bends.
  2. During high discharge, water can cut through the neck and take a straighter route.
  3. Flow through the old loop decreases. Deposition blocks its entrances.
  4. The isolated former bend becomes an oxbow lake.

Floodplains and natural levees

A floodplain is the broad, relatively flat land beside a river. Lateral erosion and migrating meanders widen the valley floor. When the river overflows, water spreads across this land, slows and deposits alluvium. Repeated floods build layers of fertile sediment.

The abrupt loss of velocity beside the channel causes coarser material to settle first. Finer particles travel farther across the floodplain. Repeated near-channel deposition can build raised banks called natural levees. These are different from artificial embankments built by people.

River Wye case study

Place and context

The River Wye is about 251 km long and descends roughly 700 m from upland Wales towards sea level. It rises on Plynlimon in the Cambrian Mountains of mid-Wales, flows through places including Hay-on-Wye, Hereford and Monmouth, and joins the Severn Estuary near Chepstow.

This is a useful whole-river case study because its landscape changes from wet uplands to broad lowlands and then to a deeply cut lower valley.

Geology and relief

Rock resistance: Much of the catchment is underlain by Old Red Sandstone. In the lower Wye, more resistant Carboniferous Limestone helps produce steep wooded gorges around places such as Symonds Yat. Softer alluvium on the floodplains is more easily eroded and deposited.

Slope and drainage: Steep upland slopes encourage rapid runoff and vertical erosion. Gentler downstream relief allows broader floodplains and meanders to develop. Geology can interrupt this general pattern, explaining why a steep gorge occurs in the lower course.

River processes and landforms

Upper Wye near Plynlimon: erosion and valley development: Heavy rainfall and steep slopes produce rapid runoff and energetic flow. Hydraulic action loosens material through the force of water, while abrasion occurs when transported stones scrape the bed. Vertical erosion cuts the channel down. Weathering loosens rock on the valley sides, and mass movement carries it downhill. The river removes this material, helping form a V-shaped valley.

Middle Wye around Hereford: meanders: Tributaries add water and increase discharge. Faster flow on the outside of a bend causes lateral erosion, producing a river cliff. Slower flow on the inside loses energy and deposits sediment, building a slip-off slope. Repeated erosion and deposition move the bends across the valley floor.

Floodplain deposition: When the Wye floods, water spreads beyond the channel and slows down. It deposits alluvium, gradually building the broad, fertile floodplain around places such as Hereford. This explains both the value of the land for farming and its exposure to flooding.

Lower Wye around Symonds Yat and Tintern: river incision: Over time, the Wye has cut down into the resistant Carboniferous Limestone, leaving steep gorge sides and deeply cut bends. This downward cutting is incision. Rock resistance helps the sides remain steep, so a lower-course landscape does not always become an open, gentle valley. Slightly acidic water can also dissolve limestone through solution.

Transport links the landscape: Large stones can move by traction along the bed or saltation in short hops. Fine sediment travels in suspension, while dissolved minerals move in solution. Where flow loses the ability to carry its load, deposition supplies features such as slip-off slopes and floodplains.

Part of the riverLandscape and processHuman connection
Upper Wye: PlynlimonWet uplands, steep gradients and comparatively narrow valleys encourage rapid runoff and vertical erosion.Grazing and changes in vegetation affect how quickly rainfall reaches the channel.
Middle Wye: Hereford areaA larger discharge and broader valley support meandering, lateral erosion and floodplain deposition.Farming uses fertile alluvium; settlement and roads create assets exposed to flooding.
Lower Wye: Symonds Yat–TinternIncision through limestone produces a steep gorge, so downstream does not always mean a wide, open valley.Woodland, tourism, former quarries and riverside settlements shape land use.

Human use and pressure

About 58% of the Wye Valley National Landscape is farmland and about 27% is woodland. Farming shapes field patterns and can compact soil or add sediment and nutrients to water. Settlements, roads, tourism, canoeing and walking bring income but add pressure to habitats and river access.

Water use and pollution: Farmers abstract river water for irrigation. Fertilisers, manure and sediment can enter the river, so farm management must protect water quality as well as maintain food production. Built surfaces speed runoff, while removing vegetation or compacting soil reduces interception and infiltration. Water then reaches channels faster.

Tourism and heritage: Canoeing, fishing and walking depend on the river and its scenery. The annual Hay-on-Wye literature festival brings visitors and spending. Historically, woodland supplied charcoal and timber for shipbuilding, changing vegetation and land use. Removing trees reduces interception and root binding, which can increase runoff, soil erosion and sediment entering the channel. Water abstraction for irrigation can reduce discharge, changing the river's capacity to transport sediment, especially during low flows.

Livox Quarry, south of Tintern, is a named example of limestone extraction in the lower Wye. Quarrying removes rock and vegetation, changes slopes and creates access and transport demands. It also provides useful stone and employment. Restoration can create exposed geological features and wildlife habitats, so the long-term effect depends on how the site is managed.

The physical change can be explained as a chain: rock extraction → altered relief and vegetation → different habitats, runoff and land use.

Flood risk and management

Communities such as Hereford and Monmouth can flood after prolonged rainfall. The published textbook case study estimates around 9,000 properties at particular flood risk in the area; this is an estimate of exposure, not a count of properties flooded in one event. Warnings and local defences reduce immediate risk, but hard defences may be costly and can move water downstream.

The Herefordshire Natural Flood Management pilot (2018–2021) worked with more than 140 landowners. Measures included restoring ponds, planting trees or hedges and using barriers that slow runoff. These store water in the landscape before it reaches the river. Slower runoff can reduce peak discharge, while roots and vegetation trap sediment and help stabilise slopes. Management changes the movement of water and sediment as well as the risk to buildings.

Evaluation

Natural flood management can reduce peak flow, improve habitats and trap sediment. It needs many landowners and a large area, and it cannot prevent every flood during exceptional rainfall. The strongest strategy combines upstream slowing and storage with warnings and targeted protection for settlements. Floodplain zoning also keeps new development away from high-risk land. Tree roots stabilise slopes and reduce erosion, while planting increases interception; neither measure guarantees that flooding will stop.

Exam pointer

Build the chain upland rainfall and steep slopes → rapid runoff and energetic flow → erosion, then show how tributaries, gentler relief and deposition change the landscape downstream. Add the limestone gorge as evidence that geology can interrupt the simple pattern.

Coastal landscapes

Waves supply energy

Wind transfers energy to the sea surface. Wave size is influenced by wind strength, how long it blows and the fetch: the distance of open water over which the wind blows.

Swash carries water up the beach after a wave breaks. Backwash returns it downhill under gravity.

Wave typeTypical balanceEffect on the beach
ConstructiveSwash is stronger than backwash. Waves are usually lower and less frequent.More material is moved up than removed, helping build the beach.
DestructiveBackwash is stronger than swash. Waves are usually steeper and more frequent.More material is removed from the beach, increasing exposure to erosion.

A coastline can experience different wave conditions at different times. A storm can remove material from a beach that built up during calmer weather.

Longshore drift connects places

  1. Waves approach at an angle, influenced by the prevailing wind.
  2. Swash carries sediment diagonally up the beach.
  3. Gravity returns the backwash more directly down the slope.
  4. Repetition moves sediment along the coast in a zigzag.

The direction must be checked for the particular stretch of coast. A bend in the coastline or a change in wave conditions can change the pattern. Defences can interrupt sediment movement and affect beaches farther along that local route.

Headlands and bays

Where bands of rock with different resistance meet the sea, less resistant rock can erode faster to form a bay. More resistant rock remains projecting into the sea as a headland. Cracks and layers influence the rate as well as the rock type.

As waves approach shallow water around a headland, their direction changes through wave refraction. Energy becomes concentrated on the headland, while the bay is more sheltered. This helps maintain erosion at the headland and deposition within the bay.

Caves, arches and stacks

  1. Hydraulic action and abrasion enlarge a crack in a headland to form a cave.
  2. Continued erosion deepens the cave until it breaks through, forming an arch.
  3. Erosion at the base and weathering above weaken the arch. Its unsupported roof eventually collapses.
  4. A detached pillar remains as a stack. Further erosion can eventually reduce it to a low stump.

The collapse is a consequence of gravity acting on weakened, unsupported rock. Naming the landforms in order is only part of the explanation; the processes connect the stages.

Beaches and spits

A beach is a deposit of sand or shingle. It builds where sediment supply and deposition exceed removal. Sheltered water and constructive-wave conditions often favour accumulation.

A spit is a narrow ridge attached to land at one end. Longshore drift carries material along the coast. At a change in coastline direction or an estuary, sediment continues beyond the land and is deposited as transport weakens. Further supply extends the ridge. Changes in wind and wave direction can produce a recurved end, while river flow or currents may prevent it closing the entrance.

The sheltered water behind a spit has less energy. Fine sediment settles to form mudflats; salt-tolerant plants trap more sediment and help a salt marsh develop. The habitat and the landform therefore depend on both physical deposition and living vegetation.

North Norfolk coast case study

Place and context

The North Norfolk coast faces the North Sea. Named places include Cromer, Happisburgh, Blakeney Point and low-lying land beside the Wash. The coast contains cliffs, beaches, spits, salt marsh and areas at risk from both erosion and flooding.

Why the coast is vulnerable

Wave erosion at Happisburgh: The cliffs contain weak glacial deposits of sand, silt, clay and gravel. Hydraulic action uses the force of waves to loosen material, while abrasion occurs as waves throw sand and shingle against the cliff foot. Undercutting removes support from the material above, helping the cliff retreat.

Weathering and mass movement: Rainwater enters the cliff and saturates material, making it heavier and less stable. Slumping and sliding move it down the slope; waves then remove fallen debris and expose the cliff again. Marine erosion and mass movement therefore work together. Chalk, flint and glacial deposits differ in resistance, helping explain variation along the wider coast.

Storm surges and flooding: On 5–6 December 2013, a major North Sea surge affected the east coast, including Blakeney and Wells-next-to-Sea. Low pressure and strong winds raised sea level, and high tides increased the risk of water overtopping low land. Flooding covers land with water, whereas erosion removes material. One storm can cause both, but the processes are different.

Why the impacts matter: Homes, roads, fishing, tourism and farmland are exposed to change. Cromer's concentrated seafront assets help justify protection, while continuous defence is harder to justify on long, less developed stretches. The physical process creates the risk, but land use determines what can be damaged.

Sediment movement and depositional landforms

Longshore drift: Waves approaching at an angle carry sediment diagonally up the beach in the swash. Gravity returns it more directly downhill in the backwash. Repeated movement transports sediment along a stretch of coast. The direction varies around Norfolk: it is westwards along the Blakeney frontage, while material at Happisburgh is generally moved further south. These are not one simple route from Happisburgh to Blakeney.

Blakeney Point: spit growth: A shingle ridge extending from the Weybourne–Cley frontage has grown westwards to form Blakeney Point. Sediment is deposited where waves lose the capacity to carry it, extending the spit. Sheltered water behind the ridge has lower energy, allowing finer sediment to settle as mudflats. Salt-tolerant plants trap more sediment, helping salt marsh develop.

Holkham: wind and dune formation: A wide beach and shallow seabed expose sand at low tide. Wind transports dry sand inland. Marram grass traps it, and roots help bind it, allowing sand dunes to build. Trampling damages vegetation and exposes sand to renewed erosion, so visitor management affects the landform itself.

Sediment supply and protection: Beaches, dunes and salt marshes absorb some wave energy, but depend on sediment and vegetation. Eroding cliffs supply material; groynes and breakwaters can retain it locally and change what moves along the coast. Sediment supply, storm conditions and management therefore determine where erosion or deposition dominates. Stiffkey's salt marsh is another example of vegetation and sediment building a natural coastal buffer.

Management at Cromer

Cromer uses a sea wall, groynes and beach management. The wall protects the promenade, town and infrastructure; groynes retain beach sediment so the wider beach absorbs wave energy.

These defences protect a high-value settlement and tourism economy. They are expensive to maintain, can look intrusive and may reduce sediment moving along the coast.

Other named defences: At Sheringham, rock armour absorbs wave energy. Sea Palling combines a sea wall with offshore rock breakwaters that encourage sediment to collect behind them. These are different structures: the wider beach is linked to the breakwaters and beach management, rather than a sea wall stopping longshore drift by itself.

Adaptation at Happisburgh

Continuous hard defence is harder to justify where the coast is long, weak and less densely developed. At Happisburgh, parts of the coast continue to retreat despite local rock armour reducing wave energy. The rock sill has been repositioned as the cliff recedes. Adaptation includes moving access and facilities away from danger. Property, roads and community identity remain at risk.

This creates conflict: national policy considers cost and sustainability, while residents experience direct loss. Compensation, planning controls, warning and help with relocation can reduce harm but do not stop erosion.

Shoreline planning and evaluation

Shoreline Management Plans look ahead for roughly 100 years and select approaches such as hold the line, managed realignment or no active intervention.

Hard engineering is most defensible where many people, services and economic assets are concentrated. Allowing change elsewhere preserves sediment movement and avoids ever-rising defence costs, but the social cost to smaller communities can be severe. Management should therefore be judged by effectiveness, cost, environmental effects and fairness.

Holkham’s beach and dunes show how deposition and vegetation create a natural buffer. Wind moves dry sand inland; plants such as marram trap sand, and their roots help bind it. The dunes absorb some storm energy and support specialist habitats.

Visitors bring spending but trampling can damage vegetation, exposing loose sand to erosion. Boardwalks and steps concentrate access onto durable routes. This is a softer management approach than a sea wall: it supports natural processes, but needs visitor cooperation and cannot guarantee protection from every storm.

Exam pointer

Use named contrasts: Cromer—hold and protect; Happisburgh—adapt to retreat; Blakeney Point—deposition and habitat. Explain that all three are connected by sediment movement.
