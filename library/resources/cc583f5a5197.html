Ethical legal and environmental impacts questions & answers

1 mark2 marks3 marks4 marks6 marks9 marks

1 mark

1. Which kind of issue concerns whether a decision is fair or morally responsible?

Answer:

Ethical.

2. Which kind of issue concerns whether an action meets the law?

Answer:

Legal.

3. Which kind of issue concerns waste, energy use or damage to habitats?

Answer:

Environmental.

4. What is personal data?

Answer:

Information relating to an identifiable person.

5. What is a wearable device?

Answer:

A computing device worn on the body.

6. What is cloud storage?

Answer:

Storing data on remote servers accessed through a network.

7. What does unauthorised access mean?

Answer:

Access to a system without permission.

8. Name one action that can reduce electronic waste.

Answer:

Repair.

2 marks

9. Explain why legal conduct can still raise an ethical concern.

Answer:
  • Law and ethics answer different questions: compliance with rules does not settle fairness.
  • For example, a workplace monitoring arrangement could meet legal requirements yet still create intrusive pressure on staff.

10. A walking app stores every user's movements indefinitely. Explain a privacy risk.

Answer:
  • Location history can reveal someone's home or routine.
  • Keeping a large identifiable record increases what could be exposed or misused if access is compromised.

11. Explain data minimisation using a homework app.

Answer:
  • Collect only information needed for the purpose.
  • For example, pupil identity and assignment submissions may be needed, while continuous location tracking is unnecessary.

12. Explain one way mobile technology can improve access to services.

Answer:
  • A person can use a phone while away from a fixed computer.
  • This lets them contact support, check directions or use a learning service wherever a suitable connection is available.

13. Explain one reason cloud storage might interrupt a business's work.

Answer:
  • Access depends on the provider and a working network connection.
  • An outage can prevent staff reaching the files needed for their tasks.

14. Explain one security issue associated with an unsecured wireless network.

Answer:
  • Traffic or access may be inadequately protected.
  • An attacker could intercept information or reach resources they should not be able to use.

15. Explain why replacing an implant's software requires particular care.

Answer:
  • The device may support an important bodily function.
  • A faulty update could interrupt that function, so reliable testing and safe maintenance matter.

16. Explain one employment impact of autonomous vehicles.

Answer:
  • Some driving roles could be reduced or changed.
  • Workers may need retraining, even if new maintenance or software jobs are created.

17. Explain why a biometric health record is a privacy concern even if the company removes the user's name.

Answer:
  • Other information may still allow the person to be identified.
  • Removing a name alone does not necessarily make sensitive data anonymous.

18. Explain why an authorised security test differs from illegal hacking.

Answer:
  • The tester has permission and works within an agreed scope.
  • Unauthorised access lacks that permission, even when the person claims a helpful intention.

3 marks

19. A company offers staff fitness trackers and plans to share individual activity data with managers. Explain an ethical concern and a safeguard.

Answer:
  • Employees may feel pressured to participate because the employer has power over them.
  • Activity comparisons could unfairly disadvantage staff with different needs or circumstances.
  • A meaningful alternative and tightly limited access reduce pressure and unrelated use.

20. Explain how repairable devices can reduce environmental harm.

Answer:
  • Repair keeps useful equipment operating for longer.
  • This can reduce the need to mine materials and manufacture replacements.
  • It also reduces electronic waste, although running energy use still matters.

21. A government argues that it needs access to private communications to prevent attacks. Explain its argument and a concern for citizens.

Answer:
  • Access may help investigators identify threats or connections between suspects.
  • Broad monitoring also collects information about people who have done nothing wrong.
  • That information could be misused or exposed, reducing their privacy.

22. Explain why a mobile-only public service could disadvantage some users.

Answer:
  • Some users cannot afford a suitable device or data connection.
  • Others may have limited coverage or difficulty using the app.
  • They could be excluded from an essential service, so an accessible alternative is needed.

4 marks

23. Evaluate a school storing pupil work in the cloud.

Answer:
  • Pupils and staff can access and share work from different locations.
  • Off-site storage may support recovery if local equipment is lost, when suitable backup arrangements exist.
  • Incorrect sharing permissions or compromised accounts can expose work and personal information.
  • It is useful if accounts, access and recovery arrangements are managed well; provider security alone does not protect a publicly shared folder.

24. A smartwatch reports an unexpectedly high reading. Explain two risks of making decisions from unverified readings.

Answer:
  • A reading may be inaccurate because of the sensor or how it is used.
  • Treating it as a diagnosis could create unnecessary worry or an inappropriate response.
  • An employer or insurer could use misleading data to make an unfair judgement about somebody.
  • The data needs appropriate verification and limits on its use; it is not a complete medical assessment.

25. Explain environmental impacts at three stages in a phone's lifecycle and one way to reduce them.

Answer:
  • Manufacture: mining materials can damage habitats; production uses energy.
  • Use: the phone and the services it accesses require electricity.
  • Disposal: unsuitable handling of electronic waste can release harmful substances.
  • Keeping the phone supported and repaired for longer can reduce replacement manufacture and waste.

26. Explain why switching every meeting online does not automatically remove environmental harm.

Answer:
  • Fewer journeys can reduce travel-related emissions.
  • However, computers and network equipment still require manufacture.
  • Running the devices, networks and data centres uses energy.
  • The net effect depends on the travel avoided and the equipment and electricity used.

27. A connected medical implant sends identifiable readings to a clinic. Explain two benefits or risks of this connection.

Answer:
  • Remote readings could help the clinic notice a problem without requiring every check in person.
  • This can improve convenience and support timely review.
  • Transmitted health information is sensitive and could be exposed by inadequate protection.
  • Secure access and reliable operation are needed because unauthorised control or an interrupted service may have serious consequences.

28. Evaluate giving public Wi-Fi access in a town centre.

Answer:
  • It may help residents and visitors reach information without using their own mobile data.
  • This improves access for some people, but still requires a suitable device.
  • Poorly protected connections or excessive tracking create security and privacy risks.
  • The service is more beneficial if access is inclusive, protection is suitable and unnecessary personal data is not collected.

6 marks

29. A council is considering autonomous buses. Discuss legal and ethical issues it should consider.

Answer:

The buses could improve access for people unable to drive and reduce some errors associated with tired human drivers. However, safe operation depends on reliable sensors and software within appropriate conditions. A system that struggles in bad weather could put passengers and other road users at risk.

The council must consider the applicable approval, insurance and responsibility rules, rather than assuming either the operator or manufacturer is always liable. Cameras and journey records may affect the privacy of passengers and bystanders. Changes to driving jobs also have consequences for workers.

A suitable decision depends on demonstrated safety, clear operating limits, responsible data use and arrangements for affected staff. Technical novelty alone is not a reason to introduce the buses.

9 marks

30. A technology company plans to sell fitness watches that store identifiable health and location data in the cloud. Discuss benefits, privacy risks and legal responsibilities.

Answer:

The watches could help users recognise activity patterns and set goals. Cloud storage makes records accessible from different devices and may support sharing with somebody the user chooses. However, location history can reveal routines, while health-related records expose sensitive information. A breach could therefore harm users beyond simply losing a file.

The company needs an appropriate lawful basis, clear purposes and transparent explanations. It should collect only necessary information, protect access, keep data appropriately accurate and avoid retaining identifiable records without need. Consent is one possible basis, not a universal substitute for all these duties. Sharing records with advertisers or other organisations needs its own appropriate justification.

Useful safeguards include strong account protection, limited permissions and clear retention arrangements. The product is more defensible if people can understand and control relevant choices without being forced to accept unnecessary tracking. Convenience does not justify unrestricted collection or unrelated use.

31. A government proposes keeping all residents' location records to improve security. Evaluate the proposal.

Answer:

Location records may help investigators reconstruct journeys and identify connections relevant to an attack. Targeted access can therefore support public safety. However, keeping everyone's movements also exposes the routines of people who are not suspected of wrongdoing. The scale of the collection increases the consequences of misuse or a breach and may discourage lawful activity if people feel constantly watched.

The government must consider necessity, proportionality and applicable legal safeguards. Independent oversight, restricted access, defined purposes and limited retention can reduce risk. These safeguards do not make every collection justified: the government still needs to explain why less intrusive approaches would not meet the security aim.

I would favour justified, targeted access with effective oversight over indiscriminate permanent collection. This recognises a real security benefit while placing limits on the loss of privacy. The conclusion depends on the evidence for the need, not simply on a claim that more data must always mean greater safety.
