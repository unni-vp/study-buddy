Text A — Household economy

Selected passages from Lydia M. Child, *The American Frugal Housewife* (1832), as reproduced in the Eduqas sample assessment materials. The numbered paragraphs are separate selections, in their original order.

A1. The true economy of housekeeping is simply the art of gathering up all the fragments, so that nothing be lost. I mean fragments of time, as well as materials. 'Time is money.' For this reason, cheap as stockings are, it is good economy to knit them. Cotton and woollen yarn are both cheap; stockings that are knit wear twice as long as woven ones; and they can be done at odd minutes of time, which would not be otherwise employed.

A2. Nothing should be thrown away so long as it is possible to make any use of it, however trifling that use may be; and whatever be the size of a family, every member should be employed either in earning or saving money. Buy merely enough to get along with at first. It is only by experience that you can tell what will be the wants of your family. If you spend all your money, you will find you have purchased many things you do not want, and have no means left to get many things which you do want.

A3. Economy is generally despised as a low virtue, tending to make people ungenerous and selfish. This is true of avarice; but it is not so of economy. The man who is economical, is laying up for himself the permanent power of being useful and generous.

Text B — Before we replace it

An original modern opinion article about repair and waste.

B1. The coat needed a button. I gave it an entire shopping trip. Somewhere between noticing the gap and reaching the checkout, I had decided that a new coat was the simpler answer. Only later, hanging it beside the old one, did I see how ridiculous that decision was. The weather had not defeated my coat. A few loose threads had defeated my patience.

B2. Replacement is wonderfully easy to imagine. A photograph promises the finished object, delivered and ready. Repair asks an awkward question: what exactly is wrong? We may have to learn a small skill, find a part or ask somebody for help. Yet a missing button is not a ruined coat. Treating the two as the same can make us spend money while discarding materials and work that still have value. In my case, my neighbour offered a needle and showed me how to use it. The button came from a tin already in her kitchen.

B3. This is not a demand to keep every broken object forever. A drawer full of dead chargers is not a repair strategy. Some items are unsafe to mend without a qualified person; others cost more to repair than the owner can reasonably afford. Nor does everybody have spare hours or the same access to advice. Turning repair into a test of moral purity would make a sensible idea needlessly hostile. A practical invitation should not become another way to blame people who already have very little choice.

B4. But we could make the first question smaller. Before asking, “Which new one shall I buy?”, ask, “Could this one work again?” Libraries and community groups can help by sharing tools and knowledge. Manufacturers can help by making parts available and objects easier to take apart. Individuals have a role, but they should not have to solve a design problem with nothing more than good intentions. A useful repair culture needs support as well as enthusiasm.

B5. My old coat is back by the door. Its new button does not quite match, which gives me a useful reminder every time I fasten it. I am not against buying things. I am against forgetting to look at what we already have. Sometimes a better decision begins with a smaller question, a borrowed needle and the patience to try.

1 mark

1. In Text A, what does “economy” mean?

Answer:

Careful use of resources.

2. According to A1, how much longer do knitted stockings last than woven ones?

Answer:

Twice as long.

3. What was wrong with the coat in B1?

Answer:

A missing button.

4. Who helped the writer repair the coat?

Answer:

A neighbour.

5. In B3, what kind of person may be needed to repair an unsafe item?

Answer:

A qualified person.

6. Which two groups, besides individuals, are asked to help in B4?

Answer:

Libraries or community groups, and manufacturers.

2 marks

7. Explain what Child means by “Time is money” in A1.

Answer:

Time has value because it can be used to earn or save money. Child therefore argues that spare minutes should be put to useful work, such as knitting.

8. Give two reasons Child recommends knitting stockings.

Answer:
  • They last longer than woven stockings.
  • Knitting can use otherwise unoccupied minutes.

9. What does Child suggest may happen if a family spends all its money at first?

Answer:

It may buy things it does not need and then have no money left for things it does need.

10. Explain the difference Child makes between economy and avarice in A3.

Answer:

Economy is careful management that preserves the ability to help others. Avarice is greed, which she associates with selfishness.

11. Give two practical limits to repair acknowledged in B3.

Answer:
  • Some repairs are unsafe without a qualified person.
  • Some cost more than the owner can reasonably afford.

Lack of time or access to advice is another supported limit.

12. What does “a few loose threads had defeated my patience” suggest about the modern writer?

Answer:

The writer recognises that impatience, rather than serious damage, caused the unnecessary purchase. The self-critical admission makes the writer seem willing to question their own behaviour.

3 marks

13. Using A1 and A2, identify three ways Child says household resources can be used carefully.

Answer:
  • Knit stockings during spare minutes.
  • Keep objects that can still be used.
  • Buy only enough to begin with, then let experience guide further purchases.

14. Using B3 and B4, identify three forms of support that could make repair more practical.

Answer:
  • Qualified help for unsafe repairs.
  • Shared tools or knowledge through libraries and community groups.
  • Available parts and designs that are easier to take apart.

4 marks

15. Using both texts, explain why the writers encourage people to avoid unnecessary waste.

Answer:
  • Child says objects may still have a useful purpose, even a small one.
  • She argues that careful initial spending leaves money for the family’s actual needs.
  • The modern writer says unnecessary replacement spends money while discarding materials and work that still have value.
  • Repair can make an existing object usable again, as the coat example shows.

16. Using both texts, explain how people can reduce unnecessary spending.

Answer:
  • Child recommends knitting durable stockings.
  • She advises buying only enough initially and learning what the household really needs.
  • The modern writer recommends checking whether an existing object could work again before buying a replacement.
  • Borrowed tools and shared skills can make a repair possible without an unnecessary new purchase.

17. Explain how the structure of Text B develops its argument.

Answer:

The opening anecdote admits an unnecessary purchase, making the issue personal and ordinary. The middle broadens the problem but acknowledges practical barriers, preventing the argument from sounding absolute. It then proposes shared support. Returning to the repaired coat gives the final recommendation a concrete example and a sense of completion.

18. Compare the writers’ attitudes to useful possessions in one developed paragraph.

Answer:

Both value an object’s remaining usefulness. Child’s absolute “Nothing” makes keeping usable things a strict rule, extended even to a “trifling” use. The modern writer similarly objects to discarding materials and work that “still have value”, but rejects keeping every broken object forever. The concession makes this view more selective than Child’s firm command.

10 marks

19. How does Child try to persuade householders to manage resources carefully? Refer to Text A.

Answer:

Child begins by redefining household economy as “gathering up all the fragments”. The image makes small pieces of time and material seem recoverable rather than worthless. Extending the idea to time encourages readers to see spare moments as a resource.

The compact saying “Time is money” gives that argument a memorable form. She then makes it practical through stockings: their greater durability and the possibility of knitting in otherwise unused minutes offer clear reasons for the work. This combines a firm principle with an everyday example.

Her instruction that “Nothing should be thrown away” uses an absolute to discourage exceptions. The qualification “however trifling” actually strengthens the demand, because even slight remaining usefulness becomes a reason to keep something. Requiring “every member” to earn or save presents economy as a shared household responsibility.

Child also links the imperative “Buy merely enough” to a consequence readers can understand. Buying unnecessary things may leave no money for necessities. That explanation appeals to practical self-interest, rather than relying only on obedience.

Finally, she anticipates the objection that economy is selfish. Contrasting it with “avarice” allows her to present careful management as the source of a “permanent power” to be generous. The ending gives saving a moral purpose as well as a financial one.

20. How does the writer of Text B encourage readers to consider repair before replacement?

Answer:

The article starts with the deliberately disproportionate contrast between a missing button and “an entire shopping trip”. This makes an ordinary purchase look wasteful without beginning by accusing the reader. The admission that “loose threads had defeated my patience” directs criticism at the writer’s own convenience first.

The next paragraph contrasts the easy picture of replacement with repair’s “awkward question”. That phrase recognises why people hesitate. Yet “a missing button is not a ruined coat” reduces the problem to its actual size, exposing the leap from a small defect to replacing the whole object. The neighbour and borrowed needle then show a manageable alternative.

The writer qualifies the argument in B3. The comic image of “a drawer full of dead chargers” rejects pointless hoarding, while the references to safety, cost and limited time recognise serious barriers. Refusing a “test of moral purity” presents repair as a practical possibility rather than a way to shame people.

In B4, the paired questions move readers from choosing a new product to reconsidering an existing one. Inclusive “we” invites participation, but responsibilities are also given to libraries, community groups and manufacturers. This prevents the solution from resting entirely on individual effort.

The ending returns to the coat with its mismatched button. Its imperfection makes the example believable and memorable. The final list of a small question, a needle and patience leaves readers with modest actions, matching the article’s invitation to try.

21. What do you think and feel about Child’s advice? Support your evaluation from Text A.

Answer:

Child’s advice seems useful when it connects thrift to the needs of a household. “Buy merely enough” is followed by an explanation that experience reveals what a family actually needs. I find this persuasive because restraint is given a practical purpose: keeping money for necessities instead of spending it too early.

The stocking example also makes the argument concrete. Durability and the use of spare minutes explain why an activity might be worthwhile even when ready-made stockings are cheap. However, the extract does not explore whether every household has the same skills or time, so the example may not suit everyone equally.

Her absolute “Nothing” and insistence that “every member” should earn or save make the advice feel demanding. Even a “trifling” use is enough to justify keeping something. That may encourage resourcefulness, but it leaves little space for the burden of storing things or for rest. I therefore find the tone firmer than the circumstances always seem to justify.

The final distinction between economy and “avarice” makes her position more sympathetic. She does not present greed as a virtue: the point of saving is the continuing ability to be “useful and generous”. Overall, I value the careful planning and concern for future needs while questioning the inflexibility of some commands.

22. A reader says: “The modern writer is judgemental and unrealistic.” To what extent do you agree? Use Text B.

Answer:

The writer certainly judges unnecessary replacement critically. Comparing a missing button with “an entire shopping trip” exposes an excessive response, and “defeated my patience” suggests that convenience can win over thought. However, this criticism begins with the writer’s own mistake rather than an attack on less fortunate readers. The self-critical voice makes the judgement less superior.

The argument becomes explicitly more realistic in B3. It recognises unsafe repairs, unaffordable costs and limited spare time. Rejecting a “test of moral purity” challenges the idea that repairing everything proves a person’s goodness. This directly limits the claim that the article unfairly judges everyone.

B4 also acknowledges that willingness alone is insufficient. Manufacturers are asked to make parts available and designs easier to dismantle. The phrase “nothing more than good intentions” exposes the weakness of expecting individuals to overcome practical design barriers on their own.

The personal success with a neighbour and a needle cannot demonstrate that every repair will be easy. Nevertheless, the article does not claim that it does. Its final invitation is to ask a question and try where possible. I therefore agree that the writer criticises thoughtless buying, but the concessions and shared responsibilities make “unrealistic” an unfair description of the argument as a whole.

23. Compare the writers’ attitudes to waste and how they convey those attitudes. Use both texts.

Answer:

Both writers oppose discarding things that remain useful, but Child presents a stricter duty. “Nothing should be thrown away” is an absolute instruction, strengthened by “however trifling”. The modern writer also values materials and work that “still have value”, yet explicitly rejects keeping every broken object forever. This concession produces a more flexible attitude to the same concern.

Both make their arguments concrete through household examples. Child uses stockings to connect durability and spare time with saving. The modern article uses a personal mistake about a coat, creating a self-critical and conversational voice. Child’s example supports a general instruction, whereas the coat story invites readers to reconsider a familiar decision alongside the writer.

They also differ in how they distribute responsibility. Child says “every member” of a family should earn or save, presenting household effort as a firm expectation. The modern writer widens responsibility to libraries, community groups and manufacturers. The warning about “good intentions” recognises that individuals may be unable to repair an object without practical support.

Neither argument is simply a celebration of having more money. Child contrasts economy with “avarice” and says saving preserves generosity. The modern writer opposes a “test of moral purity” and refuses to blame people with little choice. Both anticipate a moral objection, but Child defends thrift as a virtue while the modern writer limits the moral demands placed on repair. Their arguments share a concern for usefulness while differing in certainty, tone and flexibility.

24. Compare how the two writers make careful use of resources seem worthwhile.

Answer:

Both link avoiding waste to practical benefits. Child argues that knitted stockings “wear twice as long”, making durability a reason to use spare minutes productively. The modern writer shows a repaired coat becoming usable again with a borrowed needle. Both use a familiar object to make an abstract principle understandable.

Child also warns about the consequences of spending everything at once: a household may lack money for what it actually needs. The modern article similarly connects unnecessary replacement with spending money, but adds the materials and work already invested in the object. The emphasis broadens from household management to the resources embodied in a product.

Their tones make usefulness attractive in different ways. Child’s imperative “Buy merely enough” sounds direct and confident. The modern writer’s paired questions invite readers to change the first decision they make, while the self-critical opening admits that this change is needed by the writer too. One offers firm instruction; the other models reconsideration.

Finally, Child presents the lasting ability to be “useful and generous” as a reward for economy. The modern ending offers the modest satisfaction of a working coat and a reminder to look again. Neither promises perfection, but Child stresses an enduring moral benefit while the modern article closes on a small, repeatable action.
