Listening and dictation

Listening for the requested detail

Listening is like following directions: you do not need to repeat every word, but you must identify the right person, action, time and detail.

The paper lasts about 45 minutes, including five minutes to read at the start and two minutes to check at the end. Comprehension is 40 marks; dictation is 10 marks. In comprehension, each item is played twice. Answers are in English or use the requested non-verbal response.

Before an item, identify the question's target: a reason, an activity, a time, a place or an opinion. A question asking why needs a reason, not just the activity you heard.

On the first play, establish the message and note a possible answer. On the second, check the precise detail, speaker and any negative or contrast. Do not keep rewriting an earlier answer while the next item starts.

Use reading time without deciding the answer in advance

Underline the kind of information each question requests. For a reason, prepare to listen for because or a consequence. For a price, leave space for a number and check its unit. Note two spaces when two details are required.

Predicting the kind of answer can direct attention, but a plausible guess is not evidence. If the recording contradicts your expectation, follow the recording. The first familiar topic word may belong to a rejected plan or another speaker.

Changes, opinions and distractors

The first familiar word is not always the answer. A speaker can change their plan or contrast past and present.

Je voulais aller au cinéma, mais maintenant je préfère rester à la maison.

I wanted to go to the cinema, but now I prefer to stay at home. The current preference is staying home; cinema describes the earlier wish.

Mon frère aime le foot, mais moi, je préfère le tennis. — My brother likes football, but I prefer tennis. Match each opinion to its owner.

La chambre était petite, mais propre et calme. — The room was small, but clean and quiet. This is a mixed opinion, not completely negative.

Numbers and precise English answers

Check quinze/cinquante (15/50), treize/trente (13/30), dates, prices and times. If a number is corrected, use the final one.

Le train part à neuf heures, pardon, à neuf heures et demie. — The train leaves at nine, sorry, at half past nine. The departure is 9:30.

Include the necessary qualifier:

  • trop cher = too expensive, not just expensive.
  • moins souvent = less often, not never.
  • pas assez de bus = not enough buses, not no buses.
  • à cause du bruit = because of the noise, not because it is busy.

If asked for two things, give two clear answers. Extra contradictory guesses can spoil the meaning. English spelling should communicate the meaning; French dictation has a separate accuracy requirement.

How dictation works

Dictation means writing the French you hear, rather than translating it. Each sentence is played three times:

  1. The complete sentence: understand the overall message.
  2. Short sections: write the words and their order.
  3. The complete sentence again: check missing words and agreement.

The Higher task contains at least 30 words, including two words outside the vocabulary list. Use sound–spelling patterns to attempt those words rather than leaving gaps immediately.

The response is marked across the extracts as a whole: 5 marks for communicating the meaning and 5 for transcription and grammatical accuracy. It is not automatically one mark per word or one fixed deduction per error.

Recovering silent letters

Sound and grammar work together like two clues to the same answer.

Mes amies sont arrivées hier. — My female friends arrived yesterday.

  • Mes and amies signal a plural noun.
  • Sont is the plural auxiliary.
  • Arrivées takes feminine plural agreement after être.
  • Hier identifies the finished past event.

J'ai regardé un film. The heard /e/ ending could have several written spellings, but after j'ai a past participle is needed: regardé, not regarder.

Je vais regarder un film. After vais, the next verb is an infinitive: regarder, not regardé.

Les enfants jouent. The -s of enfants and -ent of jouent may be silent; the plural subject still determines the spelling.

Final checks

  • a / à, ou / où, et / est: use the sentence meaning.
  • -er / -é / -ez: check the verb's job and subject.
  • le / la / les, un / une: check the noun.
  • Apostrophes: j'ai, l'école, d'eau.
  • Negatives: both parts where required.
  • Plural and feminine endings, including after être.
  • Word order and any omitted small words.

A sound does not reveal every ending

Elles sont arrivées needs a feminine plural participle because elles supplies that information. By contrast, an isolated sound such as ami/amie does not tell you the gender in writing. Use the surrounding words and any supplied context, rather than claiming that silent letters can all be heard.

Work in two passes: first preserve the words and message; then check grammar. For Nous avons visité le musée, the conjugated auxiliary shows that visité is a participle. For Nous allons visiter le musée, allons requires the infinitive visiter.

If one word is unfamiliar, attempt its spelling from the sound pattern and continue. A single uncertain word should not make you miss the rest of the sentence.

Practice with real audio

The AQA sound bank provides recorded vocabulary for sound–spelling practice. The assessment resources provide listening papers and available recordings. Select the matching recording and paper; keep the transcript hidden until after attempting an item.

The sample question paper and answer scheme are saved locally. The audio links require internet access.

After a difficult item, identify the missed clue: sound, word meaning, negative, time frame or speaker. Replay the phrase and connect it to its spelling. Reading a transcript helps explain a mistake, but hearing the phrase is what trains listening.
