Measurements, errors and improvements

Choose equipment appropriate to the quantity and required resolution: balance for mass, ruler for length, thermometer for temperature, measuring cylinder/pipette for volume, timer for time and calibrated probe for pH. Check zero, read scales at eye level and record units in table headings.

TermMeaningUseful response
AccuracyClose to the true valueCalibrate and correct systematic offsets
PrecisionRepeated values close togetherImprove measurement resolution and consistency
RepeatabilitySame investigator/method gives similar resultsRepeat under the same conditions
ReproducibilityDifferent investigators/equipment give similar resultsCompare independent investigations
Random errorUnpredictable scatterRepeat and calculate a mean
Systematic errorConsistent bias or offsetFind the cause and recalibrate/correct it

Systematic error: a balance that always reads 0.2 g too high keeps that offset when readings are repeated. Check and correct its zero or calibration.

Unexpected results: investigate an anomaly and repeat the measurement. Exclude it only with a justified reason, rather than because it spoils a preferred pattern.

Uncertainty: report variation, such as the range of repeats. Consider both the instrument's resolution and the spread of the readings.

Resolution and uncertainty are not the same

Resolution is the smallest change the instrument can distinguish. Uncertainty describes the interval within which the true value is considered likely to lie. A fine display cannot remove a badly calibrated instrument's systematic error.

Suppose repeated endpoint times are 48 s, 52 s and 50 s.

  1. Mean = (48 + 52 + 50) ÷ 3 = 50 s.
  2. Range = 52 − 48 = 4 s.
  3. If the question asks for half-range uncertainty, use ±2 s.

The half-range describes this observed spread; it is not a universal rule that every instrument has ±2 s uncertainty. Repeats estimate random variation, while a calibration check addresses a consistent offset.
