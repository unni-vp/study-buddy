A Christmas Carol questions & answers

1 mark

1. How many staves make up the novella?

Answer:

Five.

2. Who invites Scrooge to Christmas dinner in Stave 1?

Answer:

Fred.

3. Who is Scrooge's clerk?

Answer:

Bob Cratchit.

4. Which ghost first warns Scrooge about his conduct?

Answer:

Jacob Marley.

5. Who shows Scrooge his childhood?

Answer:

The Ghost of Christmas Past.

6. Who ends an engagement because Scrooge puts wealth above love?

Answer:

Belle.

7. Who is the generous employer remembered in Stave 2?

Answer:

Fezziwig.

8. Which two children does the second Christmas spirit reveal?

Answer:

Ignorance and Want.

9. Which Christmas spirit does not speak?

Answer:

Christmas Yet to Come.

10. What does Scrooge promise to raise at the end?

Answer:

Bob's salary.

2 marks

11. Explain what Marley's chain symbolises.

Answer:
  • Its business objects connect it with his selfish priorities while alive.
  • As a burden he created himself, it represents the consequences of repeated moral choices.

12. Explain why Scrooge's neglected childhood matters.

Answer:
  • It gives roots to his later isolation and helps restore his capacity to feel.
  • Explaining his pain does not excuse the harm he causes as an adult.

13. Explain why Fezziwig is a contrast to Scrooge as an employer.

Answer:
  • He uses his position to give workers enjoyment and a sense of belonging.
  • Scrooge initially restricts Bob's comfort and views him mainly as a cost.

14. Explain why the Cratchits' cheerful meal does not prove poverty is harmless.

Answer:
  • Affection makes the family happy despite limited resources.
  • Their food, clothes and Tiny Tim's uncertain future still reveal material deprivation.

15. Explain how the Ghost of Christmas Present makes Scrooge confront his earlier population argument.

Answer:
  • It repeats his own cruel words when discussing Tiny Tim's possible death.
  • The echo makes an abstract judgement apply to a child whom Scrooge now values.

16. Explain why the predicted future is not presented as unavoidable.

Answer:
  • Scrooge asks whether the visions show what must happen or what may happen.
  • His changed actions produce a different outcome, including Tiny Tim's survival.

17. Explain one function of Fred in the novella.

Answer:
  • His repeated invitation offers belonging despite Scrooge's rejection.
  • His generosity contrasts with his uncle's assumption that Christmas must produce financial profit.

18. Give two actions that demonstrate Scrooge's lasting change.

Answer:
  • He raises Bob's salary and supports his family.
  • He gives to charity and joins Fred's celebration.

19. Explain why the chapter name staves is meaningful.

Answer:
  • Staves are associated with musical notation.
  • The naming connects the narrative to a carol and the shared celebration Scrooge must learn to value.

20. Explain the difference between Tiny Tim's death in the vision and the ending.

Answer:
  • The future vision shows the grief that could follow if conditions remain unchanged.
  • In the ending, Scrooge helps and Tiny Tim survives.

4 marks

21. Analyse the simile solitary as an oyster.

Answer:

The simile “solitary as an oyster” makes Scrooge's isolation seem like a hard protective shell. It suggests that he resists emotional contact, not simply that he happens to live alone. The potential pearl inside can also support an interpretation of hidden value later revealed by his transformation. The image therefore allows both criticism of his closure and hope for change.

22. Analyse Belle's statement that another idol has displaced her.

Answer:

The religious metaphor “Another idol has displaced me” presents wealth as an object of worship. The verb suggests that Scrooge's devotion to money has pushed love out of its previous place. Dickens makes the change about his deepest priorities, not just his bank balance. Belle's departure turns that abstract choice into a visible personal loss.

23. Analyse how Ignorance is presented.

Answer:

The boy is an allegorical figure, embodying lack of education and understanding. His frail appearance turns a social problem into a vulnerable child whom Scrooge must face. The spirit's warning enlarges the harm beyond one person: neglect threatens the wider community. Dickens therefore makes education a shared duty rather than an optional act of kindness.

24. Analyse the image of a crutch without an owner.

Answer:

The phrase “a crutch without an owner” represents a child's possible death through an ordinary object left behind. Its symbolic absence invites the reader to imagine the missing person and the family's grief. It also challenges Scrooge's earlier impersonal view of the poor, because one valued child's loss now feels specific and painful.

25. Analyse the contrast between coldness and warmth.

Answer:

Scrooge's cold office and chilly description connect material restriction with emotional hostility. Shared fires, meals and gatherings offer warmth alongside affection. This recurring contrast makes generosity tangible, while showing that wealth alone does not create belonging. In the ending, Scrooge's changed participation reverses the earlier pattern of shutting others out.

26. Analyse the simile as light as a feather near the end.

Answer:

“as light as a feather” suggests release from the emotional weight of Scrooge's former life. The simile contrasts with Marley's heavy chain, linking joy with freedom from selfish priorities. Its place after the frightening future vision also marks a tonal shift towards possibility. The subsequent actions confirm that this feeling leads to practical change.

6 marks

27. Explain how the spirits' order supports Scrooge's transformation.

Answer:

The Ghost of Christmas Past makes Scrooge face pain, earlier kindness and choices that cost him love. The Ghost of Christmas Present then shows the effect of present inequality and makes Tiny Tim an individual worth caring about. The silent future spirit reveals where unchanged conduct could lead: lonely death and family grief. This sequence develops regret, empathy and urgency. The conversion therefore grows through different forms of understanding, rather than coming only from fear of his grave.

28. Explain how Dickens uses the narrator to shape the reader's response.

Answer:

The narrator begins with a forceful, often comic condemnation of Scrooge and makes his coldness vivid through accumulated description. Later, emotive accounts of childhood and the Cratchits encourage sympathy for vulnerable people. Direct address draws readers into the moral argument, while humour in the ending makes change attractive rather than merely dutiful. These shifts help the reader judge selfishness but also welcome recovery. The narrator guides response without removing the evidence of Scrooge's earlier harm.

29. Explain how relevant historical context strengthens an argument about poverty.

Answer:

The novella appeared in 1843, when industrial growth coexisted with severe deprivation. Scrooge's references to workhouses connect his attitude with a system intended to make poor relief unattractive. His population language echoes arguments that turn poor people into an excess number. Dickens counters this distance with the Cratchits' individual relationships and Tiny Tim's vulnerability. Context is useful here because it explains the attitudes challenged by particular words and events, rather than replacing close analysis with a history paragraph.

40 marks

30. Starting with Scrooge's rejection of the charity collectors, discuss how Dickens presents social responsibility across the novella.

Answer:

Dickens presents social responsibility as a duty to recognise and support other people, rather than treating wealth as a reason to withdraw from them. Scrooge's rejection of the collectors makes poverty seem an inconvenience that institutions should contain. His phrase “surplus population” uses dehumanising economic language, converting suffering people into an unwanted quantity. In the context of workhouse relief and population arguments, Dickens challenges the comfortable distance of those who can afford to ignore need.

Marley's visit exposes the long-term consequences of that distance. The chain he forged contains objects associated with business, so the burden visibly connects his punishment with his priorities. “Mankind was my business” then redefines responsibility through metaphor: human welfare should have mattered more than transactions. Marley warns Scrooge, but his inability to change his own past also makes Scrooge's remaining opportunity urgent.

The memory of Fezziwig offers a positive alternative. Scrooge recognises that an employer's kindness can make workers happy without great expense. The contrast with Bob's cold working conditions makes the lesson practical: authority should consider the people who depend on it.

The present spirit develops responsibility through Tiny Tim. The “crutch without an owner” represents possible loss through symbolic absence, encouraging grief for an individual instead of indifference to a population. When the spirit returns Scrooge's earlier words to him, the structural echo forces him to confront their cruelty. Ignorance and Want broaden this concern through allegory, giving deprivation and educational neglect the bodies of children.

The ending proves change through action. Sending food helps with a celebration, but raising Bob's salary addresses continuing conditions. Scrooge also gives to charity and becomes “a second father” to Tiny Tim, replacing isolation with active care. Dickens's argument therefore concerns a lasting use of wealth and power. Christmas concentrates these duties, but Scrooge learns to practise them beyond a single day.

31. Starting with Belle ending the engagement, discuss how Dickens presents Scrooge's transformation across the novella.

Answer:

Dickens presents Scrooge's transformation as the recovery of values that fear and greed have displaced. Belle's description of “Another idol” makes wealth a rival object of worship, suggesting that money has taken over his deepest loyalty. Her departure gives that priority a human cost: financial security cannot replace love.

The earlier childhood memory makes this choice more understandable without excusing it. A lonely child grew into an adult who protects himself through emotional withdrawal. The opening simile “solitary as an oyster” captures that enclosure, while allowing the possibility that something valuable remains inside. Remembering Fan and Fezziwig reveals kindness that Scrooge has experienced but failed to pass on. His defence of the generous employer also exposes standards he no longer meets in treating Bob.

The present spirit moves him beyond his own losses. The Cratchits' affection challenges the assumption that money determines worth, while Tiny Tim's uncertain survival makes need personal. The return of Scrooge's population argument forces him to recognise a contradiction between detached theory and care for a child. Dickens develops empathy before the future spirit creates terror.

The final spirit's silence gives Scrooge no comforting explanation. Scenes of indifference towards a dead man contrast with the Cratchits' grief, exposing a life without loving remembrance. At his grave, the distinction between what “Will” and “May” happen expresses a search for agency. The future is a conditional warning, so choosing differently still matters.

Scrooge's joyful awakening is a tonal reversal, but Dickens does not leave redemption as a feeling. The promises and practical help in the final stave turn it into sustained conduct. His familial role towards Tiny Tim also reverses the closed solitude of the opening. The transformation therefore combines regret, empathy and urgency, ending in responsibility. Fear starts a crisis, but kindness gives the changed life its lasting purpose.
