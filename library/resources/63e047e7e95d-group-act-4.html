Act 4: false confidence & cruelty

Act 4 — False confidence and cruelty

What happens

  • Macbeth demands answers from the witches. The apparitions warn him to beware Macduff, then appear to promise safety from anyone born of a woman and until Birnam Wood moves to Dunsinane.
  • A procession of kings connected with Banquo challenges Macbeth's hopes for his own dynasty.
  • Hearing that Macduff has fled, Macbeth orders an attack on his castle. Macduff's son is killed onstage; Lady Macduff flees pursued by the murderers. Their deaths are later reported to Macduff.
  • In England, Malcolm tests Macduff by pretending to be unfit to rule. Macduff's distress for Scotland convinces him of his loyalty. News of the family's murder turns their resistance into a deeply personal struggle.

Why this act matters

The promises use equivocation: wording that encourages a misleading interpretation. Macbeth accepts what sounds comforting while ignoring reasons for caution. Shakespeare gives the audience apparently impossible conditions whose meaning will change in the final act.

The attack on Macduff's family shows how far Macbeth has moved from the soldier who defended Scotland. Bringing vulnerable victims onto the stage makes the human cost of tyranny immediate. In contrast, Malcolm considers the qualities of a good ruler, and Macduff combines grief with action. Courage can include compassion rather than suppressing it.

Important quotations

QuotationWho and whereWhat to analyse
“Something wicked this way comes”Second Witch, 4.1Language / structure — moral label reversing Macbeth's opening reputation: Macbeth is now described as wicked even by a witch. This reverses the opening praise of “brave Macbeth”.
“none of woman born”Second Apparition, 4.1Language / structure — equivocation and delayed revelation: The phrase seems to include everyone. Macbeth mistakes its deceptive reassurance for complete protection.
“The very firstlings of my heart”Macbeth, 4.1Imagery / structure — birth metaphor marking the move to impulsive action: He decides to turn his first impulses directly into action. Compare this with his extended moral debate before Duncan's murder.
“All my pretty ones?”Macduff, 4.3Language — affectionate adjective and shocked question: The affectionate phrase and shocked question expose a father's loss, making political violence personal.
“I must also feel it as a man”Macduff, 4.3Language / structure — inclusive adverb and contrast with earlier ideas of manhood: “Also” joins feeling to action. His definition of masculinity challenges the idea that courage requires emotional hardness.
“Bleed, bleed, poor country!”Macduff, 4.3Imagery / language — personification, repetition and exclamation: Scotland is personified as a wounded body. His concern reaches beyond private revenge to national suffering.
“king-becoming graces”Malcolm, 4.3Language — ethical vocabulary within a list of virtues: While testing Macduff, he lists the virtues a ruler should possess. Kingship requires moral responsibility, not simply a crown.
“all my pretty chickens”Macduff, 4.3Imagery — vulnerable animal metaphor: The vulnerable family image exposes the predatory nature of Macbeth's violence and makes grief intensely personal.

Turning point: misleading reassurance makes Macbeth more reckless, while his cruelty strengthens the resistance against him.
