Space physics questions & answers

1 mark2 marks3 marks4 marks6 marks

1 mark

1. Which galaxy contains our solar system?

Answer:

The Milky Way.

2. Name the cloud of gas and dust from which a star forms.

Answer:

A nebula.

3. What is the stable, long-lasting stage of a star’s life called?

Answer:

Main sequence.

4. Which remnant follows the red-giant stage in the Sun-like route?

Answer:

A white dwarf.

5. Name the explosion that scatters a massive star’s material into space.

Answer:

A supernova.

6. What force maintains a planet’s orbit around the Sun?

Answer:

Gravity.

7. What happens to the observed wavelength of a red-shifted spectral line?

Answer:

It increases.

8. What is a natural satellite?

Answer:

A naturally occurring object that orbits a planet or another larger body.

2 marks

9. Explain why temperature rises as a protostar forms.

Answer:
  • Gravity pulls gas and dust together, contracting the cloud.
  • Contraction transfers energy so the protostar’s temperature rises.

10. State what happens to nuclei during fusion and name the main product of hydrogen fusion in a main-sequence star.

Answer:
  • Small nuclei join into a larger nucleus.
  • Hydrogen fusion produces helium.

11. Explain the balance that keeps a main-sequence star stable.

Answer:
  • Inward gravitational effects tend to collapse it.
  • Energy from fusion maintains an outward pressure effect that balances gravity.

12. Why is initial mass important when describing a star’s life cycle?

Answer:
  • It determines the broad later route: red giant and white dwarf for a Sun-like star.
  • Much more massive stars become red supergiants and supernovae, leaving a neutron star or black hole.

13. Explain how a supernova helps form later planets containing heavy elements.

Answer:
  • Its extreme conditions can produce elements heavier than iron.
  • The explosion distributes elements into space, providing material for later stars and planets.

14. Compare a moon and an artificial satellite around Earth.

Answer:
  • Both orbit Earth under gravitational force.
  • A moon formed naturally; an artificial satellite is constructed and launched by people.

15. Explain why a satellite can accelerate while moving at constant speed in a circular orbit.

Answer:
  • Its direction changes, so its velocity changes.
  • A change in velocity is acceleration, even at constant speed.

16. What is the relationship between stable circular orbital speed and radius around the same central body?

Answer:
  • A smaller radius has a higher orbital speed.
  • A larger radius has a lower orbital speed; a speed change requires a different stable circular orbit.

17. Why is simply seeing a red-looking galaxy not enough to establish red-shift?

Answer:
  • Scientists must compare matching spectral lines with their laboratory wavelengths.
  • Red-shift is a shift to longer wavelength, not just an object’s apparent colour.

18. A more distant galaxy has a larger red-shift than a nearer galaxy. What does this suggest about their recession speeds?

Answer:
  • Both are receding in the stated comparison.
  • The more distant, more red-shifted galaxy is receding faster.

19. What do observations of distant supernovae suggest about cosmic expansion?

Answer:
  • The expansion is accelerating.
  • This means its rate changes over time, not merely that further galaxies currently recede faster.

3 marks

20. Explain why gravity is not balanced by a forward force in a satellite’s circular orbit.

Answer:
  • Gravity provides an inward resultant force.
  • This changes the direction of motion rather than the speed in an ideal circular orbit.
  • The satellite continually accelerates towards the centre; no forward balancing force is needed to maintain motion.

21. Describe the route of a Sun-like star after its main-sequence stage.

Answer:
  • It expands into a red giant.
  • After losing outer material, it leaves a white dwarf.
  • The white dwarf cools over a very long time towards an expected black dwarf.

22. Describe the route of a much more massive star after its main-sequence stage.

Answer:
  • It becomes a red supergiant.
  • It undergoes a supernova.
  • Its remnant becomes a neutron star or black hole, depending on the remaining mass.

23. A circular orbit has radius 8.0 × 10⁶ m and period 8,000 s. Calculate average orbital speed using distance/time.

Answer:
  • One orbit is 2πr = 2π × 8.0 × 10⁶ m.
  • v = 2πr/T = (2π × 8.0 × 10⁶)/8,000.
  • v ≈ 6.3 × 10³ m/s.

24. A satellite is 600 km above a planet with radius 6,400 km. Its period is 6,000 s. Calculate its circular orbital speed.

Answer:
  • Orbital radius = 6,400 + 600 = 7,000 km = 7.0 × 10⁶ m.
  • v = 2πr/T = (2π × 7.0 × 10⁶)/6,000.
  • v ≈ 7.3 × 10³ m/s.

25. Explain why red-shift supports an expanding universe.

Answer:
  • Matching lines from most distant galaxies have longer wavelengths, showing recession.
  • More distant galaxies generally recede faster.
  • This distance–speed pattern supports expansion of space itself.

4 marks

26. Explain how a nebula develops into a main-sequence star.

Answer:
  • Gravity pulls together gas and dust.
  • Contraction raises temperature and pressure, forming a protostar.
  • When the core is hot enough, hydrogen fusion begins, producing helium and releasing energy.
  • Fusion maintains outward pressure effects that balance gravity, giving a stable main-sequence stage.

27. Explain why scientists may revise a cosmological model when new observations are made.

Answer:
  • A model should explain existing evidence and give testable predictions.
  • Scientists compare new observations with those predictions, considering measurement uncertainty.
  • Reliable evidence that does not fit may require the model to be modified or replaced.
  • For example, accelerating expansion led to further questions about dark energy rather than a claim that expansion was already completely explained.

6 marks

28. Compare the life cycles of a Sun-like star and a star much more massive than the Sun. Include shared early stages and different remnants.

Answer:
  • Both form from a nebula as gravity draws material together. Contraction produces a hot protostar.
  • Both begin hydrogen fusion and become main-sequence stars, with inward gravity balanced by outward effects maintained by fusion.
  • A Sun-like star expands into a red giant and loses outer material, leaving a white dwarf.
  • Its white dwarf cools over a very long time towards an expected black dwarf; this route does not include a supernova.
  • A much more massive star becomes a red supergiant, then undergoes a supernova that distributes elements.
  • Its remnant becomes a neutron star or black hole. Initial mass determines which broad route occurs.

29. Explain the evidence connecting galaxy observations with the Big Bang model, and distinguish accelerating expansion from the distance–speed trend.

Answer:
  • Compare known laboratory spectral lines with matching lines from distant galaxies. Their longer observed wavelengths show red-shift.
  • Most distant galaxies are receding; more distant galaxies generally show greater red-shift and faster recession.
  • This pattern supports the conclusion that space is expanding, rather than Earth being at a special centre.
  • Tracing expansion backwards supports an earlier hot, dense, compact state, as described by the Big Bang model.
  • Observations of distant supernovae also indicate that expansion is accelerating over time. This is a separate idea from comparing different galaxies at different distances.
  • Evidence supports and tests a model. Dark matter and dark energy remain incompletely understood, so the model does not mean every cause has been fully explained.
