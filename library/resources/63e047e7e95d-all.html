Macbeth

The story in five acts

Macbeth is a successful soldier who murders his king to gain the crown. Once he has power, he becomes afraid of losing it and commits more violence. His attempt to control the future destroys his security, relationships and life.

Imagine cheating to win a competition, then having to tell a new lie every time someone gets close to the truth. Macbeth's first crime starts a chain of concealment and fear. The consequences grow far beyond him, damaging Scotland itself.

Act 1 — Temptation and choice

What happens

  • The witches introduce a world of confusing appearances. Duncan hears that Macbeth has fought bravely for Scotland.
  • They greet Macbeth as Thane of Glamis, Thane of Cawdor and future king. They predict that Banquo's descendants will be kings.
  • Macbeth receives the title of Cawdor. One prediction coming true makes the promise of kingship seem believable. Duncan names Malcolm his heir, creating an obstacle to Macbeth's ambition.
  • Lady Macbeth reads her husband's letter and plans Duncan's murder during his visit. Macbeth objects, but she challenges his courage and persuades him to agree.

Why this act matters

Shakespeare puts temptation beside conscious choice. Macbeth knows that killing Duncan would betray his king, relative and guest. His soliloquy exposes these objections before he commits himself, so the audience sees his responsibility rather than a man simply obeying fate.

Duncan trusts Macbeth just after being betrayed by the previous Cawdor. This creates dramatic irony: the audience knows the danger that Duncan cannot see. Public praise and private plotting pull in opposite directions.

Turning point: Macbeth agrees to murder Duncan. A possible future becomes a deliberate plan.

Act 2 — Murder and disorder

What happens

  • Macbeth sees a dagger leading towards Duncan's room. A bell signals that the preparations are ready; he goes to kill Duncan offstage.
  • He returns carrying the bloody daggers. He is distressed by imagined voices and cannot return to the room. Lady Macbeth takes the daggers back and smears the guards with blood.
  • Knocking interrupts them. The Porter delays opening the gate; Macduff then discovers Duncan's body. Macbeth kills the guards, claiming that anger overwhelmed him.
  • Malcolm and Donalbain flee because they fear for their lives. Their flight makes them appear suspicious. Unnatural events mirror the disorder, and Macbeth goes to Scone to be crowned.

Why this act matters

The crime brings immediate psychological punishment. Macbeth gains a route to power but loses peace of mind. Blood is both visible evidence and the beginning of a lasting guilt motif. Lady Macbeth treats washing as a practical solution; he fears a stain that water cannot remove.

Keeping Duncan's murder offstage concentrates attention on anticipation and aftermath. Knocking makes discovery feel close. The Porter's imagined role as a gatekeeper of hell mixes comedy with a disturbing suggestion that the castle has become a place of evil. Darkness and unnatural behaviour in the animal world echo the broken political order.

Important quotations

QuotationWho and whereWhat to analyse
“Is this a dagger which I see before me”Macbeth, 2.1The question makes his senses uncertain. The vision can suggest a mind already consumed by the murder; it does not remove his choice.
“Macbeth does murder sleep”Macbeth reporting a voice, 2.2Sleep represents innocence and rest. The image suggests that killing Duncan also destroys Macbeth's own peace.

“Wake Duncan with thy knocking. I would thou couldst.”Macbeth, 2.2The impossible wish reveals regret. The knocking cannot reverse the irreversible act.

Turning point: Duncan's death makes Macbeth's ambition real, but kingship begins in fear and concealment.

Act 3 — Fear and isolation

What happens

  • Banquo suspects Macbeth. Macbeth fears the prediction about Banquo's descendants and hires murderers to kill Banquo and Fleance.
  • Macbeth and Lady Macbeth are unhappy despite gaining the crown. He conceals the details of his new murder plot from her.
  • Banquo is killed, but Fleance escapes. At the royal banquet Macbeth reacts to Banquo's ghost, which the guests cannot see. Lady Macbeth tries to cover for him, then dismisses the guests.
  • Macbeth decides to return to the witches. Hecate plans to exploit his confidence, while Scottish nobles increasingly oppose his rule and Macduff seeks help in England.

Why this act matters

Macbeth's motive shifts from gaining power to trying to secure it. Each attempt at safety produces another threat. Fleance's escape exposes the limits of his control, while the banquet turns private fear into a public failure of leadership.

The ghost can be read as supernatural punishment, a visible expression of guilt, or both. Whatever the production emphasises, the guests see a king who cannot command himself. The marriage also changes: Macbeth now organises violence independently, leaving Lady Macbeth to protect a public image she can no longer manage.

Important quotations

QuotationWho and whereWhat to analyse

“fruitless crown”Macbeth, 3.1The metaphor makes his kingship seem unable to produce a lasting dynasty. He fears that his crimes will benefit Banquo's family.
“Naught's had, all's spent”Lady Macbeth, 3.2The balanced contrast between nothing gained and everything spent exposes the emptiness of their achievement.
“full of scorpions is my mind”Macbeth, 3.2The animal imagery makes anxiety seem painful and poisonous. His thoughts torment him and encourage further violence.
“Thou canst not say I did it”Macbeth to the ghost, 3.4His denial points towards the guilt he tries to escape. Hiring killers does not free him from responsibility.
“I am in blood”Macbeth, 3.4The image of wading through blood presents violence as a condition he is trapped in. He uses this feeling to justify continuing rather than stopping.

Turning point: Macbeth becomes an isolated ruler whose attempts to protect his crown expose his instability.

Act 4 — False confidence and cruelty

What happens

  • Macbeth demands answers from the witches. The apparitions warn him to beware Macduff, then appear to promise safety from anyone born of a woman and until Birnam Wood moves to Dunsinane.
  • A procession of kings connected with Banquo challenges Macbeth's hopes for his own dynasty.
  • Hearing that Macduff has fled, Macbeth orders an attack on his castle. Macduff's son is killed onstage; Lady Macduff flees pursued by the murderers. Their deaths are later reported to Macduff.
  • In England, Malcolm tests Macduff by pretending to be unfit to rule. Macduff's distress for Scotland convinces him of his loyalty. News of the family's murder turns their resistance into a deeply personal struggle.

Why this act matters

The promises use equivocation: wording that encourages a misleading interpretation. Macbeth accepts what sounds comforting while ignoring reasons for caution. Shakespeare gives the audience apparently impossible conditions whose meaning will change in the final act.

The attack on Macduff's family shows how far Macbeth has moved from the soldier who defended Scotland. Bringing vulnerable victims onto the stage makes the human cost of tyranny immediate. In contrast, Malcolm considers the qualities of a good ruler, and Macduff combines grief with action. Courage can include compassion rather than suppressing it.

Important quotations

QuotationWho and whereWhat to analyse
“Something wicked this way comes”Second Witch, 4.1Macbeth is now described as wicked even by a witch. This reverses the opening praise of “brave Macbeth”.
“none of woman born”Second Apparition, 4.1The phrase seems to include everyone. Macbeth mistakes its deceptive reassurance for complete protection.
“The very firstlings of my heart”Macbeth, 4.1He decides to turn his first impulses directly into action. Compare this with his extended moral debate before Duncan's murder.
“All my pretty ones?”Macduff, 4.3The affectionate phrase and shocked question expose a father's loss, making political violence personal.
“I must also feel it as a man”Macduff, 4.3“Also” joins feeling to action. His definition of masculinity challenges the idea that courage requires emotional hardness.

Turning point: misleading reassurance makes Macbeth more reckless, while his cruelty strengthens the resistance against him.

Act 5 — Collapse and restoration

What happens

  • Lady Macbeth sleepwalks, rubbing her hands and revealing fragments of their crimes. A doctor and gentlewoman witness distress she can no longer conceal.
  • Malcolm's forces advance. Soldiers cut branches from Birnam Wood to hide their numbers, making the wood appear to move towards Dunsinane.
  • Macbeth hears that Lady Macbeth has died and speaks about life's emptiness. News of the moving wood begins to destroy his confidence.
  • He continues fighting, but Macduff reveals that he was cut from his mother's womb. Macbeth's interpretation of the birth prophecy fails. Macduff kills him and Malcolm is acclaimed king.

Why this act matters

Earlier images return with changed meanings. Lady Macbeth's imagined bloodstain contradicts her claim that a little water could clear the crime. Her fragmented prose and repeated hand-rubbing reveal a loss of control very different from her earlier commands.

The prophecies are fulfilled through meanings Macbeth did not expect. His final resistance recalls the courage praised at the start, but bravery cannot cancel his cruelty. Malcolm's closing speech promises repair. His description of the couple is condemnatory; the audience has also witnessed their private fears and wasted potential. Political order can be restored, but the dead cannot be brought back.

Turning point: Macbeth loses both the crown and the future he tried to force into existence. Lady Macbeth's death is reported rather than shown; Malcolm says she is thought to have taken her own life.

Macbeth: from defender to tyrant

Macbeth's central conflict is between ambition and conscience. He understands right and wrong but repeatedly puts his own security ahead of other people's lives.

  • Brave but morally vulnerable: “brave Macbeth” establishes real military courage. Courage on a battlefield does not guarantee good judgement as a ruler.
  • Ambitious and self-aware: “vaulting ambition” shows that he can identify his own dangerous motive. He is responsible for choices he knows are wrong.
  • Imaginative and fearful: “full of scorpions is my mind” turns anxiety into a painful physical image. His vivid imagination exposes guilt as well as the threats he invents or exaggerates.
  • Controlling and insecure: the “fruitless crown” expresses his fear of a future he cannot own. He treats other people as obstacles to control.
  • Capable of brutality and despair: Shakespeare allows us to hear suffering inside a man who causes terrible suffering. Understanding him does not require excusing him.

Relationships: Lady Macbeth challenges his resolve; Banquo offers a contrast in responding to temptation; Macduff confronts the harm caused by his rule. The witches exploit his wishes, but do not make his moral decisions for him.

A useful argument: Macbeth's desire for control exposes how little he can control himself. An audience may feel pity for his wasted potential while condemning his choices.

Lady Macbeth and the marriage

Lady Macbeth links power with the suppression of compassion. Her forceful language reveals determination, but also the effort needed to silence feelings she sees as weaknesses.

  • Ambitious: she imagines greatness for her husband and herself. Her interest in power makes the marriage a political partnership as well as a personal relationship.
  • Persuasive and manipulative: she attacks Macbeth's sense of manhood and treats murder as proof of love and courage. She knows which doubts will hurt him.
  • Apparently practical: “A little water clears us of this deed” reduces guilt to a problem of removing evidence. The word “little” reveals how much she underestimates its emotional cost.
  • Not without feeling: Duncan's resemblance to her father prevents her from killing him herself. This complicates the claim that she is naturally without compassion.
  • Unable to command her conscience: “Out, damned spot” uses the language of control against something she cannot dismiss. Her guilt makes her more than a simple symbol of evil.

The marriage: shared ambition binds the couple, but secrecy and guilt damage their trust. Lady Macbeth's influence is substantial, not unlimited: Macbeth remains responsible for his actions and becomes an independent organiser of violence.

A useful argument: her apparent strength depends on denying feelings that cannot be permanently silenced. Shakespeare makes the contrast between confident speech and emotional vulnerability central to her character.

Ambition, power and kingship

The play questions how power is gained and what it is used for.

  1. Macbeth begins by protecting a king and is rewarded for loyalty.
  2. His desire for the crown overcomes his knowledge of duty.
  3. Once crowned, he treats other people as threats to his survival.
  4. Scotland suffers under a ruler concerned with himself.
  5. Malcolm's restoration suggests that legitimate leadership should serve the country.

“Upon my head they placed a fruitless crown” (3.1) turns kingship into an image of infertility: Macbeth has power but fears he cannot pass it on. The crown produces anxiety instead of fulfilment.

Violence changes its purpose: defence of Scotland becomes murder of a guest, then political assassination, then slaughter of a family. Track that escalation instead of listing murders without explanation.

Guilt, blood and sleep

A motif is a repeated image or idea whose meaning develops.

MotifEarly meaningLater development
BloodEvidence of military braveryEvidence of murder, then an imagined permanent moral stain
SleepInnocence, rest and vulnerabilityMacbeth fears he has destroyed sleep; Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking exposes distress
WashingLady Macbeth's practical way to hide the crimeHer imagined stain cannot be washed away

Macbeth asks whether “all great Neptune's ocean” can clean his hand (2.2). The hyperbole makes guilt larger than the natural world. His next thought reverses the image: his blood could make the sea red. The stain seems able to contaminate everything around it.

The contrast with Lady Macbeth's “little water” is crucial. In Act 2 she minimises the problem; in Act 5 her body acts out the guilt she can no longer contain. The repeated action changes meaning across the play.

Appearance, deception and the supernatural

“Fair is foul, and foul is fair” (1.1) joins opposites, unsettling the audience's trust in appearances.

  • Lady Macbeth tells her husband to resemble an “innocent flower” while being a “serpent” underneath (1.5). The image separates a harmless surface from hidden danger.
  • Duncan feels safe at Macbeth's castle, while the audience knows the murder plan. This is dramatic irony: the audience knows what a character does not.
  • At the banquet, Macbeth tries to perform secure kingship but his reactions to the ghost reveal instability.
  • The apparitions' promises are equivocal: they can be technically fulfilled in a way Macbeth does not expect.

Birnam Wood appears to move because soldiers use branches as camouflage. Macduff was delivered by being cut from his mother's womb, defeating Macbeth's interpretation of the prophecy about birth. Macbeth mistakes partial information for complete protection.

Hallucination and the supernatural are not always identical. Macbeth's dagger vision can be read as an outward sign of his murderous intention and fear. Banquo's ghost may be staged to emphasise supernatural punishment, psychological guilt, or both. Explain the reading through what an audience sees and hears.

Gender, courage and vulnerability

Characters argue about what it means to be a man.

  • Lady Macbeth treats courage as willingness to carry out murder.
  • Macbeth first argues that there are moral limits to manhood: “I dare do all that may become a man” (1.7).
  • Later he manipulates the murderers through similar challenges to their masculinity.
  • Macduff insists that feeling grief is part of being a man (4.3), offering a different model of courage.

The play therefore gives competing definitions of masculinity, rather than one simple message that men are violent and women are weak. Lady Macbeth's early influence also complicates expectations of obedience within marriage.

The Scottish setting and the early seventeenth-century stage audience are different contexts. Beliefs about monarchy, witchcraft and gender can clarify a moment, but avoid claims that every person in Shakespeare's audience thought the same thing. Keep the argument grounded in the play.

Much of the play uses blank verse, usually unrhymed iambic pentameter. Do not claim every line is perfectly regular or that prose always means madness: explain the particular passage.

Duncan's murder happens offstage, focusing attention on anticipation and aftermath. The attack on Macduff's family is brought onto the stage, confronting the audience with innocent suffering. Those choices are part of dramatic structure, not merely plot information.

Writing about an extract

The extract question is worth 15 marks. Aim for about 20 minutes. Stay close to the printed passage and its changes in mood, language and behaviour.

  • Begin with a direct overview: what is happening emotionally or dramatically?
  • Follow the passage through its main shifts; use evidence from different parts.
  • Explain a specific word, image, action or speech pattern.
  • Connect the choice to character, meaning and a possible audience response.
  • Use enough context to locate the moment, without retelling the whole play.

Point → evidence → close analysis → effect → question. Treat this as a thinking route, not a compulsory sentence formula.

For example: Lady Macbeth's command “Out, damned spot” suggests that she tries to use the same forceful language that once directed her husband. Yet the stain is imagined, so the command achieves nothing. The word “damned” gives the mark a moral and religious weight. An audience may feel horror or pity at the gap between her earlier confidence and her present helplessness.

Building a whole-play argument

The essay question is worth 25 marks: 20 for the literary response and 5 for spelling, punctuation, vocabulary and sentence control. Aim for about 40 minutes, including a short plan and final check.

  1. Decide your answer to the exact question. For Lady Macbeth's change: she moves from directing others to being unable to control herself.
  2. Choose three or four moments that develop the argument across the play.
  3. Explain how Shakespeare presents the character or theme, not just what happens.
  4. Link the stages: initially, however, increasingly, by the end.
  5. Finish with the significance of the change, rather than a list of repeated points.

Write connected paragraphs in the exam. A useful paragraph does more than name a technique: it explains why that choice matters here. An interpretation, evidence and a developed explanation work together.

For Shakespeare, the assessed focus is your understanding, analysis and writing accuracy. Context can illuminate a reading, but a detached history paragraph takes space away from close analysis. Avoid diagnosing a character or stating Shakespeare's purpose as a proven fact when it is an interpretation.
