Macbeth

The story in five acts

Macbeth is a successful soldier who murders his king to gain the crown. Once he has power, he becomes afraid of losing it and commits more violence. His attempt to control the future destroys his security, relationships and life.

Imagine cheating to win a competition, then having to tell a new lie every time someone gets close to the truth. Macbeth's first crime starts a chain of concealment and fear. The consequences grow far beyond him, damaging Scotland itself.

Act 1 — Temptation and choice

What happens

  • The witches introduce a world of confusing appearances. Duncan hears that Macbeth has fought bravely for Scotland.
  • They greet Macbeth as Thane of Glamis, Thane of Cawdor and future king. They predict that Banquo's descendants will be kings.
  • Macbeth receives the title of Cawdor. One prediction coming true makes the promise of kingship seem believable. Duncan names Malcolm his heir, creating an obstacle to Macbeth's ambition.
  • Lady Macbeth reads her husband's letter and plans Duncan's murder during his visit. Macbeth objects, but she challenges his courage and persuades him to agree.

Why this act matters

Shakespeare puts temptation beside conscious choice. Macbeth knows that killing Duncan would betray his king, relative and guest. His soliloquy exposes these objections before he commits himself, so the audience sees his responsibility rather than a man simply obeying fate.

Duncan trusts Macbeth just after being betrayed by the previous Cawdor. This creates dramatic irony: the audience knows the danger that Duncan cannot see. Public praise and private plotting pull in opposite directions.

Act 2 — Murder and disorder

What happens

  • Macbeth sees a dagger leading towards Duncan's room. A bell signals that the preparations are ready; he goes to kill Duncan offstage.
  • He returns carrying the bloody daggers. He is distressed by imagined voices and cannot return to the room. Lady Macbeth takes the daggers back and smears the guards with blood.
  • Knocking interrupts them. The Porter delays opening the gate; Macduff then discovers Duncan's body. Macbeth kills the guards, claiming that anger overwhelmed him.
  • Malcolm and Donalbain flee because they fear for their lives. Their flight makes them appear suspicious. Unnatural events mirror the disorder, and Macbeth goes to Scone to be crowned.

Why this act matters

The crime brings immediate psychological punishment. Macbeth gains a route to power but loses peace of mind. Blood is both visible evidence and the beginning of a lasting guilt motif. Lady Macbeth treats washing as a practical solution; he fears a stain that water cannot remove.

Keeping Duncan's murder offstage concentrates attention on anticipation and aftermath. Knocking makes discovery feel close. The Porter's imagined role as a gatekeeper of hell mixes comedy with a disturbing suggestion that the castle has become a place of evil. Darkness and unnatural behaviour in the animal world echo the broken political order.

Act 3 — Fear and isolation

What happens

  • Banquo suspects Macbeth. Macbeth fears the prediction about Banquo's descendants and hires murderers to kill Banquo and Fleance.
  • Macbeth and Lady Macbeth are unhappy despite gaining the crown. He conceals the details of his new murder plot from her.
  • Banquo is killed, but Fleance escapes. At the royal banquet Macbeth reacts to Banquo's ghost, which the guests cannot see. Lady Macbeth tries to cover for him, then dismisses the guests.
  • Macbeth decides to return to the witches. Hecate plans to exploit his confidence, while Scottish nobles increasingly oppose his rule and Macduff seeks help in England.

Why this act matters

Macbeth's motive shifts from gaining power to trying to secure it. Each attempt at safety produces another threat. Fleance's escape exposes the limits of his control, while the banquet turns private fear into a public failure of leadership.

The ghost can be read as supernatural punishment, a visible expression of guilt, or both. Whatever the production emphasises, the guests see a king who cannot command himself. The marriage also changes: Macbeth now organises violence independently, leaving Lady Macbeth to protect a public image she can no longer manage.

Act 4 — False confidence and cruelty

What happens

  • Macbeth demands answers from the witches. The apparitions warn him to beware Macduff, then appear to promise safety from anyone born of a woman and until Birnam Wood moves to Dunsinane.
  • A procession of kings connected with Banquo challenges Macbeth's hopes for his own dynasty.
  • Hearing that Macduff has fled, Macbeth orders an attack on his castle. Macduff's son is killed onstage; Lady Macduff flees pursued by the murderers. Their deaths are later reported to Macduff.
  • In England, Malcolm tests Macduff by pretending to be unfit to rule. Macduff's distress for Scotland convinces him of his loyalty. News of the family's murder turns their resistance into a deeply personal struggle.

Why this act matters

The promises use equivocation: wording that encourages a misleading interpretation. Macbeth accepts what sounds comforting while ignoring reasons for caution. Shakespeare gives the audience apparently impossible conditions whose meaning will change in the final act.

The attack on Macduff's family shows how far Macbeth has moved from the soldier who defended Scotland. Bringing vulnerable victims onto the stage makes the human cost of tyranny immediate. In contrast, Malcolm considers the qualities of a good ruler, and Macduff combines grief with action. Courage can include compassion rather than suppressing it.

Act 5 — Collapse and restoration

What happens

  • Lady Macbeth sleepwalks, rubbing her hands and revealing fragments of their crimes. A doctor and gentlewoman witness distress she can no longer conceal.
  • Malcolm's forces advance. Soldiers cut branches from Birnam Wood to hide their numbers, making the wood appear to move towards Dunsinane.
  • Macbeth hears that Lady Macbeth has died and speaks about life's emptiness. News of the moving wood begins to destroy his confidence.
  • He continues fighting, but Macduff reveals that he was cut from his mother's womb. Macbeth's interpretation of the birth prophecy fails. Macduff kills him and Malcolm is acclaimed king.

Why this act matters

Earlier images return with changed meanings. Lady Macbeth's imagined bloodstain contradicts her claim that a little water could clear the crime. Her fragmented prose and repeated hand-rubbing reveal a loss of control very different from her earlier commands.

The prophecies are fulfilled through meanings Macbeth did not expect. His final resistance recalls the courage praised at the start, but bravery cannot cancel his cruelty. Malcolm's closing speech promises repair. His description of the couple is condemnatory; the audience has also witnessed their private fears and wasted potential. Political order can be restored, but the dead cannot be brought back.

What the doctor can and cannot establish

The doctor observes Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking and repeated actions. He recognises a disturbance that his ordinary medical skills cannot cure. His religious language connects conscience with suffering, but he does not establish that she is possessed. Her words return to the crimes the audience already knows, making the scene a revelation of concealed guilt.

Malcolm's final speech promises political repair. Because the play ends before his reign is shown, describe the prospect of restored order, rather than claiming that the audience witnesses a successful reign.

Macbeth: from defender to tyrant

Macbeth's central conflict is between ambition and conscience. He knows murder is wrong but increasingly puts his own power and security ahead of other people's lives.

  • Brave but morally vulnerable: his battlefield courage does not guarantee good judgement.
  • Ambitious and self-aware: he recognises his dangerous motive before choosing to kill Duncan.
  • Fearful and controlling: gaining the crown creates anxiety about losing it. Later, hubris — overconfidence in the prophecies — makes him mistake reassurance for safety.
  • Isolated and despairing: power damages his marriage and peace of mind. His suffering does not excuse the suffering he causes.

Key quotations

  • “brave Macbeth” — Captain, 1.2: the praise establishes the honour he later loses. His violence initially protects Scotland.
  • “vaulting ambition” — Macbeth, 1.7: the metaphor suggests leaping too far and falling. He identifies the motive that will destroy him.
  • “fruitless crown” — Macbeth, 3.1: the image of infertility reveals his fear of having no lasting dynasty. Power brings insecurity rather than fulfilment.
  • “full of scorpions is my mind” — Macbeth, 3.2: poisonous animal imagery makes his anxiety seem painful and dangerous.
  • “I am in blood” — Macbeth, 3.4: he imagines himself wading through violence and uses this to justify continuing. He still has responsibility for his choices.
  • “Tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow” — Macbeth, 5.5: repetition makes the future seem monotonous and empty, reversing his earlier excitement about greatness.

Relationships: Lady Macbeth challenges his resolve; Banquo offers a different response to temptation; Macduff confronts his tyranny. The witches exploit his wishes without making his decisions for him.

Central argument: Macbeth's desire to control the future exposes his failure to control himself.

Lady Macbeth and the marriage

Lady Macbeth links power with suppressing compassion. Her forceful language initially hides vulnerability; guilt later destroys the control she tries to maintain.

  • Ambitious and manipulative: she wants greatness for the couple and uses expectations of manhood to shame Macbeth into murder. Her influence is strongest early on; he later acts independently.
  • Apparently practical: she treats guilt as a problem of removing evidence, underestimating its emotional cost.
  • Not without feeling: Duncan's resemblance to her father prevents her from killing him herself.
  • Overwhelmed by guilt: sleepwalking and imagined bloodstains expose feelings she cannot permanently silence.

Key quotations

  • “unsex me here” — Lady Macbeth, 1.5: the imperative asks spirits to remove the tenderness she associates with femininity. Cruelty is something she must seek.
  • “innocent flower” / “serpent” — Lady Macbeth, 1.5: contrasting images teach Macbeth to hide danger behind a harmless appearance.
  • “When you durst do it, then you were a man” — Lady Macbeth, 1.7: she makes manhood depend on murder, turning a moral objection into a supposed failure of courage.
  • “A little water clears us of this deed” — Lady Macbeth, 2.2: “little” minimises the crime. Her confidence contrasts with Macbeth's immediate horror.
  • “Naught's had, all's spent” — Lady Macbeth, 3.2: the balanced contrast exposes the emptiness of their achievement: they have sacrificed everything without gaining happiness.
  • “Out, damned spot” — Lady Macbeth, 5.1: the command fails against an imagined stain. “Damned” gives her guilt a moral and religious force.

The marriage: shared ambition initially binds them. Macbeth later plans violence independently, while she loses influence and emotional control. Her persuasion is powerful, but his choices remain his responsibility.

Central argument: her apparent strength depends on denying feelings that cannot be permanently silenced.

The witches: temptation and deceptive promises

The witches make dangerous possibilities attractive. They tempt Macbeth and encourage false confidence, but do not remove his responsibility.

  • Deceptive: a true prediction makes later promises seem trustworthy. Their language uses equivocation: more than one possible meaning.
  • Unsettling: paradoxes, thunder, rhyme and chant-like rhythms introduce disorder and uncertainty.
  • Catalysts, not decision-makers: the prophecies accelerate his desire for power. Macbeth first encounters them unexpectedly, then actively seeks their reassurance. They never command him to murder Duncan.

Key quotations

  • “Fair is foul, and foul is fair” — witches, 1.1: the paradox mixes good and evil, preparing the audience to distrust appearances.
  • “All hail, Macbeth, that shalt be king hereafter!” — Third Witch, 1.3: the ceremonial greeting flatters him. It promises kingship without supplying a method or date.
  • “Double, double toil and trouble” — witches, 4.1: repetition, rhyme and strong beats sound ritual-like. “Double” can suggest multiplying trouble.
  • “Something wicked this way comes” — Second Witch, 4.1: describing Macbeth as “wicked” reverses his opening reputation and shows how far he has changed.
  • “none of woman born” — Second Apparition, 4.1: the apparently universal protection hides an unexpected meaning. Attribute this to the apparition summoned in their scene.

Relationships: Banquo questions their motives; Macbeth increasingly accepts the interpretation he wants. Their influence can suggest supernatural evil or expose his desires, while the extent of their power remains uncertain.

Central argument: the witches make destructive choices seem promising and vulnerability feel like safety.

Banquo: temptation, integrity and a threatening legacy

Banquo is Macbeth's fellow soldier and Fleance's father. He is a foil: his different response to temptation helps reveal Macbeth's moral failure.

  • Cautious and principled: he doubts the witches' motives and wants advancement without sacrificing loyalty.
  • Tempted, not perfectly innocent: he hopes their prediction for his descendants will come true, but does not murder to achieve it.
  • A threat to Macbeth: his courage, judgement, suspicions and promised descendants deepen Macbeth's insecurity.

Key quotations

  • “The instruments of darkness tell us truths” — Banquo, 1.3: “darkness” suggests evil. He recognises that a true statement can still be part of a trap.
  • “My bosom franchised and allegiance clear” — Banquo, 2.1: “franchised” means free. He insists on keeping his conscience and loyalty untainted.
  • “Thou played'st most foully for 't” — Banquo, 3.1: his private speech reveals suspicion of Macbeth's dishonest rise. Suspicion is not proof.
  • “May they not be my oracles as well” — Banquo, 3.1: the question reveals hope in the prophecies, complicating the idea that he is untouched by ambition.
  • “royalty of nature” — Macbeth about Banquo, 3.1: Macbeth recognises natural nobility in a man without a crown. His admiration becomes fear.

Relationships: Fleance's escape leaves the future beyond Macbeth's control; the play does not show him becoming king. Banquo's ghost can suggest guilt, supernatural punishment or both. The guests cannot see it, but see Macbeth lose authority.

Central argument: Banquo exposes Macbeth's poor choices and shows that killing a perceived rival cannot remove fear or guilt.

Macduff: loyalty, grief and resistance

Macduff resists Macbeth because he cares about Scotland and its people. His courage includes compassion and grief, offering an alternative to Macbeth's cruelty.

  • Loyal and independent: his horror at Duncan's death is genuine. He seeks support for Scotland rather than a crown for himself.
  • Emotionally open: his grief makes the human cost of tyranny visible and challenges the idea that manhood excludes feeling.
  • Morally conflicted: leaving his family creates tension between public duty and responsibility at home. Macbeth is responsible for ordering their murders.

Key quotations

  • “O horror, horror, horror!” — Macduff, 2.3: repetition and exclamation convey shock at Duncan's murder, contrasting with Macbeth's concealment.
  • “Bleed, bleed, poor country!” — Macduff, 4.3: personification makes Scotland a wounded body. His concern extends beyond personal gain.
  • “All my pretty ones?” — Macduff, 4.3: the affectionate phrase and shocked question reveal a father's devastating loss.
  • “But I must also feel it as a man” — Macduff, 4.3: “also” joins emotion with action. Compassion is compatible with courage.
  • “Turn, hellhound, turn!” — Macduff, 5.8: repeated imperatives demand confrontation. Animal and religious imagery condemn Macbeth as a savage force of evil.
  • “Untimely ripped” — Macduff, 5.8: his delivery by being cut from his mother's womb defeats Macbeth's interpretation of the prophecy. Macduff remains human.

Relationships: Malcolm tests his loyalty before trusting him. Lady Macduff questions his departure, and he later blames himself for the family's deaths. His final resistance serves Scotland as well as personal revenge.

Central argument: Macduff connects national suffering with personal grief. As an avenging hero, he seeks justice for his family while helping free Scotland; his violence serves a different purpose from Macbeth's.

Duncan: generous kingship and misplaced trust

Duncan is Scotland's king and Malcolm's father. He rewards loyalty and values service, but cannot reliably recognise betrayal. His goodness makes Macbeth's murder more disturbing; his mistakes prevent him from being a flawless ruler.

  • Generous and appreciative: he rewards Macbeth and Banquo rather than treating their success as a threat.
  • Trusting, with vulnerable judgement: the old Thane of Cawdor betrays him; he then places confidence in Macbeth, repeating the danger.
  • A contrast to tyranny: his nurturing language presents leadership as helping others flourish. Macbeth increasingly rules through fear.

Key quotations

  • “noble Macbeth” — Duncan, 1.2: language — approving adjective: he rewards the soldier he believes is loyal. Transferring Cawdor's title to another future traitor creates a structural irony.
  • “There's no art” — Duncan, 1.4: structure — dramatic irony: he admits that faces cannot reveal intentions. Macbeth enters immediately afterwards, exposing the gap between Duncan's trust and the danger the audience knows.
  • “I have begun to plant thee” — Duncan, 1.4: imagery — gardening metaphor: he treats Macbeth's advancement as growth he should nurture. Good kingship supports others, rather than destroying rivals.
  • “pleasant seat” — Duncan, 1.6: language / structure — reassuring adjective and dramatic irony: Macbeth's castle seems welcoming, but the audience knows a murder is being planned. Apparent comfort conceals danger.
  • “plead like angels” — Macbeth about Duncan, 1.7: imagery / form — religious simile in Macbeth's soliloquy: Duncan's virtues become a powerful argument against murder. Macbeth recognises his goodness before choosing to betray him.

Relationships and role: Duncan is Macbeth's king, relative and guest, so the murder breaks several duties at once. Naming Malcolm as his heir also creates an obstacle to Macbeth's ambition. His death is a structural turning point, replacing temptation with guilt and political disorder.

Central argument: Duncan represents benevolent authority whose vulnerability exposes both the danger of trusting appearances and the cruelty of Macbeth's choices.

Malcolm: legitimate succession and cautious leadership

Malcolm is Duncan's eldest son and chosen heir. He offers an alternative to Macbeth's tyranny, combining a claim to the crown with concern for Scotland. The ending promises restoration, rather than showing that every problem has already been solved.

  • Cautious: after his father's murder he flees danger, then tests Macduff before trusting him. His caution contrasts with Duncan's misplaced confidence.
  • Concerned with moral fitness: his test asks whether Macduff puts Scotland's welfare above loyalty to a particular ruler.
  • Not a flawless ideal: his challenge to Macduff's manhood allows Macduff to offer a more compassionate definition of courage.

Key quotations

  • “The Prince of Cumberland” — Duncan naming Malcolm, 1.4: language / structure — title and obstacle: the public appointment establishes succession; Macbeth's private reaction treats Malcolm as something to overcome.
  • “king-becoming graces” — Malcolm, 4.3: language — a list of virtues: justice, mercy and courage define good kingship. He pretends to lack these qualities to test Macduff's loyalty to Scotland.
  • “Dispute it like a man” — Malcolm, 4.3: language — imperative: he urges Macduff to confront his grief through action. Macduff's response insists that feeling also belongs to manhood.
  • “planted newly” — Malcolm, final scene: imagery / structure — gardening metaphor: the rebuilding language recalls Duncan's nurturing speech and contrasts with the destruction of Macbeth's reign.
  • “by the grace of grace” — Malcolm, final scene: language — religious phrasing: his closing promise places restoration within a language of divine favour. This expresses a claim to rightful rule, rather than proving that the audience has witnessed God's choice.

Relationships and role: Duncan's appointment gives him a recognised claim. Macduff's integrity earns his trust, and their alliance turns resistance into a practical challenge to Macbeth. The invitation to the coronation completes a movement from stolen power towards restored authority.

Central argument: Malcolm links legitimate succession with responsible leadership; a title alone is not enough to make a good king.

Ambition / Power

Ambition is wanting to achieve something. Power is the ability to influence or control others. Macbeth's tragedy begins when he values gaining power more than the moral limits on how he should gain it.

Imagine winning a competition by cheating, then becoming more worried about being found out than enjoying the prize. Macbeth gains the crown but spends his reign trying to protect it. Success brings fear instead of satisfaction.

How the theme develops

  • Desire: the witches make kingship seem possible. Macbeth soon sees Malcolm as an obstacle and wants to hide his wishes.
  • Choice: Lady Macbeth pressures him, but his own soliloquy shows that he knows murder is wrong. Ambition overcomes his conscience.
  • Insecurity: after becoming king, he fears Banquo's descendants and acts independently to destroy perceived threats.
  • Emptiness: gaining power damages his marriage, peace of mind and sense of purpose. His final despair exposes the cost of the future he pursued.

Quotations to analyse

“vaulting ambition” — Macbeth, 1.7

Imagery / form — riding metaphor within a soliloquy: The metaphor suggests a rider leaping too far and falling. His ambition reaches beyond a safe or proper limit. Macbeth identifies the danger himself, making his later choice more blameworthy.

“To be thus is nothing” — Macbeth, 3.1

Language — absolute negative judgement: “Thus” means being king. The absolute negative “nothing” makes his achievement seem worthless unless he can feel secure. Wanting power turns into wanting impossible certainty.

“fruitless crown” — Macbeth, 3.1

Imagery — metaphor of infertility: The image of infertility suggests a crown that cannot produce a lasting dynasty. Macbeth fears that his crimes will benefit Banquo's descendants, rather than his own. Power does not give him ownership of the future.

“Tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow” — Macbeth, 5.5

Language / structure — repetition in the late speech of despair: Repetition makes time seem monotonous and empty. The future that once excited him now feels pointless. Shakespeare links the pursuit of greatness with the loss of meaning.

Essay argument: ambition becomes destructive when achievement matters more than conscience; power then deepens Macbeth's insecurity instead of solving it.

Kingship

Kingship asks a different question from ambition: what makes someone worthy to rule? Shakespeare contrasts a ruler who serves the country with a tyrant who treats it as a possession.

Think of a team captain. Wearing the badge gives them authority, but good leadership depends on protecting the team, rewarding effort and earning trust. Macbeth holds the title while losing the qualities that justify it.

Regicide, usurpation and succession

Taking a leader's badge by harming its owner is different from earning the right to lead. Macbeth obtains the crown, but his methods destroy the trust that should support his rule.

  • Regicide: murdering a king. Macbeth kills Duncan in Act 2.
  • Usurpation: taking power without a legitimate right. Macbeth's regicide clears the way for his seizure of the throne.
  • Legitimate succession: the recognised transfer of authority. Duncan publicly names Malcolm Prince of Cumberland in 1.4; Macbeth treats that appointment as an obstacle.
  • Primogeniture: inheritance through the firstborn, traditionally the eldest son. Malcolm is Duncan's eldest son, but base an argument on the appointment shown in the play rather than assuming all Scottish succession followed one simple rule.

Religious and political meaning

The Divine Right of Kings was the belief that royal authority came from God. Within that belief, killing a king attacked a sacred as well as a political order. Macduff's description of Duncan's body as “The Lord's anointed temple” (2.3) uses a religious metaphor: Duncan becomes a sacred building violated by murder. His description of the act as “sacrilegious” gives that judgement explicit force.

Shakespeare wrote during James I's reign, when his company had royal patronage. This helps explain the play's interest in treason and kingship. Its condemnation of regicide could appeal to royal concerns, but that is an interpretation of the play's effect, rather than proof of a single purpose. The play's Scotland and Shakespeare's stage audience are different historical settings.

The cost of taking power

  • Before murder: Macbeth's soliloquies expose conscience and anticipated consequences. The dagger vision occurs before Duncan is killed.
  • After gaining the crown: the “fruitless crown” metaphor shows that possession does not give Macbeth the dynasty or security he wants.
  • During his rule: the “full of scorpions” metaphor makes anxiety painful and poisonous. More violence attempts to protect his first crime, while Scotland suffers.

Three rulers to compare

RulerWhat his leadership reveals
DuncanRewards loyal service and inspires affection. His trusting nature also leaves him vulnerable: he misjudges the old Cawdor and then Macbeth.
MacbethGains the crown through betrayal. He relies increasingly on fear, murder and surveillance, putting his own security ahead of Scotland.

Quotations to analyse

Macbeth about Duncan, 1.7:

his virtues
Will plead like angels

Imagery — religious simile: Duncan's goodness is presented as a powerful argument against murder. Macbeth recognises the king's virtues before betraying him. His awareness makes the betrayal deliberate.

“royalty of nature” — Macbeth about Banquo, 3.1

Language / imagery — metaphor distinguishing inner nobility from rank: Banquo has natural nobility despite not wearing a crown. This separates moral authority from a royal title and exposes Macbeth's fear of someone whose qualities he respects.

“Bleed, bleed, poor country!” — Macduff, 4.3

Imagery / language — personification, repetition and exclamation: Scotland is personified as a wounded body. A ruler's private wrongdoing becomes a national injury. Good leadership should heal this damage rather than create it.

“mercy” and “courage” — Malcolm, 4.3

Language — paired virtues within a longer list: These are among the qualities Malcolm lists while pretending to lack the virtues of a king. Their combination matters: leadership needs compassion as well as strength. Macduff's distress at Malcolm's supposed corruption helps Malcolm judge his loyalty.

Essay argument: Shakespeare distinguishes possessing power from deserving it. Malcolm's restoration promises repair, but it cannot undo the lives lost under Macbeth.

Violence

Violence is not presented in the same way throughout the play. Its purpose, victims and consequences change. Macbeth's violence begins in defence of Scotland and ends in attacks on innocent people.

Like a tool that can protect someone or injure them, strength alone is not evidence of goodness. Shakespeare asks who uses force, whom it harms and what it is meant to achieve.

Track the changing purpose

  1. Battlefield defence: Macbeth's violence earns praise because he fights against Scotland's enemies.
  2. Betrayal: murdering Duncan attacks a king, relative and guest whom Macbeth should protect.
  3. Political assassination: he hires killers to attack Banquo and Fleance because he fears a rival dynasty.
  4. Terror: the attack on Macduff's household targets vulnerable people who cannot threaten him in battle.
  5. Resistance: Macduff's confrontation with Macbeth uses force to end tyranny and avenge its victims.

Quotations and dramatic choices

“brave Macbeth” — Captain, 1.2

Language / structure — approving adjective establishing the tragic reversal: The approving adjective establishes a violent soldier as a national defender. This starting point makes his later brutality a reversal, rather than an unchanged trait.

“blood will have blood” — Macbeth, 3.4

Language / imagery — repetition within the blood motif: Repetition presents bloodshed as something that calls forth further bloodshed. Macbeth fears consequences, revenge and exposure, yet continues the actions that create them.

“I am in blood” — Macbeth, 3.4

Imagery — extended metaphor of wading through blood: The image of wading through blood makes violence seem to surround him. He argues that returning would be as difficult as going on. This is his justification for continuing, not proof that he has no choice.

“All my pretty ones?” — Macduff, 4.3

Language — affectionate adjective and shocked question: The affectionate phrase shifts attention from the murderer to the bereaved family. Shakespeare makes the cost of political violence personal and emotionally immediate.

Stagecraft: Duncan's murder occurs offstage, directing attention towards anticipation and guilt. Macduff's son is killed onstage, confronting the audience with a helpless victim. The final combat makes resistance visible. These choices shape how the audience judges different acts of violence.

Essay argument: Macbeth's increasing readiness to harm innocent people marks his decline from defender to tyrant; the play judges violence through its motives and effects.

Guilt

Guilt is the distress caused by recognising wrongdoing. Macbeth and Lady Macbeth try to hide the evidence of murder, but removing physical evidence cannot remove their knowledge of what they have done.

Think of deleting a dishonest message: the screen may look clean, but the person who sent it still remembers it. Washing blood from their hands cannot simply restore the couple's peace of mind.

Quotations to analyse

“Macbeth does murder sleep” — Macbeth reporting a voice, 2.2

Imagery — personification of sleep: Personification turns sleep into another victim of the crime. Killing Duncan seems to destroy Macbeth's own ability to rest. The voice reveals immediate mental disturbance, not a separate physical murder.

“all great Neptune's ocean” — Macbeth, 2.2

Imagery / language — classical allusion and hyperbole: The hyperbole makes guilt too large for an entire ocean to wash away. His next thought reverses the image: his blood could turn the sea red. The stain seems able to contaminate the world around him.

“A little water clears us of this deed” — Lady Macbeth, 2.2

Language / structure — minimising adjective and later reversal: The minimising adjective “Little” downplays the crime and treats guilt as a practical cleaning problem. Her confidence contrasts with Macbeth's horror and prepares the audience for her later collapse.

“Out, damned spot” — Lady Macbeth, 5.1

Language / dramatic form — failed imperative within fragmented sleepwalking prose: Her imperative cannot control an imagined stain. “Damned” gives guilt a moral and religious force. Hand-rubbing and fragmented prose turn private distress into visible stage action.

Compare the couple: Macbeth initially voices guilt while Lady Macbeth dismisses it. Later, she compulsively relives the crime while he increasingly responds to fear with further violence. They both suffer, but they do not react identically.

Essay argument: Shakespeare makes guilt a lasting consequence of deliberate wrongdoing. The repeated washing image changes from confidence to helplessness.

Gender

Characters disagree about what makes someone a man or a woman. The play offers competing definitions of courage, rather than a simple rule that men are violent and women are weak.

Imagine someone being pressured with “prove you're brave” before doing something dangerous. Lady Macbeth uses this kind of challenge to make Macbeth's refusal seem shameful. Macduff later offers a different view: courage can include grief.

Quotations and competing ideas

“unsex me here” — Lady Macbeth, 1.5

Language / form — imperative in an appeal to spirits: The imperative asks supernatural forces to remove qualities she associates with femininity, especially tenderness. She links cruelty with the power to act. Needing to request this change also suggests that ruthlessness is something she must work to achieve.

“I dare do all that may become a man” — Macbeth, 1.7

Language — moral qualification of courage: “Become” means be fitting or appropriate here. Macbeth initially gives manhood a moral limit: being brave does not mean committing any act. His objection shows an alternative to Lady Macbeth's definition.

“When you durst do it, then you were a man” — Lady Macbeth, 1.7

Language — conditional claim and manipulation of identity: She makes masculinity conditional on committing murder. This is emotional manipulation, turning a moral objection into a supposed failure of courage and identity. Macbeth later uses comparable challenges against the hired murderers.

“But I must also feel it as a man” — Macduff, 4.3

Language / structure — inclusive adverb and contrast with earlier ideas of manhood: The adverb “Also” joins emotion with action. Macduff refuses to treat grief for his family as incompatible with manhood. His response challenges the idea that compassion must be suppressed before someone can act bravely.

What to compare

  • Lady Macbeth has considerable influence early in the marriage, complicating an expectation of female obedience. Her influence later declines as Macbeth plans independently.
  • Her own attachment to her father and later guilt complicate the hardness she tries to perform.
  • Macduff combines feeling and resistance, although some of his other remarks still reflect conventional gender assumptions.

The Scottish setting and Shakespeare's early seventeenth-century stage audience are different contexts. Gender expectations can clarify the pressure characters use, but do not assume every member of the audience held identical beliefs.

Essay argument: Shakespeare exposes the danger of equating masculinity with cruelty and presents compassion as compatible with courage.

The supernatural

The supernatural involves forces beyond ordinary nature: witches, apparitions and ghosts. Shakespeare uses them to raise questions about temptation, fate and human choice.

Think of someone relying on a prediction because it promises the result they want. The prediction may influence their decisions, but it does not explain away the decisions themselves. Macbeth increasingly lets supernatural reassurance replace careful judgement.

Different supernatural experiences

  • The witches: make predictions and create an atmosphere of disorder through thunder, paradoxes and ritual-like speech.
  • The apparitions: supply warnings and deceptive reassurance, encouraging Macbeth to feel secure.
  • Banquo's ghost: disrupts the banquet and makes Macbeth's hidden guilt or supernatural punishment visible onstage. Other guests cannot see it.
  • The dagger vision: may be a hallucination expressing Macbeth's intention and fear. Do not automatically treat every disturbing vision as an independent supernatural being.

Quotations to analyse

“The instruments of darkness tell us truths” — Banquo, 1.3

Imagery / structure — metaphor of evil agents and a warning before the crime: The warning shows that a correct prediction can still lead someone towards harm. Banquo's caution contrasts with Macbeth's growing dependence.

“Is this a dagger which I see before me” — Macbeth, 2.1

Language / form — question within a soliloquy: The question makes his perception uncertain. The apparent weapon gives visible form to the murder already occupying his mind. It does not remove his ability to choose.

“Something wicked this way comes” — Second Witch, 4.1

Language / structure — moral label reversing Macbeth's opening reputation: Macbeth has become the wicked presence approaching the witches. The description reverses his opening reputation and suggests increasing association with evil.

“none of woman born” — Second Apparition, 4.1

Language / structure — equivocation and delayed revelation: The apparently universal protection depends on an unexpected meaning. Macbeth trusts the comforting interpretation and becomes more reckless. Macduff's unusual birth later exposes his error.

Dramatic effect: sound, entrances, cauldron action and the ghost's appearance make uncertainty something the audience experiences. A production may stress supernatural power, psychological disturbance, or a combination; support the interpretation with what is said and staged.

Essay argument: supernatural promises tempt Macbeth and intensify his fear, while his repeated choices preserve his responsibility for the harm he causes.

Appearance and reality

This theme asks whether the surface can be trusted. Characters hide their intentions, and reassuring words conceal danger. The gap between what others see and what the audience knows creates tension.

Think of a friendly-looking message that hides a scam. Its harmless appearance encourages trust. Duncan's welcoming hosts similarly hide a deadly intention, while Macbeth accepts comforting promises without examining their limits.

Quotations to analyse

“Fair is foul, and foul is fair” — witches, 1.1

Language — paradox and reversed word order: The paradox introduces a world in which good and evil appearances become confused. It prepares the audience to look beneath attractive surfaces.

“innocent flower” and “serpent” — Lady Macbeth, 1.5

Imagery — contrasting flower and serpent images: The contrasting images separate harmless appearance from hidden threat. She teaches Macbeth to make hospitality a disguise. The serpent can also suggest temptation and betrayal.

“There's no art” — Duncan, 1.4

Structure — dramatic irony created by the next entrance: He says it is impossible to read a person's mind from their face. Macbeth enters just after Duncan admits that his trust in the previous Cawdor was misplaced. The placement creates dramatic irony: Duncan is about to repeat the mistake with another Cawdor.

Three important gaps

AppearanceReality and dramatic effect
Welcoming hostsMacbeth and Lady Macbeth plan Duncan's murder. The audience's knowledge makes Duncan's trust painful to watch.
Secure, commanding kingMacbeth's reactions to Banquo's ghost expose fear and disorder during a public banquet.
Promises of safetyBirnam Wood appears to move when soldiers carry branches. Macduff's unusual delivery defeats Macbeth's interpretation of the birth prophecy.

The apparitions use equivocation: words that can be fulfilled in a way Macbeth does not expect. They do not need to make an openly false promise to mislead him. He mistakes partial information for complete protection.

Connect the themes: appearance and reality concerns the gap between surface and truth. The supernatural is one source of that gap; the couple's deliberate deception is another.

Essay argument: Shakespeare shows that trusting appearances without examining motives or meanings leaves people vulnerable to betrayal, including Macbeth himself.

Much of the play uses blank verse, usually unrhymed iambic pentameter. Do not claim every line is perfectly regular or that prose always means madness: explain the particular passage.

Duncan's murder happens offstage, focusing attention on anticipation and aftermath. The attack on Macduff's family is brought onto the stage, confronting the audience with innocent suffering. Those choices are part of dramatic structure, not merely plot information.

Hearing a change in control

Blank verse is unrhymed verse usually organised around five iambic beats. A beat is a pattern of stress, not simply a count of written words. Listen for how a sentence fits or interrupts its line, then explain the particular effect. An irregular line alone does not prove that a character has lost control.

The witches' rhyming couplets can give a speech an enclosed, chant-like ending. In contrast, a soliloquy lets an audience follow an argument that is still unfolding. Macbeth's questions and competing reasons in Act 1 make his decision feel like a moral struggle, rather than an instruction he cannot resist.

The Porter scene briefly changes the tone after Duncan's murder. Its jokes about admitting sinners to hell make Macbeth's castle seem morally corrupted. The knocking also brings the outside world closer to discovering the crime. Comic relief can therefore increase the significance of the surrounding horror.

Writing about an extract

The extract question is worth 15 marks. Aim for about 20 minutes. Stay close to the printed passage and its changes in mood, language and behaviour.

  • Begin with a direct overview: what is happening emotionally or dramatically?
  • Follow the passage through its main shifts; use evidence from different parts.
  • Explain a specific word, image, action or speech pattern.
  • Connect the choice to character, meaning and a possible audience response.
  • Use enough context to locate the moment, without retelling the whole play.

Point → evidence → close analysis → effect → question. Treat this as a thinking route, not a compulsory sentence formula.

For example: Lady Macbeth's command “Out, damned spot” suggests that she tries to use the same forceful language that once directed her husband. Yet the stain is imagined, so the command achieves nothing. The word “damned” gives the mark a moral and religious weight. An audience may feel horror or pity at the gap between her earlier confidence and her present helplessness.

Connecting themes to the question

The themes are like different lenses on the same event. Choose the lenses that sharpen your answer, rather than describing every lens you own.

Three linked ideas: Macbeth's inner conflict

Argument: Macbeth understands the moral cost of murder, but repeatedly allows desire and then fear to override that understanding.

  1. Ambition against conscience: in 1.7 he identifies his duties and then “vaulting ambition”. The soliloquy lets the audience hear the choice he is hiding from Duncan.
  2. Gender and concealed intention: Lady Macbeth's manhood challenge pressures him to prove himself. Her “innocent flower” / “serpent” comparison uses contrasting images to turn outward loyalty into a disguise.
  3. Guilt becoming renewed violence: the “full of scorpions” metaphor (3.2) shows that gaining power has not brought peace. He responds to insecurity by organising further murders, so internal suffering creates danger for others.

These are possible routes through a whole-play question, not required paragraph labels. Three well-developed ideas can work; use more or fewer when the question and evidence need them. A single paragraph may connect more than one theme.

Keep each paragraph focused

  • Answer the question: make a claim about its exact focus, such as inner conflict or leadership.
  • Use evidence: choose a short, accurate quotation or a precise event reference.
  • Analyse a choice: explain a word, image, speech form, contrast or dramatic placement.
  • Develop the meaning: show what the choice reveals and how it shapes an audience's understanding.
  • Link the stages: explain what changes or remains unresolved across the play.

For a 15-mark extract, use the printed passage throughout. For a 25-mark essay, choose relevant moments from across the play. Context can deepen a particular reading; it should not replace close analysis.

Qualify a claim when the evidence needs it: the witches influence Macbeth, but do not command Duncan's murder; Banquo is principled, but still tempted; courage can be admired without excusing tyranny. Explain the qualification with evidence instead of simply disagreeing with the question.

Building a whole-play argument

The essay question is worth 25 marks: 20 for the literary response and 5 for spelling, punctuation, vocabulary and sentence control. Aim for about 40 minutes, including a short plan and final check.

  1. Decide your answer to the exact question. For Lady Macbeth's change: she moves from directing others to being unable to control herself.
  2. Choose three or four moments that develop the argument across the play.
  3. Explain how Shakespeare presents the character or theme, not just what happens.
  4. Link the stages: initially, however, increasingly, by the end.
  5. Finish with the significance of the change, rather than a list of repeated points.

Write connected paragraphs in the exam. A useful paragraph does more than name a technique: it explains why that choice matters here. An interpretation, evidence and a developed explanation work together.

For Shakespeare, the assessed focus is your understanding, analysis and writing accuracy. Context can illuminate a reading, but a detached history paragraph takes space away from close analysis. Avoid diagnosing a character or stating Shakespeare's purpose as a proven fact when it is an interpretation.
