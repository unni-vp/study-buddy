Atoms, mixtures & electrons

Atoms, elements and compounds

Atoms are the tiny building blocks of matter. Think of elements as different types of building brick; a compound is a fixed design made from more than one type, joined chemically.

  • An atom is the smallest part of an element that can exist.
  • An element contains atoms with the same number of protons. Its symbol may have one or two letters: oxygen is O, sodium is Na. The second letter is lower case.
  • A compound contains two or more elements chemically combined in fixed proportions. Water is H₂O: two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom.
  • A mixture contains substances that are not chemically combined. Their chemical properties remain unchanged, and their proportions can vary.
  • A chemical reaction forms new substances and often transfers energy. Compounds require chemical reactions to separate them into their elements; mixtures can be separated physically.

A periodic table is supplied in the exam. Also recognise Br bromine, I iodine, Fe iron, Cu copper, Zn zinc, Ag silver and Pb lead, and use the table to identify other symbols.

  • A formula gives the ratio of atoms: CO₂ contains one carbon and two oxygen atoms; Ca(OH)₂ contains one calcium, two oxygen and two hydrogen atoms.
  • Compounds containing two elements often end in -ide, such as sodium chloride. Names ending in -ate often indicate oxygen is present too, such as sodium sulfate.
  • Word equation: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide.
  • Balanced symbol equation: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. There are two Mg atoms and two O atoms on each side.
  • Balance by changing coefficients in front of formulae. Never change a substance's formula to make the equation balance.

Separating mixtures

Choose the method from the substances' physical properties. Separation does not create new substances.

MethodWhen and how it worksExample
FiltrationAn insoluble solid stays as the residue; liquid passes through as the filtrateSand from water
CrystallisationEvaporate some solvent, then cool a concentrated solution so crystals form; filter and dry themCopper sulfate crystals from solution
Simple distillationA liquid evaporates, then cools and condenses in a condenser; collect the distillatePure water from salt solution
Fractional distillationSeparates miscible liquids with different boiling points; repeated evaporation/condensation in a column improves separationEthanol and water
ChromatographySubstances move at different speeds because they have different attractions to the solvent and stationary phaseDyes in ink
  • To recover both salt and sand: add water to dissolve the salt, filter off the sand, then crystallise salt from the filtrate.
  • In distillation, the lower-boiling liquid is usually collected first. The condenser removes thermal energy so vapour becomes liquid; cooling water enters at the lower end and leaves at the upper end.
  • For chromatography, use a pencil baseline above the solvent level. Ink would dissolve, and a submerged sample could wash into the solvent. Mark the solvent front promptly.
  • Do not use filtration to remove dissolved salt: dissolved particles pass through the filter.
  • Wear goggles and handle hot glassware carefully. Keep flammable solvents away from flames.

  • Atomic number = number of protons. It identifies the element.
  • Mass number = protons + neutrons. Therefore neutrons = mass number − atomic number.
  • A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so their charges cancel.
  • Ions form when atoms lose or gain electrons. The nucleus is unchanged: losing electrons makes a positive ion; gaining them makes a negative ion.

Example: sodium-23 has atomic number 11. It contains 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons. A Na⁺ ion still has 11 protons and 12 neutrons, but only 10 electrons.

Size and scale

  • Typical atomic radius: about 0.1 nm = 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • Nuclear radius: around 10⁻¹⁴ m, roughly ten thousand times smaller or less. Almost all the atom's mass is in this tiny region.
  • 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m. To convert nanometres to metres, multiply by 10⁻⁹.
  • A shell drawing is a model: nuclei and electrons cannot both be drawn to true scale on a useful classroom diagram.

Isotopes and relative atomic mass

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Their mass numbers differ.

Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 both have 17 protons. They have 18 and 20 neutrons respectively. Neutral atoms of both have 17 electrons, so their chemical properties are very similar.

Relative atomic mass, Aᵣ, is an average that accounts for the abundance of each isotope. It is not necessarily a whole number and has no unit.

Aᵣ = Σ(isotope mass × percentage abundance) ÷ 100

If a sample contains 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37:

Aᵣ = [(35 × 75) + (37 × 25)] ÷ 100 = 35.5

This is like finding a class average when many more students scored 35 than 37: the mean lies closer to the more common value. Do not take a simple halfway average unless the abundances are equal.

Electrons and the periodic table

Electrons occupy the lowest available energy levels first. For the first 20 elements, fill shells using 2, 8, 8, then 2.

  • Oxygen: 8 electrons → 2,6.
  • Sodium: 11 electrons → 2,8,1.
  • Calcium: 20 electrons → 2,8,8,2.

The modern periodic table puts elements in increasing atomic number.

  • A period is a row. Its number tells you how many electron shells are occupied in the neutral atom.
  • A group is a column. Main-group elements share the same number of outer electrons, giving similar chemical reactions.
  • Group 1 has one outer electron; Group 7 has seven. Group 0 has a full outer shell: eight electrons, except helium, which has two.
  • Metals lie mainly to the left and centre; non-metals mainly to the right. Metals form positive ions by losing electrons. Non-metals often gain electrons or share them in covalent bonds.
  • Metals usually conduct heat/electricity and can be bent or shaped. Non-metals generally conduct poorly; physical exceptions include graphite.
