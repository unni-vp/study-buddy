Food, water & energy resources

Energy choices

Renewable sources are naturally replenished, such as wind, solar, hydroelectric, tidal and biomass. Non-renewable sources are finite, such as coal, oil, natural gas and uranium.

ResourceRenewable?Share of UK primary energy use (2025)How it is used / strengthsLimitations
CoalNo; finite fossil fuel.About 0.8%, including other solid fuelsEnergy-dense fuel; historically important in UK power and industry.High carbon emissions and air pollution. UK coal-fired power ended in September 2024.
Natural gasNo; finite fossil fuel.About 35.5%Heating and flexible electricity generation; power stations can respond to demand.Carbon dioxide on combustion, methane leakage and exposure to import prices.
OilNo; finite fossil fuel.About 38.8%Especially important for transport fuels and chemical feedstocks.Emissions, spill risk and insecure or costly supplies.
Nuclear: uraniumNo; uranium is finite.About 4.8%Large, dependable electricity output with low operational carbon emissions.Long construction, high capital cost, radioactive waste and decommissioning.
WindYes.About 4.6%Onshore and offshore turbines generate low-operational-carbon electricity.Output varies with wind; grid links, storage and suitable locations matter.
SolarYes.About 1.1%Panels use sunlight; rooftops can generate near consumers.No solar output at night and lower winter output; large farms use land.
HydroelectricityYes, through the water cycle.About 0.3%Stored water can provide controllable power.Dams flood land and alter river habitats; suitable sites are limited.
Tidal and waveYes.Less than 0.1%Tides are predictable; waves offer energy around exposed coasts.High engineering costs, marine maintenance and effects on habitats or navigation.
Bioenergy and wasteMixed: biomass and biogas can be renewable when replaced sustainably; fossil-based waste is not.About 12.6%Includes wood, biogas and waste fuels for heat and electricity, plus biofuels for transport.Land, transport, combustion emissions and methane leakage affect sustainability.

The shares refer to UK primary energy consumption in 2025, covering transport, heating and energy used to generate electricity. Net electricity imports account for about 1.6%.

Renewable describes whether a resource is replenished. Low carbon describes its emissions. They are different tests: nuclear is low carbon during operation but its uranium fuel is non-renewable. Biomass regrowth can replace feedstock, but production and transport can still cause emissions.

Insulating a home reduces the energy it needs; building a power station increases supply. Comparing both avoids assuming that every future shortage must be solved by producing more energy.

Solar planning example: Hacheston, Suffolk: An appeal for a 25 MW solar park on about 50 hectares of agricultural land was dismissed in May 2014. Renewable electricity was a benefit, but landscape, heritage and the loss of productive farmland weighed against it. The example shows that a renewable source can still have local environmental costs.

Wind planning example: Kirkby Moor, Cumbria: A 2015 proposal would have replaced 12 turbines with six turbines up to 115 m high. Cumbria County Council objected because of landscape, visual and cumulative effects near sensitive habitats. Fewer turbines do not automatically mean a smaller visual impact if the replacements are much taller. This describes that proposal and objection, rather than the site's current planning status.
