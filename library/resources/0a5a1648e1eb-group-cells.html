Cells, batteries and fuel cells

Cells and batteries

A chemical cell converts energy from a chemical reaction into electrical energy. A simple cell uses two different metals in contact with an electrolyte, connected through an external circuit.

Two routes for charge: electrode reactions drive electrons through the external wires. Inside the cell, mobile ions in the electrolyte carry charge and complete the circuit. Electrons do not travel through the electrolyte as they do through the wires.

Cell voltage depends on both the electrode materials and the electrolyte. Changing either can change the measured potential difference.

A battery consists of two or more cells connected in series. Their voltages add when they face the same way. Three 1.5 V cells therefore provide 4.5 V.

Comparing cells

Connect a voltmeter to a fixed reference electrode and a test metal in the same electrolyte. Change the test metal while keeping electrolyte type/concentration, temperature and the electrode setup constant.

In a given series of measurements, the polarity and voltage help order metal reactivity. Use the stated lead convention: a negative voltage means the polarity is reversed relative to the chosen reference, not that the cell has “negative energy”. Do not assume the highest signed reading is always the most reactive without checking the setup.

Cell typeWhy it stops or how it is renewed
Non-rechargeable, including ordinary alkaline cellsA reactant is used up, so the reaction stops
RechargeableAn external electrical current reverses the chemical reactions, restoring reactants

Compare lifetime cost, capacity, mass, recharge time, replacement frequency and disposal. Do not try to recharge a cell designed for single use.

Polarity and energy supply

A chemical cell drives electrons around the external circuit from its negative electrode towards its positive electrode. A voltmeter reverses sign if its leads are reversed. The changed sign describes the measurement direction, not a negative quantity of stored energy.

Using more electrode material may allow a cell to operate for longer, but it does not necessarily increase voltage. Voltage depends on the chemical system. Connecting cells in series adds their voltages only when their orientations are taken into account.
