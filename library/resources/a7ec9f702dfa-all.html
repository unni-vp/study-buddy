UK environmental challenges

The UK's climate

Why the weather changes

The UK lies where different air masses meet. An air mass is a large body of air with similar temperature and moisture.

Air massUsual source and weather
Tropical maritimeAtlantic Ocean; mild and wet
Polar maritimeNorth Atlantic; cool and showery
Tropical continentalContinental Europe or North Africa; hot and dry in summer
Polar continentalEastern Europe; cold and dry, sometimes snowy after crossing the North Sea
Arctic maritimeArctic; very cold, often with wintry showers
  • Prevailing south-westerly winds often bring moist Atlantic air.
  • The North Atlantic Drift transfers warm water towards north-west Europe and helps keep UK winters milder than places at similar latitude.
  • Continentality means land heats and cools faster than sea. Inland areas usually have a greater temperature range than coastal areas.
  • Relief makes western uplands wetter: air rises, cools and condenses, producing relief rainfall.

Extreme weather

UK extremes include strong winds, heatwaves, cold spells, drought and intense rainfall. An event becomes hazardous when it affects people, property, infrastructure or ecosystems.

Link the air mass to the weather

An air mass gains characteristics from the surface over which it forms, then changes as it travels. Maritime air crosses water and can carry moisture. Continental air is usually drier, but crossing the North Sea can add moisture before it reaches Britain.

Tropical continental air can bring summer heat, while polar continental air can bring very cold winter weather. The same source direction does not guarantee an identical temperature in every season. A long-lasting high-pressure system can bring settled dry conditions; persistent low-pressure systems can bring repeated fronts, rain and strong winds.

Warm Atlantic water transfers heat to the air above it. Prevailing winds help carry this influence towards Britain. The sea also heats and cools more slowly than land, moderating coastal temperatures. Inland locations usually have a greater annual temperature range.

Western uplands force moist air to rise. As it rises it cools, water vapour condenses and rain falls. The air reaching the sheltered east has often lost some moisture, helping explain the UK's rainfall contrast.

Flooding: Somerset Levels

Place and physical setting

The Somerset Levels and Moors are a broad, flat wetland landscape between the Mendip, Blackdown and Quantock Hills. Rivers including the Parrett and Tone cross land drained by channels known as rhynes.

About 18% of Somerset lies below the average high-tide level, and the Levels and Moors cover more than 60,000 hectares. This makes drainage slow and allows a small rise in water level to spread over a large area.

Causes of the 2013–14 flooding

Prolonged rain: A powerful jet stream steered repeated Atlantic low-pressure systems towards the UK. At least 12 major winter storms affected the country from mid-December 2013 to mid-February 2014. Persistent rain saturated the soil, so little more water could infiltrate and surface runoff increased.

Restricted drainage: The low-lying Levels drain slowly. High tides and high river levels restricted discharge towards the Bristol Channel, while river flow exceeded channel and pumping capacity. Silt in parts of the Parrett and Tone reduced capacity further. Dredging could help flow locally, but could not remove the underlying problem of exceptional rainfall on low land.

People and exposure: Homes, roads and farms occupy the floodplain, including land below high-tide level. This means a flood can disrupt settlements and transport as well as cover agricultural land. The severity of a disaster therefore depends on both the physical event and what has been built in its path.

Scale and effects

Flood extent and homes: In early 2014, about 100 million m³ of floodwater was reported across roughly 65 km² of the Levels. Across the wider Somerset event, the county council reported about 600 homes affected, including isolation and disruption. The wider economic assessment counted at least 165 homes flooded internally, a narrower measure than all homes affected. Northmoor was evacuated, and Muchelney was cut off by road.

Social effects: Damaged homes, disrupted journeys and interrupted services left residents stressed and isolated. The wider economic assessment recorded 81 road closures, showing why even households and businesses outside the flooded area faced disruption.

Economic effects: Flooded farmland meant livestock had to be moved or sold. Businesses lost trade and faced clean-up costs, while disrupted roads affected customers and deliveries. An economic impact study estimated the cost to Somerset at up to about £147 million. The wider assessment recorded around 7,000 businesses indirectly affected, rather than all being flooded inside.

Environmental effects: Floodwater carried sediment and pollutants and damaged some farmland and habitats. Bare or waterlogged agricultural land could not be used normally for weeks. Some wetland areas also benefited from restored wet conditions, so effects differed between habitats rather than being uniformly harmful.

Immediate response

Emergency support: Emergency services, the military and volunteers helped evacuate residents and deliver supplies to cut-off communities. Boats maintained access where roads were impassable, while police patrols and road closures helped reduce danger.

Pumping water: Temporary and permanent pumps removed water when river and tidal conditions allowed. High-capacity pumps were brought from the Netherlands. These actions protected life and increased drainage, but could not quickly empty such a large area while surrounding water levels stayed high.

Long-term management

Dredging and drainage: Sections of the Parrett and Tone were dredged after the floods. Programmes in 2014, 2016 and 2018 removed about 270,500 m³ of silt. 21 permanent pumping stations help manage water levels, but channels and pumps need continued maintenance.

Keeping access open: The Muchelney–Drayton road was raised, and structures at Beer Wall were improved to maintain access and move water. Raised roads reduce isolation even when surrounding land floods.

Catchment-wide planning: The Somerset Rivers Authority coordinates drainage, maintenance, resilient roads and natural flood management. Upstream soil management, tree planting and temporary storage slow runoff before it reaches the Levels. The 20-Year Flood Action Plan combines local works with wider funding and coordination.

ScaleNamed actionWhat it achieves and what limits it
LocalEvacuation, supplies and access support for places such as Muchelney.Protects life and keeps isolated communities supplied; it does not remove the floodwater source.

Evaluation

Dredging and pumping improve channel flow and reduce the duration of some floods. Raised roads protect access even when land floods. Natural flood management adds storage upstream and may improve habitats.

No measure removes risk from land below high-tide level during exceptional rainfall. Pumping costs energy, channels need repeated maintenance, and moving water faster can transfer risk. The strongest strategy combines maintenance, storage, resilient infrastructure, warnings and acceptance that some floodplain land will still hold water.

Exam pointer

Use evidence in a chain: saturated low-lying land + prolonged rain + restricted drainage → 65 km² flooded and communities cut off → dredging, pumping, raised roads and upstream storage.

From rainfall to river response

A storm hydrograph shows how discharge changes during and after rainfall. Discharge is the volume passing a point each second, usually measured in m³/s. Rainfall and discharge use different units.

FeatureWhat to read
Rising limbDischarge increases as more water reaches the channel.
Peak dischargeThe highest river flow during the event.
Lag timeThe interval between peak rainfall and peak discharge.
Falling limbFlow decreases as stored water drains away.

Saturated ground and impermeable surfaces can increase rapid surface runoff, giving a steeper rise and shorter lag. Soil, vegetation and temporary storage can delay water. The Somerset event involved repeated storms and prolonged restricted drainage, so a single sharp rainfall peak would not describe the whole episode.

Food, water and energy

Food production

Mechanisation means using machinery for tasks such as ploughing, sowing and harvesting. It increases the area that can be worked and reduces labour needed per unit of output. Larger fields make machinery easier to use, but removing hedgerows reduces habitats and wildlife connections.

Intensive farming raises output through inputs such as fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation. Fertilisers supply nutrients, but runoff can enrich rivers, encouraging algal growth and oxygen depletion. Pesticides can affect non-target organisms. Heavy machinery may compact soil, reducing infiltration and increasing runoff.

Commercial fishing uses boats and equipment to catch fish for sale. Powerful vessels, sonar and large nets improve catches, but taking fish faster than stocks reproduce causes overfishing. Some methods also damage the seabed or catch unwanted species. Catch limits, suitable mesh sizes, protected areas and monitoring can help stocks recover, although restrictions affect fishers' short-term income.

Water supply

UK rainfall and demand do not match neatly. Wetter northern and western uplands can have a surplus, while some more densely populated southern and eastern areas face pressure on supplies, especially during dry periods. A wet national climate does not guarantee that every place has enough usable water at the right time.

StrategyHow it helpsEnvironmental and practical trade-off
ReservoirA dam stores water during wetter periods so it can be released when needed.Flooding the valley changes habitats and may displace land uses. The dam also alters downstream flow and sediment movement.
Water transferPipelines, canals or connected rivers move water from a source area to an area of demand.Construction and pumping cost money and energy. Removing too much water can harm the source river and its users.
Leak reductionRepairing pipes makes more of the treated supply available to consumers.Finding and replacing damaged infrastructure can be expensive and disruptive.
Demand managementEfficient fittings, metering and suitable behaviour reduce the amount required.Savings depend on uptake, affordability and how people use the equipment.

These choices can be combined. Reducing unnecessary demand can delay the need for a new reservoir or reduce the amount transferred.

Energy choices

Renewable sources are naturally replenished, such as wind, solar, hydroelectric, tidal and biomass. Non-renewable sources are finite, such as coal, oil, natural gas and uranium.

The shares refer to UK primary energy consumption in 2025, covering transport, heating and energy used to generate electricity. Net electricity imports account for about 1.6%.

Renewable describes whether a resource is replenished. Low carbon describes its emissions. They are different tests: nuclear is low carbon during operation but its uranium fuel is non-renewable. Biomass regrowth can replace feedstock, but production and transport can still cause emissions.

Insulating a home reduces the energy it needs; building a power station increases supply. Comparing both avoids assuming that every future shortage must be solved by producing more energy.

Solar planning example: Hacheston, Suffolk: An appeal for a 25 MW solar park on about 50 hectares of agricultural land was dismissed in May 2014. Renewable electricity was a benefit, but landscape, heritage and the loss of productive farmland weighed against it. The example shows that a renewable source can still have local environmental costs.

Wind planning example: Kirkby Moor, Cumbria: A 2015 proposal would have replaced 12 turbines with six turbines up to 115 m high. Cumbria County Council objected because of landscape, visual and cumulative effects near sensitive habitats. Fewer turbines do not automatically mean a smaller visual impact if the replacements are much taller. This describes that proposal and objection, rather than the site's current planning status.

Changing UK energy supply

Since 1950, the UK's energy mix has shifted as coal declined, oil and gas expanded, nuclear developed and renewable electricity grew.

Changes are influenced by:

  • resource availability and price;
  • technology and infrastructure;
  • energy security;
  • climate targets and environmental rules;
  • government subsidies, planning and international agreements;
  • public acceptance.

Demand also changes with population, industry, transport, household technology and energy efficiency.

DUKES 2025 figures for 2024: renewables generated 50.4% of UK electricity, compared with 31.8% from fossil fuels. This is a snapshot of electricity generation, not all the energy used for heating, transport and industry.

The long-term pattern is a move away from coal and towards renewable electricity. Gas still matters because it can provide flexible generation when weather-dependent output is low.

Energy security and decision making

Energy security means having a dependable supply that meets needs at an affordable cost. Producing more energy within the UK can reduce reliance on imported fuels, but domestic production can still depend on weather, equipment, investment or imported components.

FactorHow it influences the UK's choices
EconomicsConstruction costs, fuel prices, maintenance and network upgrades affect affordability. A plant with a low running cost may still need expensive investment first.
PoliticsPlanning rules, support payments and agreements influence which projects are built. Producer decisions and conflict abroad can change imported fuel prices.
EnvironmentCarbon emissions, habitat change, visual effects and waste affect whether a project is acceptable. Benefits and costs occur at different scales.
ReliabilityA varied mix, stronger grid connections, storage and controllable output help meet demand when one source is unavailable.

Organisations such as OPEC, a group of oil-producing countries, can influence world supply through production decisions. International climate agreements encourage lower-carbon choices. These influences affect the UK even when the decisions are made elsewhere.

Fracking: obtaining gas changes the environment

Fracking, or hydraulic fracturing, releases gas trapped in shale. A well is drilled into the rock, and a mixture containing water and sand is pumped in at high pressure. The rock fractures, and sand helps keep openings available so gas can flow towards the well.

Potential benefits include an additional domestic gas supply, jobs and local contracts. Costs include water use, wastewater handling, heavy vehicle movements, noise and changes to the landscape. Poor control can create contamination risks, and operations can trigger small earthquakes. Burning the extracted gas releases carbon dioxide, while methane leakage adds to climate effects. An additional domestic fossil-fuel supply is therefore not the same as a renewable or zero-carbon supply.

Reduce demand as well as increasing supply

Energy efficiency provides a similar service using less energy: insulation reduces the energy needed to maintain a comfortable home temperature. Conservation reduces unnecessary use, such as turning off lighting in an empty room.

Local households, businesses and councils can improve buildings and travel choices. National decisions shape building standards, funding, large infrastructure and the energy mix. Upfront costs can prevent some households from making improvements, so the availability of support affects how fairly benefits are shared.

Energy case studies

Hinkley Point C, Somerset

Place, purpose and scale

Hinkley Point C is under construction on the Somerset coast beside earlier nuclear stations. It will use two reactors and is designed to supply electricity for about six million homes for around 60 years, equal to roughly 7% of UK electricity demand.

The project received its Development Consent Order in March 2013. When the government approved the investment in 2016, it described an £18 billion project led by EDF, with Chinese partner CGN. That is a historical estimate, not the final construction bill. Its national purpose is to provide dependable, low-operational-carbon electricity and improve energy security. Planned generation begins only once the station is operating; construction impacts occur before that benefit is available.

Economic and social benefits

Employment and spending: Construction was expected to support about 25,000 employment opportunities and around 1,000 apprenticeships. Project estimates suggested about £1.5 billion could enter the local economy through wages and contracts. Long-term operation also creates skilled work and demand for local suppliers.

Energy security: Once operating, the station is designed to provide dependable electricity, helping supply demand when wind or solar output is low. This benefit depends on the plant being completed and available, so it is separate from the employment created during construction.

Local and environmental costs

Construction increases traffic, demand for temporary accommodation and pressure on local services. A very large coastal site changes the landscape and can disturb habitats. Agreements with local authorities fund transport, housing and community mitigation.

Nuclear generation produces low carbon emissions during operation, but construction has embodied emissions. Radioactive waste needs secure long-term management, and decommissioning adds future cost.

Evaluation

Hinkley Point C offers very large, dependable low-carbon output and substantial employment. Its drawbacks are high cost, long construction, local disruption and responsibility for nuclear waste. The balance depends on scale and time: national consumers may gain long-term energy security, while nearby communities face construction traffic, accommodation demand and landscape change. Jobs and supply contracts benefit some people, but local impacts still need management. The station is strongest as one part of a diverse low-carbon system.

Dryholme Farm, Silloth, Cumbria

Place and scheme

Dryholme Farm near Silloth developed a farm-based anaerobic digestion plant. A 2015 government feasibility report listed its installation cost at about £4 million, illustrating the substantial initial investment needed even for a local scheme. Planning documents described a site of about 1.97 hectares, designed to use roughly 15,000–16,000 tonnes of crops each year plus farm slurry.

Feedstock included maize and grass silage, triticale, vegetables and wheat. At least 70% of crop feedstock was intended to come from the farm's own 368 acres, with other local farms supplying additional material.

How it works

  1. Crops and slurry enter a sealed digester without oxygen.
  2. Microorganisms break the material down and release methane-rich biogas.
  3. A combined heat and power engine burns the gas.
  4. The plant exports electricity to the grid and can use heat locally.
  5. Digestate returns nutrients to farmland as fertiliser.

The plant has a capacity of about 1 MW, enough in planning estimates for roughly 1,200 homes, and became operational in 2011.

Two useful outputs: biogas supplies energy; digestate returns nutrients to farmland. They follow different routes.

Benefits

Energy and nutrients: Dryholme supplies controllable renewable electricity, turning farm slurry and crops into biogas. Digestate returns nutrients to fields and may reduce the need for manufactured fertiliser. The useful outputs are therefore both energy and a material that supports farming.

Farm income and local links: Electricity sales diversify farm income, reducing reliance on one source of earnings. Using local feedstock can limit transport distance and link the plant to nearby farms, although deliveries are still needed.

Costs and limitations

Land and transport: Growing energy crops uses land that could produce food. Intensive cultivation may also reduce biodiversity. Feedstock deliveries create traffic, noise and emissions, so local sourcing reduces some impacts without removing them.

Pollution and scale: Poor management can cause odour, water pollution or methane leakage. The scheme's roughly 1 MW output is useful locally but small compared with national demand. Its benefits depend on safe operation as well as the amount of electricity produced.

Evaluation

Dryholme is most sustainable when it uses nearby wastes and residues, prevents methane leakage and manages digestate safely. Heavy reliance on purpose-grown crops weakens the benefit because land, fertiliser and transport are required. It is a valuable local energy strategy, but cannot replace national-scale supply.

Evaluating a strategy

Use four tests:

  • Environmental: emissions, habitats, land and waste.
  • Economic: construction, running cost, jobs and energy price.
  • Social: reliability, health, disruption and public acceptance.
  • Scale and time: local impacts may differ from national benefits; short-term costs may support long-term supply.
