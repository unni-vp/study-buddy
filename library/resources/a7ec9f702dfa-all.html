UK environmental challenges

The UK's climate

Why the weather changes

The UK lies where different air masses meet. An air mass is a large body of air with similar temperature and moisture.

Air massUsual source and weather
Tropical maritimeAtlantic Ocean; mild and wet
Polar maritimeNorth Atlantic; cool and showery
Tropical continentalContinental Europe or North Africa; hot and dry in summer
Polar continentalEastern Europe; cold and dry, sometimes snowy after crossing the North Sea
Arctic maritimeArctic; very cold, often with wintry showers
  • Prevailing south-westerly winds often bring moist Atlantic air.
  • The North Atlantic Drift transfers warm water towards north-west Europe and helps keep UK winters milder than places at similar latitude.
  • Continentality means land heats and cools faster than sea. Inland areas usually have a greater temperature range than coastal areas.
  • Relief makes western uplands wetter: air rises, cools and condenses, producing relief rainfall.

Extreme weather

UK extremes include strong winds, heatwaves, cold spells, drought and intense rainfall. An event becomes hazardous when it affects people, property, infrastructure or ecosystems.

Flooding: Somerset Levels

The Somerset Levels are the flood case study used in this course.

Causes

  • Prolonged and heavy rainfall saturated the ground and increased river discharge.
  • The low-lying, flat landscape drains slowly.
  • High water levels, limited channel capacity and tidal conditions can make drainage harder.
  • Farming, settlements and drainage systems affect how quickly water reaches channels and where it is stored.

Effects

  • Homes, farms and roads can be flooded or cut off.
  • Livestock, crops and local businesses may be affected.
  • Travel, services and daily life are disrupted.
  • Floodwater can damage habitats or carry pollutants, but flooding may also deposit sediment.

Management

  • Short term: warnings, evacuation, rescue, temporary barriers and pumps.
  • Long term: maintaining or dredging selected channels, improving drainage, raising roads or defences, and storing water on floodplains or wetlands.
  • Effective management combines methods because moving water away faster can transfer risk downstream.

Exam judgement: A response is successful if it reduces risk over time without creating unacceptable cost or environmental damage elsewhere.

Food, water and energy

Food production

  • Mechanisation uses machinery to raise output and reduce labour needs.
  • Intensive farming can increase yields but may cause soil damage, pollution or habitat loss.
  • Commercial fishing supplies food and jobs, but overfishing can reduce stocks and disrupt food webs.
  • Sustainable approaches include quotas, protected areas, careful fertiliser use and restoring soils and habitats.

Water supply

  • Reservoirs store water but flood land and alter river ecosystems.
  • Water transfer moves water from areas of surplus to areas of demand, but it is costly and can affect donor areas.
  • Reducing leakage and demand may avoid some environmental impacts of new supply schemes.

Energy choices

Renewable sources are naturally replenished, such as wind, solar, hydroelectric, tidal and biomass. Non-renewable sources are finite, such as coal, oil, natural gas and uranium.

ChoiceMain strengthsMain limitations
Wind and solarRenewable; low operational carbon emissionsIntermittent; location and storage matter
Biomass/biogasCan use organic material and produce controllable energyLand, feedstock, transport and emissions must be managed
Fossil fuelsReliable and energy denseFinite; greenhouse gases and pollution
NuclearLarge, reliable output with low operational carbon emissionsHigh cost; long construction; radioactive waste

Changing UK energy supply

Since 1950, the UK's energy mix has shifted as coal declined, oil and gas expanded, nuclear developed and renewable electricity grew.

Changes are influenced by:

  • resource availability and price;
  • technology and infrastructure;
  • energy security;
  • climate targets and environmental rules;
  • government subsidies, planning and international agreements;
  • public acceptance.

Demand also changes with population, industry, transport, household technology and energy efficiency.

Energy case studies

Hinkley Point C, Somerset

Hinkley Point C is used to consider a large national energy project.

  • It is intended to provide a large, dependable supply of nuclear electricity.
  • Nuclear generation has low operational carbon emissions and can support energy security.
  • Construction creates jobs and contracts and requires major transport and infrastructure planning.
  • Disadvantages include very high cost, long construction, local disruption and the need to manage radioactive waste safely.
  • Its sustainability depends on weighing long-term dependable output against financial, environmental and waste-management costs.

Dryholme Farm, Silloth, Cumbria

Dryholme Farm is used to consider anaerobic digestion.

  1. Organic material is placed in a sealed tank without oxygen.
  2. Microorganisms break it down and produce biogas, containing methane.
  3. The gas can generate heat and electricity.
  4. The remaining digestate can be used as fertiliser when managed safely.

Benefits can include using organic material, generating controllable renewable energy and reducing reliance on fossil fuels. Limitations include construction cost, transport of feedstock, odour or traffic concerns and the need to prevent methane leakage.

Evaluating a strategy

Use four tests:

  • Environmental: emissions, habitats, land and waste.
  • Economic: construction, running cost, jobs and energy price.
  • Social: reliability, health, disruption and public acceptance.
  • Scale and time: local impacts may differ from national benefits; short-term costs may support long-term supply.

High-yield checklist

  • Link UK weather to air masses, ocean influence, relief and continentality.
  • Build a flood answer as physical cause + human influence + effects + management.
  • Compare supply schemes with demand reduction.
  • Know how the UK energy mix has changed and why.
  • Use Hinkley Point C and Dryholme Farm to evaluate contrasting energy strategies.
