Ecosystem basics

How ecosystems work

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms interacting with one another and with their non-living surroundings. Think of it as a web: changing one strand can affect several others.

ComponentMeaning and role
BioticLiving parts, including plants, animals, microorganisms and people.
AbioticNon-living conditions, including temperature, rainfall, light, water and soil characteristics.
ProducerAn organism such as a green plant or alga that makes food using light energy.
ConsumerAn organism that obtains food by eating other organisms.
DecomposerAn organism that breaks down dead material and waste, releasing nutrients that can be used again.

Soil contains both non-living material and living organisms. Calling soil conditions abiotic does not mean there are no organisms in soil.

A food chain follows one feeding route. A food web links several routes, because an organism can have more than one food source or predator. Arrows show the direction in which food and its energy are transferred, from the organism eaten towards the eater.

Interdependence means that parts rely on one another. Less rainfall may reduce plant growth, leaving herbivores with less food and predators with fewer prey. Animals can also affect plants through grazing, pollination or seed dispersal.

Nutrients are recycled, but energy is transferred through the system and eventually dissipated as heat. Nutrient cycling therefore needs dead material, decomposers, soil and plant uptake; it is not simply another name for a food chain.

The world's major ecosystems

Climate is the main control. Temperature and rainfall affect vegetation, and vegetation affects soil and animal life.

EcosystemDistributionClimatePlants / producersAnimals
Coral reefWarm tropical and subtropical seas, including Andros in The Bahamas.Warm, clear, sunlit shallow water; reef-building corals are sensitive to heat stress.Algae within coral tissues photosynthesise; nearby seagrass beds are also productive habitats.Coral polyps, reef fish, crustaceans and sea turtles. Coral is an animal, not a plant.
Tropical rainforestNear the Equator: Amazon, Congo and South-East Asia, with other named regions.Hot and humid throughout the year, commonly with more than 2,000 mm annual rain.Evergreen layered forest, lianas and epiphytes; many broad leaves shed water.Very diverse insects, birds, reptiles and mammals; examples include toucans, monkeys and jaguars in the Americas.
PolarHigh latitudes in the Arctic and Antarctica.Long cold winters, brief cool summers and generally low precipitation.Sparse vegetation; mosses, lichens and small plants occur where conditions permit.Polar bears occur in the Arctic; penguins in the Southern Hemisphere. Marine life includes seals and whales.
Tropical grassland: savannaSeasonally wet tropics, such as East Africa and northern Australia.Hot all year, with a marked wet and dry season.Grasses with scattered drought-adapted trees, such as acacias and baobabs in Africa.African examples include zebras, elephants and lions; species differ between continents.
Temperate grasslandMid-latitude continental interiors, such as the North American prairies and Eurasian steppes.Warm or hot summers, cold winters; too little rainfall for continuous forest.Grasses dominate; many survive grazing, fire and seasonal cold.Bison on North American prairies, burrowing mammals and grassland birds.
Temperate forestMid-latitudes, including western Europe, eastern North America and eastern Asia.Distinct seasons; rainfall supports woodland.Broad-leaved deciduous trees, shrubs and seasonal ground plants; evergreen types also occur.Deer, foxes, woodland birds, insects and many decomposers.
Hot desertOften near 20–30° north and south, including the Sahara and Australian deserts.Usually less than 250 mm rain a year; large daily temperature ranges are common.Sparse drought-adapted shrubs; cacti in parts of the Americas store water in stems.Reptiles and nocturnal mammals; camels store fat, not water, in their humps.

Major rainforest areas include the Amazon Basin, Central America, the Congo Basin, Madagascar, South-East Asia and Australasia. Major warm-water reefs include the Great Barrier Reef, Red Sea reefs, New Caledonia, the Mesoamerican Reef, Florida Reef and Andros Barrier Reef.

Why the pattern varies

Near the Equator, strong sunlight and frequent rising moist air support hot, wet conditions. Around the subtropical high-pressure belts, sinking air often suppresses rainfall, helping explain many hot deserts. Higher latitudes receive less intense sunlight and have greater seasonal contrasts.

Local conditions modify the broad pattern. Higher land is usually cooler; waterlogged ground supports different plants from well-drained soil. Soil nutrients and acidity also affect growth. A map showing a biome does not mean every part of that region has identical vegetation.

Polar regions and hot deserts can both have low rainfall, but they have very different temperatures and adaptations. A climate graph should therefore be read using both the temperature line and rainfall bars, including seasonal changes, rather than total rainfall alone.
