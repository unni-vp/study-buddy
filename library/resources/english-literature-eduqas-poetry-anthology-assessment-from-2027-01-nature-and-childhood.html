Nature & childhood

The Schoolboy: meaning

  • Blake’s child enjoys nature but suffers under fearful schooling. Bird-in-cage and damaged-plant images link restriction to lost development.
  • Cruel eye: authority becomes surveillance. Appeals to parents make adults responsible for the future.

Blake: how it works

  • Six five-line stanzas; songlike pattern contrasts with confinement. Questions demand an answer rather than accepting adult power.
  • Context: childhood and resistance to restrictive authority. Compare Catrin’s mother–child conflict.

Wordsworth: meaning

  • A solitary walker finds company in dancing daffodils. Later, the inward eye brings the scene back.
  • Personification moves the flowers’ dance into the heart. The gain is emotional wealth.

Wordsworth: how it works

  • Four six-line stanzas, ABABCC. Final stanza shifts from past encounter to recurring present pleasure.
  • Romantic nature, imagination and personal feeling. Solitude becomes rewarding, unlike the opening loneliness.

Blackberry Picking: meaning

  • Heaney’s remembered childhood moves from eager collecting to rotten fruit. Sensory attraction becomes disgust.
  • Blood, eyes and Bluebeard imagery add unease. Desire outlasts knowledge of inevitable decay.

Heaney: how it works

  • Long first stanza gathers excitement; shorter second stanza reverses it. Rural Northern Irish childhood informs concrete details.
  • Compare: Wordsworth preserves pleasure through memory; Heaney cannot preserve fruit. Blake blames restriction, not natural decay.
