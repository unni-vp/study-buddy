Ambition, power & kingship

Ambition / Power

Ambition is wanting to achieve something. Power is the ability to influence or control others. Macbeth's tragedy begins when he values gaining power more than the moral limits on how he should gain it.

Imagine winning a competition by cheating, then becoming more worried about being found out than enjoying the prize. Macbeth gains the crown but spends his reign trying to protect it. Success brings fear instead of satisfaction.

How the theme develops

  • Desire: the witches make kingship seem possible. Macbeth soon sees Malcolm as an obstacle and wants to hide his wishes.
  • Choice: Lady Macbeth pressures him, but his own soliloquy shows that he knows murder is wrong. Ambition overcomes his conscience.
  • Insecurity: after becoming king, he fears Banquo's descendants and acts independently to destroy perceived threats.
  • Emptiness: gaining power damages his marriage, peace of mind and sense of purpose. His final despair exposes the cost of the future he pursued.

Quotations to analyse

“vaulting ambition” — Macbeth, 1.7

Imagery / form — riding metaphor within a soliloquy: The metaphor suggests a rider leaping too far and falling. His ambition reaches beyond a safe or proper limit. Macbeth identifies the danger himself, making his later choice more blameworthy.

“To be thus is nothing” — Macbeth, 3.1

Language — absolute negative judgement: “Thus” means being king. The absolute negative “nothing” makes his achievement seem worthless unless he can feel secure. Wanting power turns into wanting impossible certainty.

“fruitless crown” — Macbeth, 3.1

Imagery — metaphor of infertility: The image of infertility suggests a crown that cannot produce a lasting dynasty. Macbeth fears that his crimes will benefit Banquo's descendants, rather than his own. Power does not give him ownership of the future.

“Tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow” — Macbeth, 5.5

Language / structure — repetition in the late speech of despair: Repetition makes time seem monotonous and empty. The future that once excited him now feels pointless. Shakespeare links the pursuit of greatness with the loss of meaning.

Essay argument: ambition becomes destructive when achievement matters more than conscience; power then deepens Macbeth's insecurity instead of solving it.

Kingship

Kingship asks a different question from ambition: what makes someone worthy to rule? Shakespeare contrasts a ruler who serves the country with a tyrant who treats it as a possession.

Think of a team captain. Wearing the badge gives them authority, but good leadership depends on protecting the team, rewarding effort and earning trust. Macbeth holds the title while losing the qualities that justify it.

Regicide, usurpation and succession

Taking a leader's badge by harming its owner is different from earning the right to lead. Macbeth obtains the crown, but his methods destroy the trust that should support his rule.

  • Regicide: murdering a king. Macbeth kills Duncan in Act 2.
  • Usurpation: taking power without a legitimate right. Macbeth's regicide clears the way for his seizure of the throne.
  • Legitimate succession: the recognised transfer of authority. Duncan publicly names Malcolm Prince of Cumberland in 1.4; Macbeth treats that appointment as an obstacle.
  • Primogeniture: inheritance through the firstborn, traditionally the eldest son. Malcolm is Duncan's eldest son, but base an argument on the appointment shown in the play rather than assuming all Scottish succession followed one simple rule.

Religious and political meaning

The Divine Right of Kings was the belief that royal authority came from God. Within that belief, killing a king attacked a sacred as well as a political order. Macduff's description of Duncan's body as “The Lord's anointed temple” (2.3) uses a religious metaphor: Duncan becomes a sacred building violated by murder. His description of the act as “sacrilegious” gives that judgement explicit force.

Shakespeare wrote during James I's reign, when his company had royal patronage. This helps explain the play's interest in treason and kingship. Its condemnation of regicide could appeal to royal concerns, but that is an interpretation of the play's effect, rather than proof of a single purpose. The play's Scotland and Shakespeare's stage audience are different historical settings.

The cost of taking power

  • Before murder: Macbeth's soliloquies expose conscience and anticipated consequences. The dagger vision occurs before Duncan is killed.
  • After gaining the crown: the “fruitless crown” metaphor shows that possession does not give Macbeth the dynasty or security he wants.
  • During his rule: the “full of scorpions” metaphor makes anxiety painful and poisonous. More violence attempts to protect his first crime, while Scotland suffers.

Three rulers to compare

RulerWhat his leadership reveals
DuncanRewards loyal service and inspires affection. His trusting nature also leaves him vulnerable: he misjudges the old Cawdor and then Macbeth.
MacbethGains the crown through betrayal. He relies increasingly on fear, murder and surveillance, putting his own security ahead of Scotland.
MalcolmTests loyalty before trusting it. He considers the moral qualities of leadership and offers restoration, although the play ends before showing his reign.

Quotations to analyse

Macbeth about Duncan, 1.7:

his virtues
Will plead like angels

Imagery — religious simile: Duncan's goodness is presented as a powerful argument against murder. Macbeth recognises the king's virtues before betraying him. His awareness makes the betrayal deliberate.

“royalty of nature” — Macbeth about Banquo, 3.1

Language / imagery — metaphor distinguishing inner nobility from rank: Banquo has natural nobility despite not wearing a crown. This separates moral authority from a royal title and exposes Macbeth's fear of someone whose qualities he respects.

“Bleed, bleed, poor country!” — Macduff, 4.3

Imagery / language — personification, repetition and exclamation: Scotland is personified as a wounded body. A ruler's private wrongdoing becomes a national injury. Good leadership should heal this damage rather than create it.

“mercy” and “courage” — Malcolm, 4.3

Language — paired virtues within a longer list: These are among the qualities Malcolm lists while pretending to lack the virtues of a king. Their combination matters: leadership needs compassion as well as strength. Macduff's distress at Malcolm's supposed corruption helps Malcolm judge his loyalty.

Essay argument: Shakespeare distinguishes possessing power from deserving it. Malcolm's restoration promises repair, but it cannot undo the lives lost under Macbeth.
