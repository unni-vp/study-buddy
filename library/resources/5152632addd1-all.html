Ecosystems of the planet

How ecosystems work

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms interacting with one another and with their non-living surroundings. Think of it as a web: changing one strand can affect several others.

ComponentMeaning and role
BioticLiving parts, including plants, animals, microorganisms and people.
AbioticNon-living conditions, including temperature, rainfall, light, water and soil characteristics.
ProducerAn organism such as a green plant or alga that makes food using light energy.
ConsumerAn organism that obtains food by eating other organisms.
DecomposerAn organism that breaks down dead material and waste, releasing nutrients that can be used again.

Soil contains both non-living material and living organisms. Calling soil conditions abiotic does not mean there are no organisms in soil.

A food chain follows one feeding route. A food web links several routes, because an organism can have more than one food source or predator. Arrows show the direction in which food and its energy are transferred, from the organism eaten towards the eater.

Interdependence means that parts rely on one another. Less rainfall may reduce plant growth, leaving herbivores with less food and predators with fewer prey. Animals can also affect plants through grazing, pollination or seed dispersal.

Nutrients are recycled, but energy is transferred through the system and eventually dissipated as heat. Nutrient cycling therefore needs dead material, decomposers, soil and plant uptake; it is not simply another name for a food chain.

The world's major ecosystems

Climate is the main control. Temperature and rainfall affect vegetation, and vegetation affects soil and animal life.

Major rainforest areas include the Amazon Basin, Central America, the Congo Basin, Madagascar, South-East Asia and Australasia. Major warm-water reefs include the Great Barrier Reef, Red Sea reefs, New Caledonia, the Mesoamerican Reef, Florida Reef and Andros Barrier Reef.

Why the pattern varies

Near the Equator, strong sunlight and frequent rising moist air support hot, wet conditions. Around the subtropical high-pressure belts, sinking air often suppresses rainfall, helping explain many hot deserts. Higher latitudes receive less intense sunlight and have greater seasonal contrasts.

Local conditions modify the broad pattern. Higher land is usually cooler; waterlogged ground supports different plants from well-drained soil. Soil nutrients and acidity also affect growth. A map showing a biome does not mean every part of that region has identical vegetation.

Polar regions and hot deserts can both have low rainfall, but they have very different temperatures and adaptations. A climate graph should therefore be read using both the temperature line and rainfall bars, including seasonal changes, rather than total rainfall alone.

Tropical rainforest processes

Structure, light and adaptations

Warmth and water support growth throughout much of the year, but plants still compete for light and nutrients. The forest has layers:

LayerConditions and living things
Emergent layerTall trees project above the canopy, receiving strong light and greater exposure to wind.
CanopyCrowns form a dense layer with abundant leaves, fruit and habitats for tree-dwelling animals.
UnderstoreyLess light penetrates. Shade-tolerant plants and young trees grow beneath the canopy.
Forest floorLittle light reaches the ground. Dead material is broken down rapidly, and dense low vegetation is limited in deep shade.

Drip tips help leaves shed rainwater. Buttress roots spread around a tall tree's base, helping support it where roots are shallow. Lianas root in the ground and climb other plants to reach light. Epiphytes use branches as a place to grow; they obtain water and nutrients from rain, debris or material around their roots, rather than normally taking food from the supporting tree.

Animals also connect structure and function. A monkey's grasping limbs and tail help movement among branches. Camouflage can reduce detection by predators or prey. An adaptation is useful because of the particular challenge it addresses.

Nutrient stores and transfers

Imagine nutrients moving between three containers: living biomass, dead litter and soil. The size of a store is different from the speed at which nutrients pass through it.

  1. Plants take up dissolved nutrients through roots and build new biomass.
  2. Fallen leaves, waste and dead organisms transfer nutrients into litter.
  3. Decomposers work rapidly in warm, moist conditions, releasing nutrients into the soil.
  4. Dense roots quickly absorb them again, maintaining the large living store.

Much of the ecosystem's nutrient stock is in living vegetation. Rapid recycling supports rich vegetation even where soil has a relatively small available nutrient store. Leaching removes dissolved nutrients as water moves through soil; erosion removes soil particles. These are different losses.

Clearing trees removes biomass and reduces future litter inputs. Ash can briefly supply nutrients after burning, but exposed ground is vulnerable to leaching and erosion. Fast growth under intact forest does not prove that cleared land will stay fertile.

The water cycle depends on trees

Leaves intercept rainfall, delaying its arrival at the ground. Water then reaches soil by dripping from foliage or flowing down trunks. Some infiltrates; roots absorb water and trees release vapour through transpiration. Moisture returned to the atmosphere contributes to cloud and rainfall formation.

Removing canopy and roots can increase rapid surface runoff and soil loss, while reduced transpiration weakens moisture recycling. The exact effect depends on replacement vegetation, soil and climate, but the key link remains: vegetation both responds to and influences water availability.

Case study: Peruvian Amazon rainforest

Place and importance

Location and climate: The Peruvian Amazon lies east of the Andes and forms part of the wider Amazon Basin. It covers roughly 60% of Peru. The lowland rainforest is hot and wet throughout the year, at around 28 °C with roughly 2,600 mm of annual rainfall. Conditions vary across this large region and with elevation.

Value to people: Hardwoods such as mahogany supply timber, while gold, oil and natural gas create opportunities for extraction. Forest plants provide food and medicines, and tourism supports employment. Indigenous communities depend on forest resources, so development can affect their land, livelihoods and culture.

Value to the planet: A 2011 account recorded 2,937 known amphibian, bird, mammal and reptile species across Peru, with about 16% endemic: found naturally nowhere else. These are national figures for those animal groups, rather than a total of every rainforest animal, including insects. They show why habitat loss can threaten irreplaceable biodiversity. The forest also stores carbon and regulates water. Trees absorb carbon dioxide through photosynthesis and return moisture to the air through transpiration. Clearing and burning trees reduces carbon storage as well as releasing carbon into the atmosphere.

Interdependence

Climate and vegetation: Heavy rainfall and year-round warmth support rapid plant growth. Dense vegetation provides food and habitats, while roots help protect the soil.

Pollination: Bees and butterflies gain food from flowers while transferring pollen between plants. Plants depend on these animals for reproduction, and the animals depend on the plants for food. Epiphytes grow on branches to reach light and obtain water from rain and moisture around them.

Nutrients and water: Decomposers rapidly break down dead material, and shallow roots absorb the released nutrients. Trees return water to the atmosphere through transpiration, helping maintain rainfall. Plants therefore depend on the climate while also influencing it.

Threats and consequences

Forest clearance and roads: Commercial and small-scale farming clear land for crops and cattle. Logging removes valuable hardwoods, including mahogany, and fragments habitats. The Interoceanic Highway improves access between Peru and Brazil but also opens remote forest to settlement, logging and mining. Coca cultivation and fires used to clear land add pressure; fires can spread beyond the intended area.

Illegal hardwood extraction: A 2005 estimate, cited in a 2009 trade assessment, suggested 95% of Peruvian mahogany was logged illegally. This historical estimate concerns mahogany, rather than all logging today. High timber values encourage extraction, while the forest's size makes monitoring and enforcement difficult.

Mining and river pollution: Gold mining removes forest and disturbs riverbanks. Mercury used to separate gold can contaminate water and food webs, putting wildlife and people's health at risk. These effects extend beyond the mine because communities downstream also depend on river water.

Scale and carbon loss: From 2005 to 2015, Peru lost more than 134,000 hectares of forest per year on average. The World Bank reported that land-use change produced more than half of Peru's greenhouse-gas emissions. Clearing forest releases stored carbon as well as destroying habitats.

Soil and water changes: Removing trees takes away the main nutrient store and reduces the supply of leaf litter. Fewer roots absorb and retain nutrients, while exposed soil suffers greater raindrop impact, erosion and leaching. Reduced interception increases rapid runoff, and lower transpiration returns less moisture to the atmosphere. These linked effects show interdependence: changing vegetation also changes soil, water and wildlife.

Camisea: benefits and risks: The Camisea natural gas project, operating since 2004, supplies energy and can create employment and revenue. However, extraction sites and pipelines introduce infrastructure into sensitive forest and raise concerns about habitats and Indigenous communities. Its impact depends on access routes, safeguards, consultation and monitoring, so different types of forest use should not be treated as equally damaging.

Sustainable management

Indigenous land rights: Legal recognition and secure land title strengthen communities' ability to protect forest from illegal use. Between 2015 and 2021, a World Bank-supported programme helped recognise 253 native communities and title 58 communities covering about 230,000 hectares.

Sustainable farming: Peru's FOLUR project, running from 2023 to 2029, supports deforestation-free coffee and cocoa across 17 provinces. Improving production on existing farmland can provide income without further forest clearance.

Protected forests and connected habitats: The Purús–Manu conservation corridor links areas including Alto Purús and Manu National Parks with Indigenous territories. WWF's 2015 account describes about 10 million hectares, five protected areas and 63 native communities. It includes habitats for monkeys, river dolphins and valuable trees such as mahogany. Restoring damaged land and connecting habitats help wildlife move between areas and reduce the isolation caused by fragmentation.

Enforcement and evaluation: Satellite monitoring can identify clearance, but controls on illegal mining and logging still need enforcement. Secure land rights, community support and reliable sustainable incomes make conservation more practical. Rules alone may fail where enforcement is weak or illegal extraction remains profitable.

Coral reef processes

Conditions and the coral–algae partnership

Coral is an animal. Reef-building polyps produce hard calcium-carbonate skeletons, creating structures that provide habitats for other organisms. Most warm-water reef builders need temperatures above roughly 18 °C; the school guide's 23–25 °C favourable range lies within the wider 23–29 °C optimum reported for many species.

ConditionWhy it matters
Warm water within a suitable rangeSupports growth, but prolonged unusual heat can cause stress. Warmer is not always better.
Shallow, clear waterAllows light to reach the photosynthetic algae within coral tissues. Sediment or cloudy water can reduce it.
Suitable salinityReef-building organisms are adapted to salty seawater. Large freshwater inputs can disturb conditions.

The algae make food by photosynthesis and share products with the coral. The coral provides a protected place and useful substances for the algae. Both benefit from this mutual relationship.

Nutrients are retained and reused

Surrounding tropical seawater can contain few available nutrients. A reef remains productive partly because organisms retain and reuse nutrients efficiently. Waste and dead material are broken down, nutrients become available to producers, and feeding transfers them through the community. Exchanges within the coral–algae partnership help keep nutrients in use.

Grazing fish can prevent algae from overwhelming coral. Nearby mangroves and seagrass provide nursery and feeding habitats and can trap sediment before it reaches the reef. Damage to one connected habitat can therefore affect organisms using another.

Bleaching and its consequences

When stressed, coral can lose its algae and become pale or white: coral bleaching. The coral loses an important food source and becomes more vulnerable. Bleaching does not mean the animal is already dead; recovery is possible if conditions improve, while prolonged stress can cause death.

Losing living coral reduces habitat quality and can weaken fisheries, tourism and coastal protection. Marine reserves can reduce local fishing or physical damage, but they cannot by themselves stop regional marine heatwaves. Management needs to address both local pressures and wider causes.

Case study: Andros Barrier Reef, The Bahamas

Place and importance

Andros is the largest island in The Bahamas. Its barrier reef stretches for about 190 miles (306 km) and is commonly described by the Bahamian government as the world's third-largest barrier reef. It runs broadly parallel to the eastern shoreline, with deeper water separating parts of the reef from the land. It supports fisheries, tourism, biodiversity and coastal protection. Surveys in 1997–1998 recorded 164 reef-fish species, showing the variety of life supported by the reef.

Interdependence and value

Connected habitats: Coral provides shelter and feeding grounds for fish and invertebrates. Reef fish graze algae that could otherwise smother coral. Nearby seagrass beds, mangroves and creeks provide further feeding and nursery areas, and young fish may move between them as they grow. Clearing nursery vegetation can therefore affect fisheries beyond the damaged site.

Coastal protection and livelihoods: The reef absorbs wave energy, reducing coastal erosion and storm damage. Fishing for species such as snapper, grouper and lobster supplies food and income, while snorkelling, diving and guided fishing support tourism. Protecting the ecosystem helps protect both the coast and the local economy. Research and monitoring also depend on healthy habitats.

Threats

Heat and storms: Marine heat can cause coral bleaching and, if stress continues, coral death. Strong storms can break reef structures. Local visitor rules cannot remove the wider threat from warming seas.

Water quality and habitat damage: Agricultural chemicals, sewage and oil or chemicals from boats can pollute the sea. Pollution and sediment reduce water quality and the light available to coral's algae. Dredging and coastal development can damage connected habitats, while anchors and careless visitor contact can harm the reef itself. This also threatens tourism income because visitors depend on attractive, living habitats.

Disrupted food webs: Overfishing removes important species, while invasive lionfish disturb food webs. These changes can weaken the relationships that help keep the reef healthy.

Protection and sustainable use

Marine parks: North Marine Park protects about 5,000 acres, and South Marine Park about 3,500 acres. Protected areas can limit damaging activities and allow fish stocks and habitats to recover. Sustainable fishing rules help protect breeding populations, while monitoring tracks bleaching, disease and changes in coral or fish numbers.

Protecting the habitat network: West Side National Park protects tidal flats and mangrove creeks on western Andros, including nursery habitat for bonefish and tarpon. It complements protection of the eastern barrier reef but is a different location. The Crab Replenishment Reserve protects land-crab habitat and breeding stocks.

Tourism and evaluation: Eco-tourism creates income and gives businesses a reason to conserve the reef. Success still depends on understood boundaries, enforcement, responsible visitors and control of nearby pollution and overfishing. Local protection improves resilience, but wider greenhouse-gas reductions are needed to limit marine heat stress.

Exam links to remember

  • Start an ecosystem answer with the named link: climate → vegetation → soil/animals.
  • For each case study learn place, process, value, threat, management and limitation.
  • Use a developed chain: “Trees are cleared, so interception falls, therefore runoff and soil erosion increase.”
  • Do not say every management strategy is fully successful. Add a supported judgement.
