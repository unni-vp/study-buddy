# People of the UK

## The UK in the wider world
### Trade connects the UK to other places

**Exports** are goods and services sold abroad. **Imports** are bought from other countries. Think of trade as a network of exchanges: British firms sell specialised products and services, while households and businesses buy goods that are made elsewhere.

Goods are physical products. Services include activities such as finance, consultancy, transport and tourism. A country can import and export the same broad product category because firms make different models or components.

| Trade flow | Important examples |
|---|---|
| UK goods exports | Machinery and power-generation equipment, medicines, cars and aircraft. |
| UK service exports | Business services, financial services and other specialised work for overseas customers. |
| UK goods imports | Cars, machinery, medicines, fuels, electronics and food. |

### Major trading partners

This **2025 snapshot** combines exports and imports of **goods and services**. A goods-only ranking would be different.

| Partner | Share of total UK trade in 2025 |
|---|---:|
| United States | 17.5% |
| Germany | 8.0% |
| Netherlands | 6.2% |
| China | 5.5% |
| France | 5.5% |

The United States was the largest individual partner, while nearby European economies also remained important. Distance, major ports, transport links, demand, specialist skills and trading arrangements help explain these connections. Global links create markets and jobs, but can also expose firms to overseas price changes or disrupted supply chains.

### Compare diversity and inequality carefully

One measure cannot describe every aspect of a place. Use several, and check whether the data cover a whole region or a small neighbourhood.

| Measure | What it can reveal | What it can hide |
|---|---|---|
| Employment and job type | Availability of work and the balance of different industries. | A job may be insecure, poorly paid or difficult to reach. |
| Average income | Differences in earnings or household resources. | An average can hide high inequality and different living costs. |
| Life expectancy | Broad differences in health and living conditions. | Neighbourhoods within one city may differ greatly. |
| Educational attainment | Differences in qualifications and access to opportunities. | Qualifications alone do not guarantee a suitable local job. |
| Broadband access | Ability to use digital services, study and work online. | A connection does not guarantee that a household can afford equipment or has suitable skills. |
| Ethnicity | Patterns of cultural diversity and community identity. | Ethnicity is not a scale on which one population is more developed than another. |

### Why economic development is uneven

**Location and infrastructure:** Firms need access to workers, customers and suppliers. Good transport and digital connections reduce delays and make a place more attractive for investment. A remote location with poor connections may struggle to compete.

**Economic change:** Primary work extracts resources, secondary work manufactures products, tertiary work provides services, and quaternary work uses research and knowledge. The decline of mining or manufacturing can remove jobs and reduce spending in nearby businesses. Automation can also reduce employment even when a factory continues producing goods.

**Government policy and investment:** Infrastructure, skills training and regeneration can attract employers. New investment may create a positive multiplier: firms provide jobs, employees spend locally, and that spending supports other businesses. The reverse can follow closure.

Regional patterns are broad tendencies. Prosperous and deprived neighbourhoods can exist in the same region, so “the North is poor and the South is rich” is too simple.

## Economic change: Salford Quays

### Place and industrial background

Salford Quays lies beside the Manchester Ship Canal, west of central Manchester. The former Manchester Docks handled imported and exported goods, including cotton for the textile economy, and supported warehouses, engineering and transport employment. The **Manchester Ship Canal opened in 1894**, allowing ocean-going ships to reach inland Manchester and Salford.

### Why the docks declined

**Changing ships and trade:** Larger container ships needed handling facilities and access that the inland docks could not easily provide. Coastal ports became more competitive, while manufacturing decline reduced linked trade and employment.

**Closure and its consequences:** The docks closed in **1982**, leaving unemployment, polluted water, derelict land and a damaged image. Decline became a negative cycle: fewer jobs reduced local spending, dereliction discouraged investment and the tax base weakened. This shows the wider effects of **deindustrialisation**: losing industry affects the surrounding community as well as the workers directly employed.

### Regeneration strategy

**Preparing the land:** Salford Council bought much of the dockland and published a development plan in **1985**. Public investment cleaned water, reclaimed land, improved roads and public spaces, and prepared sites for private development. Housing, offices, leisure facilities and services replaced derelict dockland.

**Culture as a starting point:** **The Lowry** opened in **2000**, supported by **£64 million of National Lottery funding**. The arts venue attracted visitors and helped change the area's image, encouraging spending and further investment. The funding figure should be understood as a grant, rather than the total cost of every part of the regeneration.

**Media and transport:** **MediaCity** opened in **2011**, attracting the BBC, ITV, education and creative businesses. It now hosts more than **250 creative and technology businesses** alongside major broadcasters. Metrolink connections improved access from the wider city region, helping employees and visitors reach the new district.

### Economic effects

**Jobs and the multiplier effect:** Offices, television production, digital firms and education widened the employment base. MediaCity's broadcasters and education sites act as **anchor organisations**: they attract suppliers, workers and students, whose spending supports shops, cafés, transport and housing. This extra activity is a **multiplier effect**.

**Ambition versus measured change:** Early MediaCity publicity projected up to **10,000 jobs** and an extra **£1 billion** for the regional economy over five years. These were forecasts, not proof that every job had already been created. The BBC relocation involved **five departments**, including **BBC Sport**, with staff beginning to move in **2011**.

**Who benefits:** Some jobs require specialist skills, while others may be insecure. Training, affordable transport and access to vacancies help existing residents benefit. A firm moving from elsewhere in Greater Manchester does not necessarily create a completely new regional job, so new buildings alone are not enough to measure success.

### Social effects

New homes, cultural venues and public spaces improved opportunities and the area's image. However, rising land and housing values can exclude lower-income households. Regeneration may produce visible success while inequality remains in surrounding neighbourhoods.

### Environmental effects

Brownfield land was reused rather than leaving derelict sites or building only on greenfield land. Water and public-space improvements created a more attractive waterfront. Construction, traffic and high resource use create continuing environmental costs.

### Evaluation

Salford Quays is a strong example of physical and economic regeneration: abandoned docks became a nationally important media and cultural district. Its social success is less complete because benefits depend on affordable housing, transport, training and whether jobs are accessible to existing communities.

### Exam pointer

Use the sequence **dock closure in 1982 → dereliction and job loss → public land and infrastructure investment → Lowry and MediaCity → economic gains but uneven social benefits**.

## Population change

### Population structure

A **population pyramid** shows age and sex structure. A broad base suggests many young people; a larger upper section suggests an ageing population.

Since 1900, death rates have fallen and people generally live longer. Birth rates later fell, so older age groups now make up a larger share of the UK population.

![Illustrative population pyramid with labelled age groups, sex and relative bar length](diagrams/population-pyramid.svg)

**Read the shape:** identify the age group and side, then use bar length. This model illustrates the skill; it is not a UK census dataset.

### Demographic Transition Model
The **Demographic Transition Model (DTM)** links changes in birth and death rates with changes in population. It is like two taps affecting a tank: births add people and deaths remove them. The gap between the rates determines **natural change**, not the size of the population by itself.

| Stage | Typical change | Effect on population |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Birth and death rates are both high and fluctuate. | Natural growth is generally small. |
| 2 | Death rates fall while birth rates remain high. | The large gap produces rapid natural increase. |
| 3 | Birth rates fall while death rates remain low. | Natural increase continues but slows. |
| 4 | Both rates are low. | Natural change is small, although total population may still change through migration. |
| 5 | Birth rates can fall below death rates. | Natural decrease becomes possible. Migration can offset it. |

Since 1900, the UK has moved from a pattern of falling death rates and later falling birth rates towards low rates and an older age structure. Better healthcare, sanitation and living conditions helped reduce deaths; changing family choices and access to contraception contributed to smaller families. Recent natural change can be very small or negative, but this does not by itself prove that the total population is falling.

![Schematic five-stage birth-rate and death-rate model](diagrams/demographic-transition.svg)

The model simplifies reality. Countries do not follow identical timings, wars or disease can interrupt trends, and **migration is not included**. A population pyramid shows the resulting age structure; the DTM instead focuses on birth and death rates.

### Ageing population
A population **ages** when older people make up a greater proportion of the total. Longer life expectancy, lower birth rates and large generations reaching retirement all contribute.

| Effect | Why it matters | Possible response |
|---|---|---|
| More demand for health and social care | A larger older population can increase the need for treatment, carers and accessible services. Needs vary between individuals. | Train care workers, coordinate services and adapt housing so people can live independently. |
| Pressure on pensions and public spending | More pensioners relative to workers can make funding harder. | Encourage saving, review pension arrangements and support people who want to work longer. |
| Demand for workers | Some sectors may face shortages as experienced staff retire. | Training, flexible employment and working-age migration can help fill gaps. |
| Economic and social contributions | Older people spend money, volunteer, provide childcare and may remain in paid work. | Design accessible services and avoid treating every older person as dependent. |

A response has limits. Later retirement may be difficult for someone with ill health or a physically demanding job. Migration can support the workforce, but migrants also need housing and services and will themselves age. A combination of measures is more realistic than one permanent solution.

## Migration and ways of life

**Immigration** means moving into a country. **Emigration** means moving out. **Net migration** is immigration minus emigration.

People migrate because of **push factors** such as unemployment or conflict and **pull factors** such as jobs, education, family and safety.

### Impacts of immigration

- Workers fill skills gaps and pay taxes.
- Migrants can start businesses and add cultural diversity.
- Population growth can support local services.
- Rapid growth can put pressure on housing, schools, healthcare and transport if capacity does not keep pace.
- Effects vary by place, time and the age and skills of migrants.

Avoid one-sided claims. A strong answer considers **social and economic benefits and challenges**.

### Migration flows change over time

The UK has received migrants from European countries and from other regions through work, study, family and humanitarian routes. European movement was particularly important after EU enlargement in the 2000s. The end of free movement and new arrangements from 2021 changed that context; work and study have been major reasons for non-EU immigration in recent years.

In the ONS estimate for **2025**, study-related arrivals represented **47%** and work-related arrivals **23%** of non-EU+ immigration. These percentages describe that particular group and year, not every person arriving in Britain. People also leave: immigration alone is not net migration.

Impacts depend on **where people settle**, their needs and the pace of investment. An extra worker may fill a vacancy and pay tax, while a rapidly growing neighbourhood may need additional school places. These effects can occur together. Demand pressure does not mean every migrant uses more public resources than they contribute.

## Urban change

- **Suburbanisation:** people and activities move from the inner city to the urban edge.
- **Counter-urbanisation:** people move from cities to smaller settlements or rural areas.
- **Re-urbanisation:** people and investment return to inner-city areas.

These movements affect housing demand, traffic, land values, services and the character of communities.

| Trend | Why it happens | Possible consequences |
|---|---|---|
| **Suburbanisation** | Households seek space; land at the edge may be cheaper, and transport allows commuting. | Urban sprawl, greenfield development, longer journeys and greater demand for roads and services. |
| **Counter-urbanisation** | Some households leave cities for smaller settlements, seeking environment, space or remote-working opportunities. | Commuting villages, higher house prices and changed local services; access differs by income. |
| **Re-urbanisation** | Regeneration, new homes, jobs and amenities attract people back to inner areas. | Brownfield reuse and city-centre spending, but possible higher rents and displacement. |

## Leeds city case study

### Place, population and importance

Leeds is the largest city in West Yorkshire and a major centre for finance, business services, retail, healthcare, education and culture. Rail and motorway links connect it to northern England and the rest of the UK.

The city's population was about **812,000 in 2021**, an increase of **8.1%** since 2011. Around **471,000 people work in Leeds**, showing its regional employment role.

### Migration and changing character

Universities attract students from the UK and abroad. National and international migrants add workers, skills, languages, food, religion and businesses. The 2021 population included about **9.7% Asian**, **5.6% Black** and **3.4% mixed ethnic groups**.

Population growth supports services and the economy, but benefits and pressures vary by neighbourhood. New arrivals and natural increase add demand for housing, schools, healthcare and transport.

**Leeds West Indian Carnival**, first held in **1967**, shows how migration can shape leisure, music, food and the use of public space. Universities attract people from across the UK and overseas, supporting rental housing, cafés and entertainment. These are cultural and economic changes together, rather than simply a change in population size.

Demand can raise rents in popular neighbourhoods and create seasonal pressure on services. Effects differ between places, so one lively student area cannot represent the living conditions of every Leeds resident.

### Housing challenge

Leeds had about **362,780 homes**, and private renting accounted for roughly **21.8%**. The median house price rose from about **£175,000 in 2015 to £225,000 in 2022**. The city also contains about **19,500 pre-1919 back-to-back homes**, some needing improvements in energy efficiency and condition.

Responses include new affordable housing, brownfield redevelopment and upgrading older homes. Around **595 affordable homes were delivered in 2021**, but demand, construction cost and rising prices mean the shortage cannot be solved quickly.

### Inequality challenge

About **175,000 residents** were estimated to live in relative poverty. Lower income affects housing quality, health, education and access to opportunities. Growth in the city centre does not automatically remove deprivation elsewhere.

Successful regeneration must connect disadvantaged communities to skills, jobs and services, rather than concentrating only on high-value buildings.

### Transport challenge

Large commuter flows cause congestion, air pollution and unreliable journeys. People without good public transport may struggle to reach jobs or education.

Leeds promotes buses, park-and-ride, walking and cycling through the **Connecting Leeds** programme. These can reduce car dependence, but routes, price, reliability and safe connections determine whether people actually change travel behaviour.

**South Bank and Holbeck:** Regeneration reuses former industrial land for homes, jobs, education, leisure and public spaces. **Holbeck Urban Village** supports creative and digital businesses in a historic industrial setting. Pedestrian and cycle links connect neighbourhoods with the centre, offering alternatives to car use. These changes can improve sustainability, but housing affordability and access for existing communities remain important.

### Waste and low-carbon living

The city aims to reduce waste, reuse materials and improve recycling. Collection and processing must keep pace with population and business growth.

The **Leeds PIPES** heat network uses heat recovered from the Recycling and Energy Recovery Facility. By 2026 it extended for more than **30 km**, served about **4,100 properties** including **2,000 council flats**, and supplied around **30 public and commercial buildings**. It saved more than **7,000 tonnes of carbon in 2025**.

### Evaluation

Leeds combines transport, housing, waste and low-carbon heat measures, so it addresses several parts of sustainability. PIPES turns waste heat into lower-carbon energy and directly serves homes. Yet high housing costs, poverty and unequal transport access show that environmental projects alone do not make a city socially sustainable.

### Exam pointer

Organise a developed answer as **challenge → named response → number showing scale → limitation**. For example: housing prices rose, affordable homes were built, but output remains small compared with total need.
