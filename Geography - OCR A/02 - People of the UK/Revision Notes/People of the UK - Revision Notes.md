# People of the UK

## The UK in the wider world

### Trade and connections

The UK is connected to other countries through trade, investment, migration, transport and communication.

- **Exports** are goods and services sold to other countries.
- **Imports** are goods and services bought from other countries.
- Important exports include services, medicines, machinery and vehicles.
- Important imports include manufactured goods, food, fuels and raw materials.
- Trade patterns change with price, demand, transport costs, political agreements and specialised skills.

**Analogy:** Trade is like a huge swap system. The UK sells what it can produce competitively and buys products that other places supply more cheaply or in greater quantities.

### Measuring development within the UK

One indicator never tells the whole story. Use several:

- **Employment:** the number and type of jobs available.
- **Income:** earnings and household wealth.
- **Life expectancy:** average length of life.
- **Educational attainment:** qualifications and achievement.
- **Ethnicity:** the cultural diversity of a population.
- **Broadband access:** connection to digital services and opportunities.

### Why development is uneven

- **Location:** accessibility and distance from major markets affect investment.
- **Economic change:** the decline of an industry can remove many linked jobs.
- **Infrastructure:** good transport and broadband attract firms and workers.
- **Government policy:** investment, tax decisions and regeneration can help selected areas.

## Economic change: Salford Quays

Salford Quays is used to study the effects of economic decline and later growth.

### Reasons for decline

The docks lost importance as shipping technology and the location of industry changed. Fewer goods passed through the area, so dock and linked industrial employment declined. Job losses were followed by unused buildings, derelict land and a poorer image, making further investment less likely.

### Regeneration strategy

Former industrial land was reused for housing, offices, culture, leisure and media-related employment. New public spaces, buildings and transport connections improved accessibility and changed how residents, businesses and visitors viewed the area. The strategy aimed to replace a single declining industrial function with a more varied economy.

### Economic and environmental effects

Regeneration attracted businesses, visitors and investment and created jobs in new sectors. Reusing brownfield land improved the appearance of the waterfront and reduced pressure to build entirely on undeveloped land. Better infrastructure also connected the Quays more closely to the surrounding urban area.

### Social effects and inequality

New homes, leisure spaces and employment can improve quality of life, but the benefits are not automatically shared. Housing may be expensive, and some new jobs may require skills that existing residents do not have. Physical improvement can therefore occur faster than reductions in deprivation.

### Exam pointer

Use the sequence **dock decline → unemployment and dereliction → regeneration investment → mixed results**. Finish with the judgement that Salford Quays shows strong physical and economic renewal, while social success depends on affordable housing and accessible jobs.

## Population change

### Population structure

A **population pyramid** shows age and sex structure. A broad base suggests many young people; a larger upper section suggests an ageing population.

Since 1900, death rates have fallen and people generally live longer. Birth rates later fell, so older age groups now make up a larger share of the UK population.

### Demographic Transition Model

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Population change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | High | High | Small, fluctuating growth |
| 2 | High | Falls rapidly | Rapid growth |
| 3 | Falls | Low | Growth slows |
| 4 | Low | Low | Stable or slow growth |
| 5 | Very low | Low | Possible natural decrease |

The model is useful for showing a broad pattern, but countries do not all change at the same speed.

### Ageing population

Causes include lower birth rates, longer life expectancy and the ageing of large generations.

- **Benefits:** experienced workers, volunteering, spending by retired people and family support.
- **Challenges:** greater demand for healthcare, pensions and social care; fewer workers per dependent person.
- **Responses:** later retirement, workplace pensions, adapting homes and services, and encouraging working-age migration.

## Migration and ways of life

**Immigration** means moving into a country. **Emigration** means moving out. **Net migration** is immigration minus emigration.

People migrate because of **push factors** such as unemployment or conflict and **pull factors** such as jobs, education, family and safety.

### Impacts of immigration

- Workers fill skills gaps and pay taxes.
- Migrants can start businesses and add cultural diversity.
- Population growth can support local services.
- Rapid growth can put pressure on housing, schools, healthcare and transport if capacity does not keep pace.
- Effects vary by place, time and the age and skills of migrants.

Avoid one-sided claims. A strong answer considers **social and economic benefits and challenges**.

## Urban change

- **Suburbanisation:** people and activities move from the inner city to the urban edge.
- **Counter-urbanisation:** people move from cities to smaller settlements or rural areas.
- **Re-urbanisation:** people and investment return to inner-city areas.

These movements affect housing demand, traffic, land values, services and the character of communities.

## Leeds city case study

### Importance and character

Leeds is the major UK city used in this course.

Leeds is an important regional centre for employment, shopping, education, culture and transport. Its concentration of higher-order services attracts workers, students and visitors from a wide area. Businesses, universities and transport connections also link the city to national and international networks.

### Migration and city character

Migration from within the UK and from other countries has helped the city grow. It has added skills, businesses, languages, food and cultural traditions. The effects vary between neighbourhoods because housing, income, age structure and access to services are uneven across the city.

### Urban challenges

**Housing:** Population change and smaller households increase demand. Building new homes and improving older housing can help, but affordability and the quality of neighbourhoods remain uneven.

**Transport:** Commuting and movement across a large urban area can create congestion and air pollution. People without convenient public transport may also have poorer access to employment and services.

**Waste:** A large population and busy economy generate substantial waste. Collection, recycling and disposal must keep pace without increasing pollution or using excessive land.

**Inequality:** Investment and regeneration may improve selected places while other communities continue to experience lower incomes, poorer environments or weaker access to opportunities.

### Sustainable urban living

A sustainable strategy should connect rather than isolate solutions. Reliable public transport and safe walking or cycling routes can reduce car use. Brownfield redevelopment and energy-efficient homes can provide housing while limiting outward growth. Recycling, reduced waste and accessible green space improve environmental quality.

Sustainability also has a social side. Housing, services and jobs need to be affordable and reachable for existing communities. A scheme that looks greener but excludes lower-income residents has not solved the whole problem.

### Exam pointer

For Leeds, organise the answer as **importance → migration and character → challenge → sustainable response**. Evaluate success using social, economic and environmental evidence rather than describing how a project looks.

## High-yield checklist

- Compare several development indicators.
- Explain uneven development using a chain: **cause → change → consequence**.
- Know the causes, effects and responses to an ageing population.
- Give balanced migration impacts.
- Use Salford Quays for economic change and Leeds for the major-city case study.
- Evaluate whether improvements are sustainable and fairly shared.
