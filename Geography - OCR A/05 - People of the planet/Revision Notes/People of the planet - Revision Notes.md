# People of the planet

## Understanding development

**Development** means improvement in people's quality of life. It has three connected parts:

- **Economic:** income, jobs, trade and infrastructure.
- **Social:** health, education, equality and access to services.
- **Environmental:** safe water, clean air and using resources without damaging the future.

Countries are often described as **ACs** (advanced countries), **EDCs** (emerging and developing countries) or **LIDCs** (low-income developing countries). These are broad groups; inequality within a country means an average never describes everyone.

### Measuring development

| Indicator | What it shows | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GNI per person** | Average income linked to a country's people and businesses | Easy to compare economic wealth | Hides inequality and does not measure health or education |
| **HDI** | Life expectancy, education and income combined | Gives a broader picture of quality of life | Still uses national averages and misses some inequalities |
| **Internet users** | Access to communication and digital services | Suggests infrastructure and technological connection | Access may be slow, expensive or unequal |

Uneven development affects migration, health, education, employment and political influence. Poorer places may lose skilled workers; richer places may attract investment and gain more power in trade decisions.

### Why development is uneven

- **Colonialism:** borders and economies were often designed to export raw materials rather than benefit local people.
- **Trade:** primary products may earn low or unstable prices, while manufactured imports cost more.
- **Natural resources:** resources can fund services and industry, but benefits may be lost through conflict, corruption or foreign control.
- **Aid:** emergency aid saves lives; development aid funds long-term projects. Aid can build skills and infrastructure but may create dependency or reflect donor priorities.
- **Landlocked location, climate hazards, conflict and debt** may make trade and public investment harder.

## Country case study: Ethiopia

### Location, environment and resources

Ethiopia is a landlocked country in the **Horn of Africa**. Its varied relief includes highlands and the low-lying Rift Valley. Rain-fed farming supports many rural households, so drought and unreliable rainfall can damage crops, livestock, food supply and income.

Resources and opportunities include fertile highland areas, coffee, livestock, minerals, rivers with hydroelectric potential and a large working-age population. Difficult terrain, drought, soil erosion and lack of direct sea access raise transport and production costs.

### Political and historical influences

Ethiopia was not colonised for a long period, although Italy occupied it from 1936 to 1941. Later conflict, authoritarian rule and regional tensions disrupted development. Investment in roads, energy and public services supported growth, but conflict, debt, inflation and restrictions on private business created new pressures.

### Trade and foreign investment

- Major goods exports include **coffee**, cut flowers, sesame, pulses and livestock products.
- Ethiopia imports machinery, vehicles, fuel, medicines and manufactured goods.
- Depending on a few agricultural exports makes earnings vulnerable to drought and changing world prices.
- Foreign direct investment in factories and industrial parks can create jobs, skills, tax income and export growth.
- Possible costs include low wages, profits leaving the country, pollution and dependence on foreign firms.

### Population, employment and services

Ethiopia has a large and rapidly growing population. Agriculture remains important, while manufacturing, construction and services are expanding. Rapid growth creates a large labour supply but also strong demand for schools, healthcare, housing and jobs.

World Bank evidence shows both progress and limits. Between **2004 and 2016**, the national poverty rate fell from **39% to 24%**. Infrastructure programmes extended nearby safe-water access to **60 million more people**, doubled electricity access and increased child vaccinations. Yet income per person remains low, many rural livelihoods depend on rainfall and conflict has reversed some gains.

UNDP reported that Ethiopia's HDI rose from **0.283 in 2000 to 0.470 in 2018**, but remained in the low human-development group. School access and health improved, while educational quality, adult literacy and rural–urban differences remained challenges.

### Technology

Mobile phones, digital payments, improved seeds, irrigation and better transport can connect farmers to prices, finance and markets. However, electricity, internet coverage, cost and skills are uneven, particularly in rural areas.

### Rostow's model

Rostow's model suggests countries pass through five stages: **traditional society**, **preconditions for take-off**, **take-off**, **drive to maturity**, then **high mass consumption**. Ethiopia shows features of the preconditions and take-off stages: large-scale infrastructure and industry are growing, but agriculture still employs many people.

**Evaluation:** The model is easy to apply, but assumes every country follows the path taken by Western economies. It underplays colonial relationships, conflict, inequality and environmental limits.

### Aid project: Oxfam goat aid

In **Kanbi, southern Ethiopia**, Oxfam America's local partner DUBAF used the traditional **hirbaa-dabaree** sharing system to rebuild herds after drought. The programme initially targeted **220 households**. Each first-round household received **seven goats**, then passed offspring to another family.

**How it supports development:**

- Goat milk improves nutrition.
- Families can sell offspring for income and build an asset they own.
- Passing on kids spreads the benefit without buying a completely new herd for every household.
- The system uses local knowledge and strengthens community responsibility.

**Limitations:** goats still need water, grazing and veterinary care. Severe drought or disease could kill the animals, and a small herd cannot solve wider problems such as weak roads, schools or healthcare.

## A changing urban world

- A **city** is a large, densely populated urban settlement.
- A **megacity** has more than **10 million** people.
- A **world city** has major global influence through finance, business, culture or politics.
- **Urbanisation** is the increase in the proportion of people living in urban areas.

ACs are highly urbanised but often grow slowly. Many LIDCs and EDCs have faster urban growth because of natural increase and rural-to-urban migration.

### Causes of rapid urbanisation

- **Push factors:** few rural jobs, low farm income, drought, poor services or conflict.
- **Pull factors:** jobs, education, healthcare, transport, family links and perceived opportunity.
- **Natural increase:** a youthful city population produces more births than deaths.

### Effects of rapid urban growth

**Opportunities:** larger labour force and market; new businesses; improved access to services; cultural exchange.

**Challenges:** housing shortages; informal settlements; congestion; air pollution; pressure on water, sanitation, waste collection, schools and hospitals; unequal access to secure work.

## Major-city case study: Rosario, Argentina

### Location and role

Rosario lies on the **Paraná River** in Santa Fe Province, about 300 km north-west of Buenos Aires. The river port links a productive agricultural region to world markets. Food processing, grain and oilseed exports, manufacturing, logistics, commerce, education and culture give Rosario regional and national importance.

Argentina's 2022 census recorded **1,030,069 people**, **402,085 households** and **456,040 dwellings** in the municipality's **178.69 km²**. Municipal material says Rosario generates more than **half of Santa Fe Province's gross product** with its surrounding agri-industrial area.

### Migration and ways of life

People have moved to Rosario from rural areas and neighbouring provinces for work, education and services. A city migration profile found that **63.8% of internal migrants** came from adjoining provinces. Migration adds workers, skills and cultural diversity, but rapid growth can increase pressure on jobs and housing.

Living conditions vary sharply. Central and riverside districts have formal housing, services and cultural attractions, while lower-income households may have insecure work and poorer access to services. More than **80 migrant community associations** reflect the city's diversity.

### Housing

A historic city survey identified **91 informal settlements**, about **10% of the urban area**, housing roughly **115,000 people**. The **Rosario Hábitat** programme targeted **11 settlements and about 5,200 families** through legal land ownership, infrastructure, community participation, employment support and some relocation.

This integrated approach tackles several causes of poor housing at once. However, citywide housing need is larger than one programme and land, funding and population change can slow progress.

### Transport

Buses are central to public transport, while walking and cycling schemes provide alternatives to cars. Rosario's 2018 greenhouse-gas inventory attributed **34.3% of emissions** to transport, showing why cleaner buses and active travel matter. Congestion, cost, safety and unequal route access remain challenges.

### Waste

Waste collection and separation are necessary to prevent pollution and health risks. Large commercial and industrial generators produce about **20% of the city's waste**, so Rosario requires them to manage waste separately. Success depends on participation, reliable collection and markets for recycled materials.

### Sustainable strategy: urban agriculture and the Green Belt

Rosario began its **Urban Agriculture Programme in 2002**, turning unused land into productive gardens. The **Green Belt Project**, launched in 2016, supports agroecological food production near the city.

By 2021 the wider programme involved about **25 hectares**, **155 growers**, **nine productive garden spaces** and **seven garden parks**. It creates income, supplies local food, reuses urban land, shortens supply chains and adds green space.

**Evaluation:** The scheme joins social, economic and environmental aims, making it a strong example of sustainable urban living. Its scale is still small beside the needs of a million-person city, and long-term success depends on secure land, training, water, markets and funding.

## Exam links to remember

- When using an indicator, always give **one strength and one limitation**.
- For Ethiopia, connect a factor to a consequence: “Landlocked → longer route to a port → higher export costs.”
- For Rosario, structure a developed answer as **challenge → named strategy → evidence → limitation**.
- A judgement should compare how far a strategy improves lives with the size of the remaining problem.
