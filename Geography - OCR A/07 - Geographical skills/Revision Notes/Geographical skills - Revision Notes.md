# Geographical skills

## Cartographic skills

### Grid references

Read **along the corridor, then up the stairs**.

- A **four-figure grid reference** identifies a grid square: easting first, then northing.
- A **six-figure grid reference** identifies a point more precisely by dividing the square into tenths.
- Always read eastings from left to right and northings from bottom to top.

### Scale and distance

Map scale compares map distance with real distance.

- At **1:50,000**, 1 cm on the map represents 50,000 cm = 500 m = 0.5 km.
- At **1:25,000**, 1 cm represents 250 m = 0.25 km.
- For a winding route, follow it with string or the edge of paper, then compare the measured length with the scale.

**Worked example:** A straight line is 6 cm on a 1:50,000 map. Real distance = 6 × 0.5 km = **3 km**.

### Relief and gradient

- **Contour lines** join points of equal height.
- Close contours show a steep slope; wide spacing shows a gentle slope.
- A **spot height** gives the exact height of one point.
- A **cross-section** shows the side profile of land along a line.

**Gradient = vertical change ÷ horizontal distance**

Keep both measurements in the same units. A rise of 100 m over 2 km is 100 ÷ 2000 = 1/20, written **1:20**.

### Map types

| Map | Best use |
|---|---|
| **Choropleth** | Shaded areas show rates or values; use comparable data such as percentages. |
| **Isoline** | Lines join equal values, such as temperature or pressure. |
| **Flow-line** | Line width shows the amount moving between places. |
| **Desire-line** | Straight lines connect an origin to destinations. |
| **Sphere of influence** | Shows the area served by a settlement or service. |
| **Route map** | Shows a journey or pathway. |
| **Sketch map** | Simplifies the most relevant features. |

### GIS

A **Geographical Information System** stores, layers and analyses location-based data.

- Layers might include roads, flood risk, population and land use.
- Overlaying layers helps reveal patterns and relationships.
- GIS is powerful, but results depend on the accuracy, date and scale of the source data.

### Direction, area and a cross-section

A **bearing** is measured clockwise from north and written with three digits. East is 090°, south is 180° and west is 270°. Use the north line at the starting point, not the edge of a tilted map.

To estimate area, count full grid squares and combine partly covered squares. On a standard OS map the grid is usually spaced at 1 km, making a full square **1 km²**. Check the grid and scale rather than assuming this for every diagram.

To draw a cross-section:

1. Lay paper along the required line and mark each contour crossing with its height.
2. Transfer these positions to the horizontal axis at the map's scale.
3. Plot the heights on a labelled vertical scale and join them smoothly.
4. Label major features. If vertical and horizontal scales differ, recognise that slopes may look exaggerated.

In a six-figure reference such as **325478**, the easting is 32 plus five tenths of the square; the northing is 47 plus eight tenths. Estimate within the square from its bottom-left corner. Use the map to locate the square before adding the extra digits.

## Graphical skills

### Choosing the right graph

| Data or purpose | Suitable presentation |
|---|---|
| Categories | Bar chart |
| Continuous grouped data | Equal-interval histogram |
| Change over time | Line graph |
| Relationship between two variables | Scatter graph |
| Parts of a whole | Pie chart |
| Temperature and rainfall through a year | Climate graph |
| Population age and sex | Population pyramid |
| Direction and frequency | Rose chart |
| Values at places on a map | Proportional symbols |

### Read the labels before explaining a graph

Start with **what is measured, where, when and in which units**. A chart of UK electricity generation cannot describe all energy consumption. A historical disaster map cannot automatically describe the present day. A population pyramid shows age and sex, but its shape alone does not establish why people migrated or births changed.

| Presentation | What to check | What you can reasonably conclude |
|---|---|---|
| **Measured chart** | Year, source, units, scale and category definitions. | Describe the pattern with values and keep the conclusion within that dataset. |
| **Model diagram** | Which relationships are simplified and what is left out. | Explain the model’s proposed pattern, without treating it as measured data for a particular country. |
| **Disaster map** | Time span, event definition, reporting and whether values are totals or rates. | Compare the recorded pattern, then consider exposure and vulnerability as well as the hazard. |

**Percentage points and percentage change are different.** If one generation share is 50.4% and another is 31.8%, the difference is **18.6 percentage points**. Percentage change instead divides the change by a starting value and multiplies by 100.

### Scatter graphs

- Put the possible cause on the x-axis and the possible effect on the y-axis.
- A **positive correlation** rises from left to right.
- A **negative correlation** falls from left to right.
- **No correlation** means no clear relationship.
- A line of best fit summarises the trend; an **anomaly** lies away from it.
- Correlation suggests a relationship but does not prove causation.

### Building a clear presentation

Use a suitable title, labelled axes, units, even scale and an accurate key. Choose intervals that use the available space without distorting the pattern.

To analyse, use **pattern + evidence + exception**:

1. State the overall trend.
2. Support it with values and locations.
3. Identify and suggest a reason for an anomaly.

### Histograms, radial charts and dispersion

In an **equal-interval histogram**, neighbouring bars touch because the grouped variable is continuous. A bar chart uses separated bars for distinct categories. Check that class boundaries do not overlap, and avoid leaving a gap for an interval with no observations.

A **radial graph** uses axes extending from one centre to compare several characteristics. Keep the same direction of scoring and scale when comparing sites. A **rose chart** displays direction and frequency, such as how often wind comes from each direction.

A **dispersion diagram** plots individual values on a scale. It makes clustering, spread and unusual values visible instead of hiding them inside a single mean.

### Reading cumulative frequency

Add each class frequency to the previous total to calculate cumulative frequency. Plot these totals against the **upper class boundaries** and join with a suitable increasing curve.

- Read the median at half the total frequency.
- Read the lower quartile at one-quarter of the total and the upper quartile at three-quarters.
- A percentile identifies the value below which that percentage of the observations lies.

For 40 observations, the lower quartile is read at cumulative frequency **10**, the median at **20**, and the upper quartile at **30**. Read across to the curve and then down to the value axis.

### Detecting a misleading presentation

A shortened vertical axis can exaggerate differences. Unequal intervals can distort a trend, while changing map class boundaries can make the same data appear very different. Check whether a proportional symbol's **area**, rather than just its diameter, represents the value. A larger total may simply reflect a larger population, so rates or percentages can be fairer comparisons.

## Numerical and statistical skills

### Averages and spread

- **Mean:** total of all values ÷ number of values.
- **Median:** middle value after ordering the data.
- **Mode:** most frequent value.
- **Modal class:** group containing the greatest frequency.
- **Range:** highest − lowest.
- **Interquartile range:** upper quartile − lower quartile; it measures the spread of the middle 50%.

**Worked example:** 2, 3, 3, 6, 11

- Mean = 25 ÷ 5 = **5**
- Median = **3**
- Mode = **3**
- Range = 11 − 2 = **9**

The mean is raised by the extreme value 11, so the median may better represent a typical value.

### Percentages

**Percentage = part ÷ whole × 100**

**Percentage change = change ÷ original value × 100**

Example: traffic rises from 80 to 100 vehicles. Change = 20. Percentage increase = 20 ÷ 80 × 100 = **25%**.

### Interpolation and extrapolation

- **Interpolation** estimates within the known data range; it is usually safer.
- **Extrapolation** estimates beyond the known range; it is less reliable because the trend may change.

### A calculation in clear steps

Suppose a village's population rises from 800 to 1,000.

1. Find the increase: **1,000 − 800 = 200 people**.
2. Divide by the starting population: **200 ÷ 800 = 0.25**.
3. Multiply by 100: **0.25 × 100 = 25% increase**.

Dividing by the final value would answer a different question. If a share rises from 20% to 30%, it rises by **10 percentage points**, but the percentage increase is **50%**.

### Using a sample and handling unusual values

A sample estimates a wider population. A large sample is not automatically representative: interviewing many people at only one shopping centre may still miss other groups.

Check an unusual reading against the recording sheet and conditions. Correct a proven transcription mistake, but do not delete a genuine anomaly merely because it weakens the expected trend. A median is less affected by extreme values than a mean, while the range is strongly affected by them.

## Enquiry and data quality

### Sampling

- **Random:** every item has an equal chance; reduces selection bias.
- **Systematic:** data is collected at regular intervals; easy to repeat and gives coverage.
- **Stratified:** sample proportions match important groups in the population.
- **Opportunity:** use whoever or whatever is available; quick but more biased.

### Reliability, accuracy and validity

- **Accuracy:** how close a measurement is to the true value.
- **Reliability:** whether repeating the method gives similar results.
- **Validity:** whether the method actually answers the enquiry question.

Improve data by using clear instructions, calibrated equipment, repeated readings, a larger representative sample and consistent conditions where possible.

### Sources and bias

For every source, ask:

- Who produced it and why?
- When was it collected?
- Is the sample or area representative?
- Does the scale hide local differences?
- Has the presentation selected or omitted information?

## Reaching a conclusion

A strong conclusion:

- answers the enquiry directly;
- supports each claim with processed evidence;
- recognises anomalies and limits;
- avoids claiming that correlation proves cause;
- suggests realistic improvements and explains how they would help.

## High-yield checklist

- Show every calculation and include units.
- Choose a graph or map because it suits the data.
- Quote evidence when describing a pattern.
- Separate description (**what**) from explanation (**why**).
- Evaluate the method, data and conclusion rather than saying only “get more data”.
