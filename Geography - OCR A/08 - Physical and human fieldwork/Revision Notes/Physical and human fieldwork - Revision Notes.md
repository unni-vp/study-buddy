# Physical and human fieldwork

## The enquiry process

Fieldwork is a chain. If one link is weak, the conclusion becomes less convincing.

1. **Question:** make it focused, measurable and linked to geography.
2. **Method:** decide what data will answer it and how sites will be sampled.
3. **Collection:** measure consistently and record units, location and conditions.
4. **Presentation:** choose maps, graphs and statistics that reveal patterns.
5. **Analysis:** describe patterns, use evidence and explain geography.
6. **Conclusion:** answer the question and match the strength of the evidence.
7. **Evaluation:** judge accuracy, reliability, validity and representativeness.

**Must know:** Description says **what** the results show. Explanation says **why** the pattern may exist. Evaluation judges **how trustworthy** the investigation is.

### Planning evidence that answers the question

A **hypothesis** is a testable prediction, such as expecting a measured river characteristic to change downstream. A question can also be open, asking how far a pattern exists without assuming the answer.

**Primary data** is collected first-hand for the investigation. **Secondary data** already exists, such as maps, census information or earlier river records. Secondary evidence can provide context or comparison, but its dates, boundaries and methods may differ.

**Quantitative data** contains numerical measurements or counts. **Qualitative data** describes qualities or categories. An environmental-quality score turns judgements into numbers, but its starting assessment can still be subjective. Using numbers does not automatically make evidence objective.

Before collection, assess risks and ethical issues. Work within a teacher-approved river safety plan, avoid unsafe water and banks, and keep clear of traffic. Obtain informed consent for questionnaires, respect privacy, avoid collecting unnecessary personal information and allow people to decline.

## Human fieldwork: Liverpool

### Enquiry

**How far does environmental quality change across Liverpool town centre?**

The investigation compared environmental quality, pedestrian flow and land use at several sites.

### Geographical idea

Different parts of a city centre have different functions, investment and numbers of users. Main shopping areas may receive more maintenance because they attract businesses and visitors, while quieter streets may have different land uses and levels of investment. The enquiry tested whether this expected spatial variation could be observed in Liverpool.

### Methods

| Data | How it was collected | What it helped show |
|---|---|---|
| Environmental quality | Litter, graffiti, noise, traffic, building condition and attractiveness were scored; scores were totalled | Differences in the quality of each sampled site |
| Pedestrian flow | People passing a fixed point were counted for a set time | How busy each site was |
| Land use | Field sketches and land-use maps recorded shops, services and other buildings | The function and character of each area |

The sample was **systematic** because data was collected at regular points along a route. Using several sites allowed busier central areas to be compared with quieter streets.

### Findings and conclusion

**Environmental quality:** Main shopping and central areas had the highest environmental-quality scores. According to the observations, these places were usually better maintained and more attractive than the quieter streets sampled away from the busiest centre.

**Pedestrian flow and land use:** The same central areas had the highest pedestrian counts and the greatest concentration of retail and services. Quieter streets generally had fewer pedestrians and lower quality scores.

**Overall conclusion:** Environmental quality **was not the same everywhere**. The evidence showed a spatial association between central retail land use, high pedestrian flow and better environmental quality across the sampled sites.

Do not say high pedestrian flow definitely **caused** higher environmental quality. The results show an **association**.

### Evaluation ideas

Evaluate how the sampling and measurement choices affect confidence in the results. Possible improvements include:

- Use the same scoring rules and trained observers to reduce personal judgement in the environmental-quality survey.
- Repeat pedestrian counts at comparable times or on different days to reduce the effect of a temporary event.
- Use more sites or a clearly stated interval to improve coverage.
- Photograph sites and keep field sketches to support observations.
- Combine subjective quality scores with objective data such as litter counts where practical.

### Liverpool exam pointer

Organise the answer as **enquiry → systematic sites → three methods → overall pattern → cautious conclusion → one precise improvement**. Use “the results suggest” when explaining the relationship because the investigation does not prove that pedestrian flow or retail land use caused the quality scores.

### Presenting and testing the pattern

Place environmental-quality results on a route map so the spatial pattern remains visible. A bar chart can compare scores between sites, while proportional symbols can show pedestrian counts. A land-use map provides context for why the sites differ.

A scatter graph could compare pedestrian counts with environmental-quality scores. A positive association would mean busier sampled sites tended to have higher scores. It would not establish that pedestrians improved the environment: investment, street design or land use might influence both.

Use the **same count duration** when comparing pedestrian flow. Repeat counts at comparable times if possible, because lunch breaks, weather or a one-off event can change the results. These are proposed controls and improvements; the school account does not supply an exact duration or numerical dataset.

## Physical fieldwork: River Alyn

### Enquiry

**How do river characteristics change downstream along the River Alyn?**

The investigation examined width, depth, velocity, bedload and channel or valley shape at sites from upstream to downstream.

### Geographical idea

River theory predicts that tributaries and runoff add water downstream, increasing discharge. Channels usually become wider, deeper and more efficient, while transported bedload is broken down and rounded. The enquiry tested how closely the River Alyn followed these expected downstream changes.

### Methods

| Characteristic | Method | Useful calculation or control |
|---|---|---|
| Width | Measure across the channel | Use the same bank-to-bank definition |
| Depth | Measure at intervals across the channel | Add readings and divide by their number for mean depth |
| Velocity | Time water over a measured distance using a float, or use a flow meter | Speed = distance ÷ time; exact school method is not confirmed |
| Bedload size | Measure selected stones consistently | Use a clear sampling rule and calculate an average |
| Bedload shape | Classify roundness using the same chart | Repeat with several stones to reduce the effect of one unusual rock |
| Channel and valley | Observe or sketch features at each site | Annotate evidence rather than drawing decoratively |

The account describes **systematic sampling** at a series of upstream-to-downstream sites. The exact interval has not been supplied, so do not invent it.

### Findings and explanation

**Channel size:** Width and depth generally increased downstream. This fits the expectation that tributaries and runoff add water, so a larger channel is needed to carry the growing discharge.

**Velocity:** Velocity tended to increase as the channel became larger and more efficient. In a larger channel, a smaller proportion of the water is in direct contact with the bed and banks, so the relative effect of friction may fall.

**Bedload:** Stones became smaller and rounder downstream. During **attrition**, transported stones collide and chip one another. Continued transport also increases the time available for these changes.

The broad pattern matched river theory. Local conditions, such as an obstacle or a change in channel shape, could still cause individual sites to differ.

### Evaluation ideas

Use improvements that match a real weakness:

- Repeat width, depth and velocity measurements and calculate means.
- Keep equipment, units and procedures consistent between sites.
- Take more depth readings across irregular channels.
- For a float method, use a measured reach, repeat releases and apply the same release procedure.
- Use a larger, systematically chosen bedload sample.
- Record recent weather, obstacles and human changes that may explain local variation.

### River Alyn exam pointer

Use a separate result-and-explanation chain for **width/depth**, **velocity** and **bedload**. Use “generally” or “tended to” when describing the overall pattern, and support it with the evidence available. Keep a general explanation separate from a measured result or an observed exception.

### Interpreting measurements carefully

Plot width, mean depth and velocity against downstream distance, or in site order if distances are unavailable. Use separate labelled scales, and keep recorded exceptions rather than forcing every site onto a perfect trend.

For a float measurement:

**Speed = measured distance ÷ travel time**

A float follows a particular surface route. It may be affected by wind, obstacles or faster surface water, so it does not directly measure the average velocity throughout the channel. A flow meter measures velocity at its chosen position. The school account leaves the exact method unconfirmed, so neither should be presented as definitely used.

If estimating discharge in a suitable approximately rectangular cross-section:

**Cross-sectional area = width × mean depth**

**Discharge = cross-sectional area × mean velocity**

- Area is measured in **m²**.
- Mean velocity is measured in **m/s**.
- Discharge is measured in **m³/s**.

This explains how channel size and speed combine. It is a possible calculation from suitable measurements, not a claim that discharge was measured during this school visit.

## Writing fieldwork answers

### Method questions

Write **equipment/action + control + reason**.

Example: “Count pedestrians passing a fixed point for the same length of time at every site, so results are comparable.”

### Results questions

Write **overall pattern + evidence + exception**, using your own figures if they are supplied in the exam.

### Evaluation questions

Avoid vague statements such as “do more data”. Use:

**weakness → effect on results → precise improvement → why it helps**

Example: “A single pedestrian count may reflect a temporary event, so repeat it at the same time on several comparable days and calculate a mean. This reduces the influence of an unusual count.”

### Match the evaluation to the problem

| Problem | Why it matters | Specific improvement |
|---|---|---|
| Different people interpret quality scores differently. | Site differences may partly reflect observers rather than the environment. | Agree a scoring guide, practise together and compare scores before the survey. |
| One short pedestrian count catches a temporary event. | The sample may not represent usual use. | Repeat equal-duration counts at comparable times on several days and compare the results. |
| Depth is measured at too few positions. | An uneven channel may be poorly represented. | Take more regularly spaced depth readings across the channel and calculate a mean. |
| Bedload stones are chosen because they are easy to reach. | Large or distinctive stones may be overrepresented. | Use a stated selection rule that reduces personal choice, within safe access limits. |

Repeating a method helps assess **reliability**. Checking equipment and reducing reading error improve **accuracy**. Selecting variables and sites that answer the question improves **validity**. More repeats cannot repair a method that measures the wrong thing.

## High-yield checklist

- Learn both enquiry questions exactly.
- Know which data was quantitative and qualitative.
- Link every method to its purpose.
- Use cautious language where results “generally” or “tended to” change.
- Do not invent scores, sites, intervals, equipment choices or anomalies.
- Make every improvement specific and explain its effect on evidence quality.
