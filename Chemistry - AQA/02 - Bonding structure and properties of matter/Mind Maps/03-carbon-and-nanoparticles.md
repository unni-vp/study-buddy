# Carbon & nanoparticles

## Diamond

- Each carbon bonds to four others in a rigid giant covalent network. Many strong bonds → very hard, high melting.
- No delocalised electrons or moving ions → does not conduct electricity. Useful for cutting.

## Graphite & graphene

- Graphite: each carbon bonds to three others in hexagonal layers. One electron per carbon is delocalised → conducts.
- Weak forces between layers → slide → lubricant. Strong bonds within layers → high melting.
- Graphene: a single layer; very strong and conducting, useful in composites/electronics.

## Fullerenes & nanotubes

- Fullerenes are hollow carbon cages/tubes, based on hexagonal rings; some also have five- or seven-sided rings. C₆₀ is a cage.
- Large surface area: catalysts. Hollow cages: drug delivery. Cylindrical nanotubes: high tensile strength and conductivity → composites/electronics.

## Size classes

- Nanoparticles: **1–100 nm**. Fine particles: 100–2,500 nm. Coarse particles: 2,500–10,000 nm.
- 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m. Smaller size increases surface area compared with volume; properties may differ from bulk material.

## Calculate the ratio

- Cube of side x: area = 6x²; volume = x³; **SA/V = 6/x**. Use the same length units.
- Side 10 nm → SA/V 0.6 nm⁻¹; side 5 nm → 1.2 nm⁻¹. Halving size doubles the ratio.
- More exposed surface per amount → less catalyst/coating may be needed.

## Benefits & risks

- Uses: catalysts, medicine, electronics, sunscreen and antibacterial coatings. Link each use to a useful property.
- Small particles may enter the body; effects depend on material, dose and exposure route.
- Compare benefit with toxicity/environmental evidence and uncertainties; do not assume all nanoparticles are safe or harmful.
